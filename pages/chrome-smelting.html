Chrome Smelting

Who runs the furnaces, the Eskom crisis, and the ore-export paradox
Overview Mining Transport Smelting Ferrochrome Industry End Markets

Smelting is where chromite becomes ferrochrome. For fifty years that step happened in South Africa because power was cheap. Then Eskom's tariff went through the roof, China built furnaces, and South Africa started exporting the ore that used to feed its own plants. By early 2026 only 11 of 66 ferrochrome furnaces were still running.

A short history

  • 1960s–1970s: Ferrochrome smelting is built at Witbank (eMalahleni) and Middelburg on the back of Eskom coal power. Adding value to Bushveld chromite becomes industrial policy.
  • 1970s–1990s: Samancor (Gencor/Iscor lineage) becomes the world's largest ferrochrome producer. Xstrata/Glencore builds the western-limb plants (Wonderkop, Boshoek) and later the more efficient Lion furnace at Steelpoort.
  • 2000s: South Africa is still the global ferrochrome leader. Electricity is ~25–30% of cash cost. China is a customer for alloy, not yet a rival smelter at scale.
  • 2019–2024: Load shedding and tariff increases hammer the furnaces. Plants invest in private solar/wind. Some close. China, with cheaper power and South African ore, takes the ferrochrome crown.
  • 2025: Glencore–Merafe suspends Wonderkop, Boshoek and (partially) Lion. Attributable FeCr falls 63% (301 kt → 112 kt). Ferrochrome exports collapse ~70%. Chrome ore exports boom.
  • 2026: Nersa approves a discounted tariff of 62c/kWh for Samancor Chrome and Glencore–Merafe — about a 54% cut from ~R1.39/kWh. Job cuts of up to 1,500 at Glencore are cancelled. Restart is phased, not a full revival.

There is almost no toll-smelting model. The furnace operators are principals: they mine (or buy) ore and sell alloy. Non-integrated miners (Tharisa, ARM, PGM chrome) sell ore into the same market the smelters need as feedstock — a built-in conflict when exporting ore pays better than running a furnace.

The Eskom smelting crisis

Ferrochrome is electricity wearing a metal costume. A tonne takes about 3,500–4,000 kWh. Electricity has gone from ~25–30% of South African cash cost in 2007 to 45–60% by 2024. Eskom's average industrial tariff multiplied several times after 2008 and sat well above the 62c/kWh that older plants need to break even (and well above Lion's ~40c).

Load shedding made it worse — you cannot run a submerged-arc furnace on Stage 4 — but the structural killer was the price of a kWh in a good year, not only the blackouts. China smelts South African ore on cheaper power. Indonesia added capacity. SA's share of world ferrochrome fell from ~40–45% to the mid-teens (Merafe: SA supplied ~1.6 Mt of a 15.9 Mt world in 2025, with 72% of the ore reserves).

The 62c/kWh negotiated pricing agreement is a one-year-class intervention, not a new industrial policy. Mining Yearbook 2026 quotes the electricity minister calling it the most important announcement of his tenure because it could bring ~45 furnaces back and support ~11,000 direct jobs. Glencore's Japie Fullard is careful not to oversell it: it is a stay of execution, not cheap-power 1995.

The ore-export paradox

This is the chrome paradox in one sentence: South Africa has the ore, and is losing the profit at the factory.

  • It takes about two tonnes of chromite to make one tonne of ferrochrome.
  • Ferrochrome is on the order of five times more valuable than the ore China buys.
  • South Africa exported about 24 million tonnes of chrome ore in a recent year. China took ~19.6 Mt in 2025 (up from 17.0 Mt in 2024).
  • Even if Glencore–Merafe and Samancor restart fully, domestic furnaces can convert only about 8 million tonnes of ore. The rest must still be exported. Fullard: the country cannot beneficiate all of its chrome.
  • If Glencore restarts all its smelters, up to 4 Mt of ore that would have gone to China could become 2 Mt of ferrochrome instead — real, but not a reversal of the export machine.

So the mines keep digging, the trucks keep rolling to Maputo and Richards Bay, and China keeps the smelting margin. DTIC talk of chrome export tariffs to force local beneficiation runs into the PGM industry: UG2 chrome is a by-product, and a tax on ore exports hits Sibanye and Implats as well as Tharisa. Stewart at Sibanye has already flagged that risk.

The uncomfortable equilibrium: ore mining is profitable, smelting is a policy patient, and the customer who used to buy alloy now buys rocks. Private solar at the remaining furnaces and the 62c tariff are how South Africa tries to keep a ferrochrome industry at all. They do not restore 2008.
