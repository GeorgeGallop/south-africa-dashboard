⚠ The National Party

From the founding of Afrikaner nationalism to the architect of apartheid, then to the unlikeliest of reformers: the party that built segregation into a system — and later dismantled it.

📚 J.B.M. Hertzog, 1914:
“The National Party stands for the protection of the white race, the elevation of the poor white, and the preservation of the Afrikaans language and culture against British imperialism.”
📚 F.W. de Klerk, 2 February 1990:
“The government has decided to unban the ANC, the PAC, and the SACP, and to release Mr. Nelson Mandela unconditionally. This is the beginning of a new South Africa.”

The National Party (NP) ruled South Africa from 1948 to 1994, longer than any other government since Union. It was the architect of apartheid — one of the most comprehensive systems of racial segregation and discrimination the world has ever seen. Yet the same party, under F.W. de Klerk, also negotiated itself out of power, unbanning the ANC and releasing Nelson Mandela. This page tells the story of the historical National Party (1914–2005), its rise, its rule, and its eventual dissolution. It is not about the revived National Party formed in 2008.

🎓 Founding (1914–1948) — Afrikaner Nationalism
1914 — National Party Founded

The National Party is founded in Bloemfontein by J.B.M. Hertzog, a former Boer general and a leading figure in the Afrikaner nationalist movement. The party is formed in reaction to British imperialism and the perceived marginalisation of Afrikaners in the Union of South Africa (formed in 1910). Its core platform: the protection of Afrikaans language and culture, the elevation of poor whites (especially Afrikaners), and the promotion of white supremacy through segregationist policies. [SA History]

1924 — Hertzog Becomes Prime Minister

The NP wins the 1924 general election in coalition with the Labour Party, forming the Pact Government. Hertzog becomes Prime Minister. His government strengthens segregationist legislation, including the Mines and Works Amendment Act (1926) which formalised the colour bar in skilled employment, and the Native Administration Act (1927) which entrenched separate administration for black Africans. [SA History]

1934 — Fusion with South African Party

Hertzog merges the NP with Jan Smuts' South African Party to form the United Party. A purist faction under D.F. Malan breaks away and forms the Gesuiwerde Nasionale Party (Purified National Party), maintaining the original NP's hardline Afrikaner nationalist and segregationist stance. [SA History]

1939 — Split Over WWII

Hertzog resigns after Parliament votes to enter WWII on the side of Britain. He is replaced by Smuts. Malan's Purified NP grows in influence, capitalising on anti-British sentiment among Afrikaners and opposition to South Africa's involvement in the war. [SA History]

1948 — The Apartheid Election

Malan's Herenigde Nasionale Party (Reunited National Party) wins the 1948 general election on a platform of apartheid (literally "apartness") — a far more comprehensive and systematic form of racial segregation than anything that existed before. The NP wins a narrow majority of seats (though not a majority of the vote), defeating Smuts' United Party. Apartheid becomes official government policy. [SA History]

⚠ The Apartheid Era (1948–1994) — The System
📚 Albert Luthuli, 1961 (Nobel Peace Prize Lecture):
“The National Party government has built a system of oppression that denies the vast majority of South Africans the most basic human rights — the right to vote, the right to free movement, the right to work, the right to marry whom they choose, the right to live where they choose. It is a system without moral foundation, and it will not stand.”

The NP's apartheid system was built on a foundation of legislation passed between 1949 and 1970. This section outlines the key pillars of the system.

📖 Key Apartheid Legislation
  • Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act (1949) — Banned marriages between white and non-white people.
  • Immorality Amendment Act (1950) — Extended the ban on sexual relations between races.
  • Population Registration Act (1950) — Required every person to be classified by race: White, Coloured, Indian/Asian, or Native (later Bantu, then Black). This classification determined every aspect of a person's life — where they could live, work, study, and whom they could marry. The Act established a Race Classification Board that made arbitrary and often devastating determinations.
  • Group Areas Act (1950) — Designated specific residential and business areas for each racial group. This led to the forced removal of millions of non-white South Africans from areas classified as "white." The most infamous example: Sophiatown in Johannesburg, destroyed in 1955 to make way for the white suburb of Triomf.
  • Bantu Authorities Act (1951) — Established a system of tribal authorities for black South Africans, laying the groundwork for the Bantustan (homeland) system.
  • Pass Laws (1952) — Required all black South Africans over 16 to carry a reference book (pass) at all times, containing their identity, employment history, and racial classification. Failure to produce a pass on demand was a criminal offence. Hundreds of thousands were arrested each year.
  • Bantu Education Act (1953) — Transferred black education from missionary schools to a state department designed to provide an inferior curriculum that prepared black children for lives of manual labour. Minister Hendrik Verwoerd famously stated: "There is no place for the Bantu in the European community above the level of certain forms of labour."
  • Separate Representation of Voters Act (1956) — Removed Coloured voters from the common roll in the Cape Province, stripping non-white South Africans of any parliamentary representation.
  • Promotion of Bantu Self-Government Act (1959) — Abolished black parliamentary representation entirely and created eight Bantustans (later ten) based on ethnic groups.
📍 The Bantustan System

The crowning achievement of NP social engineering was the Bantustan (Homelands) system. Black South Africans were assigned to one of ten ethnically-defined territories (e.g., Transkei for Xhosa, Bophuthatswana for Tswana, Venda, KwaZulu, etc.). The goal was to deny black people any claim to citizenship or rights in "white" South Africa. The Bantustans were independently administered puppet states, with their own flags, currencies, and governments — but they were economically non-viable, overcrowded, and entirely dependent on South Africa. By the 1980s, the Bantustans held the majority of the black population on only 13% of the land. The system was designed to create a permanent black labour force for white industry while denying black people any political rights in "white" South Africa. [SA History]

👤 Leaders of the National Party (1948–1994)

The National Party had six Prime Ministers (after 1984, State Presidents) during its 46 years in power. Each left a distinct mark on South Africa.

D.F. Malan (1948–1954)

A Dutch Reformed Church dominee (pastor) turned politician, D.F. Malan was the NP's first apartheid Prime Minister. His government laid the legislative foundations of apartheid: the Population Registration Act, Group Areas Act, Pass Laws, and Bantu Education Act. Malan was a fierce Afrikaner nationalist who believed that white (specifically Afrikaner) supremacy was divinely ordained. He resigned in 1954 at age 80. [SA History]

J.G. Strijdom (1954–1958)

An uncompromising Afrikaner nationalist, Strijdom was even more hardline than Malan. He is best known for the contentious removal of Coloured voters from the common roll in 1956 — a move that required a controversial reconstitution of the Senate to secure the necessary two-thirds majority. Strijdom declared: "The white man must retain his supremacy. The idea that the non-white man can be placed on a footing of equality with the white man is totally unacceptable." He died in office in 1958. [SA History]

H.F. Verwoerd (1958–1966)

The "Architect of Apartheid." Verwoerd was a former academic and editor of Die Transvaler. As Minister of Native Affairs (1950–1958) and then Prime Minister, he conceptualised and implemented the grand vision of "separate development" — the Bantustan system. Verwoerd believed apartheid was a benevolent policy that would allow each racial group to develop separately to its fullest potential (in practice, black South Africans were confined to impoverished Bantustans). He withdrew South Africa from the Commonwealth in 1961 after international condemnation of apartheid. Verwoerd was assassinated in Parliament in 1966 by Dimitri Tsafendas, a mixed-race parliamentary messenger. [SA History]

B.J. Vorster (1966–1978)

Vorster was a former Nazi sympathiser (interned during WWII for his pro-German activities) who became Prime Minister after Verwoerd's assassination. His tenure saw the tightening of security legislation (Terrorism Act, 1967), the brutal suppression of the Black Consciousness Movement, and the 1976 Soweto Uprising, in which police killed hundreds of protesting schoolchildren. Vorster also pursued a "détente" policy with other African states, attempting to break South Africa's diplomatic isolation. He resigned in 1978 amid the Information Scandal (Muldergate). [SA History]

P.W. Botha (1978–1989)

"Die Groot Krokodil" (The Great Crocodile) — Botha was a reformer and a hardliner. He introduced the "Total Strategy" to counter the "Total Onslaught" of communism and anti-apartheid forces. He oversaw the 1983 Constitution, which created the Tricameral Parliament (giving Coloured and Indian representation but excluding black South Africans), sparking widespread opposition and the formation of the United Democratic Front (UDF). His rule was marked by a State of Emergency (1985–1990), the brutal suppression of opposition, and the first tentative moves toward reform — including the repeal of the Pass Laws (1986) and Mixed Marriages Act (1985). Botha suffered a stroke in 1989 and was forced to resign. [SA History]

F.W. de Klerk (1989–1994)

The NP's last leader and the man who ended apartheid. De Klerk, on 2 February 1990, announced the unbanning of the ANC and other liberation movements and the release of Nelson Mandela. Over the next four years, he negotiated the transition to democracy alongside Mandela, winning the Nobel Peace Prize jointly with him in 1993. De Klerk's motivations remain debated: was he a genuine reformer who saw the moral bankruptcy of apartheid, or a pragmatic politician who recognised that apartheid was no longer sustainable and wanted to negotiate the best terms for the white minority? The truth likely contains elements of both. His own party split in 1982 when the Conservative Party broke away in opposition to any reform. [SA History]

📖 Apartheid Policies: Separate Development & Homelands

The NP's defining policy was "apartheid" — later rebranded as "separate development" under Verwoerd. It rested on four pillars:

1. Racial Classification

Every South African was classified by race at birth. This classification was used to determine where a person could live, work, study, travel, receive medical care, be buried, and whom they could marry. The Population Registration Act of 1950 created a bureaucracy of race classification that intruded into every aspect of life. The infamous "pencil test" (inserting a pencil into a person's hair to determine racial category) was one of the methods used. Families were sometimes forcibly separated when members were classified into different races.

2. Territorial Segregation

The Group Areas Act (1950) divided South Africa into zones for each racial group. Non-whites were forcibly removed from areas designated as white. Between 1960 and 1983, an estimated 3.5 million people were forcibly removed. Whole communities were bulldozed: Sophiatown (Johannesburg), District Six (Cape Town), Cato Manor (Durban). The Land Acts of 1913 and 1936 reserved 87% of the land for whites, who made up less than 20% of the population. [SA History]

3. The Homeland System (Bantustans)

The Promotion of Bantu Self-Government Act (1959) created ten Bantustans, each ostensibly for a different ethnic group. Four were declared "independent" between 1976 and 1981: Transkei (1976), Bophuthatswana (1977), Venda (1979), and Ciskei (1981). No country except South Africa recognised them. The Bantustan system was a mechanism for massive ethnic cleansing and political disenfranchisement: black South Africans were declared citizens of these puppet states and thus had no claim to citizenship or political rights in "white" South Africa. [SA History]

4. Influx Control & Pass Laws

The pass laws controlled the movement of black South Africans into "white" areas. A black person could not be in an urban area for more than 72 hours without proof of employment. Pass arrests averaged over 1,000 per day in the 1960s. The system was designed to supply white industry with cheap black labour while preventing black people from settling permanently in white areas. The government admitted that between 1916 and 1986, over 18 million people were arrested for pass law offences. [SA History]

5. Inferior Education & Employment Discrimination

The Bantu Education Act (1953) ensured black children received an education designed for manual labour, not intellectual development. The government spent approximately R600 per white child per year in the 1980s, compared to about R150 per black child. Job reservation laws reserved skilled and semi-skilled work for whites. Black workers could not perform skilled labour, join trade unions (until 1979), or receive equal pay for equal work.

🤝 The Transition (1990–1994) — Negotiations & CODESA
📚 Nelson Mandela, on F.W. de Klerk, 1993:
“Mr. de Klerk has made a decisive break with the past. He has demonstrated the courage to acknowledge that apartheid was a crime against humanity. We do not agree on many things, but we agree that the time for negotiation has come.”
2 February 1990 — The Speech

In a dramatic opening of Parliament, F.W. de Klerk announces the unbanning of the ANC, PAC, and SACP, and the imminent release of Nelson Mandela. The speech effectively signals the end of apartheid. [SA History]

1990–1991 — The Preliminaries

The NP government repeals the remaining apartheid laws: the Land Acts (1991), Group Areas Act (1991), and Population Registration Act (1991). The Pass Laws had already been scrapped in 1986. The government enters into bilateral talks with the ANC. The Groote Schuur Minute (May 1990) commits both sides to peaceful negotiations. The Pretoria Minute (August 1990) sees the ANC suspend the armed struggle. [SA History]

1991–1992 — CODESA I & II

The Convention for a Democratic South Africa (CODESA I) meets in December 1991 at the World Trade Centre in Kempton Park. Nineteen parties participate. Key disagreements: the ANC wants a unified, centralised state with a Bill of Rights; the NP wants strong regional powers (to protect white minorities) and power-sharing mechanisms. CODESA II (May 1992) collapses after the Boipatong Massacre (June 1992), when ANC supporters are killed by Inkatha hostel dwellers allegedly backed by security forces. The ANC walks out. Talks resume in September 1992 after the Record of Understanding between Mandela and de Klerk. [SA History]

1993 — Multi-Party Negotiating Process

The Negotiating Council agrees on an interim Constitution. Key compromises: a Government of National Unity (GNU) for five years, a proportional electoral system, strong provincial governments, and a sunset clause protecting civil service jobs. The NP secures entrenched power-sharing through the GNU and provisions requiring consensus on certain decisions. [SA History]

27 April 1994 — First Democratic Election

The ANC wins 62.6% of the vote. The NP comes second with 20.4%. The NP enters the Government of National Unity under Nelson Mandela — de Klerk becomes one of two Deputy Presidents. The NP's 46-year hold on power ends peacefully. [IEC]

📈 Post-1994 — Renamed & Dissolved (1994–2005)
1994–1996 — In Government of National Unity

The NP participates in the GNU under Mandela. De Klerk serves as Deputy President. The NP holds six cabinet portfolios. But the party struggles to define a new identity in a democratic South Africa. Many NP voters are disillusioned by the loss of white political power. [SA History]

1996 — NP Withdraws from GNU

De Klerk withdraws the NP from the Government of National Unity in May 1996, citing the ANC's dominance and the marginalisation of minority parties. The NP becomes the official opposition. [SA History]

1997 — De Klerk Retires from Politics

F.W. de Klerk retires from active politics. Marthinus van Schalkwyk takes over as NP leader. The party faces an existential crisis: can it survive as a democracy without the white minority constituency it relied on?

1999 — Electoral Collapse

In the 1999 general election, the NP collapses to 6.9% of the vote — down from 20.4% in 1994. The Democratic Party (DP) under Tony Leon overtakes the NP as the official opposition. The NP loses most of its white support to the DP, which campaigns on a stronger anti-ANC, anti-corruption platform. [IEC]

2000 — Rebranded as New National Party

In a desperate attempt to reinvent itself, the NP merges with the much smaller Democratic Party (not to be confused with the DP) to form the Democratic Alliance (DA). The merger is short-lived: the NP pulls out of the DA in 2001 and renames itself the New National Party (NNP). [SA History]

2004 — Final Election & Dissolution

The NNP wins only 1.7% of the vote in the 2004 general election, winning just 7 seats. On 9 April 2005, the NNP federal council votes to dissolve the party. Marthinus van Schalkwyk joins the ANC. The National Party, after 91 years of existence, is formally dissolved. [SA History]

⚠ Legacy: The NP's Contradiction

The National Party's legacy is profoundly contradictory. It built one of the most oppressive systems of racial discrimination in modern history — a system that caused immeasurable suffering, destroyed millions of lives, and left South Africa with a legacy of inequality that persists today. Yet the same party, under F.W. de Klerk, recognised that the system was unsustainable and negotiated a peaceful transition to democracy — a transition that few countries undergoing similar transformations have managed.

In 1993, de Klerk told a journalist: "We realised that we had to change. We could not continue to rule a country where the majority had no rights. The alternative was a bloody revolution that would have destroyed everything." Whether this was noble or pragmatic — or both — the NP that created apartheid and the NP that ended it cannot easily be separated. The party's dissolution in 2005 marked the final chapter of a party that shaped South Africa's 20th century more than any other.

Note on the revived National Party: In 2008, a group calling itself the National Party of South Africa was formed by former NP members. This revived party is not the subject of this page. The historical National Party dissolved in 2005.
