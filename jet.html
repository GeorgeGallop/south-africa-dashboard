📖 What Is the Just Energy Transition (JET)?

The Just Energy Transition (JET) is South Africa's national framework for shifting from a coal-dependent economy (~80% of electricity from coal, the highest of any G20 country) to a low-carbon, climate-resilient economy — while ensuring that the transition is just: that workers, communities, and regions dependent on the coal value chain are not left behind. The "just" in JET is not an adjective — it is a binding condition. If the transition destroys livelihoods, it has failed.

The JET is operationalised through the Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP) — an international financing agreement launched at COP26 in Glasgow (November 2021). South Africa was the first country in the world to negotiate a JETP, making it the global pilot for this model of climate finance. The International Partners Group (IPG) — comprising the United States, United Kingdom, European Union, France, and Germany — pledged $8.5 billion over five years (2023–2027) to support South Africa's transition.

The JET is structured around the JET Investment Plan (2023–2027), released by the South African Presidency in November 2023, which identifies a total required investment of R1.5 trillion ($98 billion) over five years across the entire economy — far exceeding the $8.5 billion JETP pledge. The Investment Plan prioritises R133 billion ($6.9 billion) for the electricity sector, R67 billion for green hydrogen, R52 billion for new energy vehicles, and R17 billion for skills development and social support.

⏳ Timeline — From Coal to Clean

2019 — Eskom's JET Office Established

Eskom established its internal Just Energy Transition Office, signalling that the utility recognised the inevitability of decarbonisation and the need to plan for the social and economic consequences of plant closures. Initial planning focused on repurposing retiring coal plants — starting with Komati Power Station — for renewable energy and community development.

2021 — Presidential Climate Commission (PCC) Formed

President Ramaphosa established the Presidential Climate Commission (PCC) — a multi-stakeholder body bringing together government, business, labour, civil society, and academia — to guide South Africa's climate response. The PCC is chaired by Ramaphosa himself, signalling presidential-level commitment. It was the PCC that developed the framework for what would become the JETP negotiation.

November 2021 — COP26: JETP Announced

At the COP26 climate summit in Glasgow, the JETP was formally announced. The IPG pledged $8.5 billion over 3–5 years. The Political Declaration — signed by South Africa and the IPG — committed to "accelerate the decarbonisation of South Africa's electricity system" while "supporting affected workers, communities, and regions." The announcement was globally significant: it was the first time rich nations had collectively pledged finance to help a developing country decarbonise — a test of whether the climate finance promises made since the 2009 Copenhagen COP could actually be delivered.

2022 — Komati Repurposing Begins

The World Bank approved a $497 million loan to Eskom for the Komati Repowering Project — converting the decommissioned 1,000 MW Komati coal plant in Mpumalanga into a renewable energy hub with 150 MW of solar PV, 70 MW of wind, and 150 MW of battery storage. The project includes a comprehensive community development programme: reskilling Eskom workers for green jobs, supporting local small businesses, and creating new economic opportunities in the Komati area. Komati became the global poster child for a just coal-to-clean transition — and also, arguably, a cautionary tale (see below).

November 2022 — JET IP Office Established

The Presidency established the JET Project Management Unit (PMU), headed by former Reserve Bank Deputy Governor Daniel Mminele (later replaced by Joanne Yawitch). The PMU's role: coordinate the complex web of international finance, government departments, Eskom, and civil society to implement the JET Investment Plan. This was recognition that the institutional architecture for managing an $8.5 billion multi-country programme did not exist and had to be built from scratch.

November 2023 — JET Investment Plan Released

After two years of development, the Presidency released the JET Investment Plan 2023–2027. The plan identified a staggering total of R1.5 trillion ($98 billion) needed over five years — far exceeding the $8.5 billion JETP pledge. It prioritised six sectors: electricity, green hydrogen, new energy vehicles, skills development, municipal capacity, and social support. The plan acknowledged that the JETP funds were only ~9% of total required investment and that mobilising private capital and multilateral development bank (MDB) finance was essential.

2024 — Implementation Grants Policy Framework

The JET PMU released the Implementation Grants Policy Framework, detailing how approximately $700 million in grant funding (the non-loan portion of the JETP) would be allocated. Key focus areas: technical assistance, capacity building, community development, and project preparation. The framework also addressed the politically contentious issue of conditionality — what South Africa must do in exchange for the funds (e.g., accelerate coal plant closures, increase renewable energy targets, reform Eskom).

💰 The Money — Where Is It Coming From and Where Is It Going?

⚠️ The most misunderstood fact about the JETP: The $8.5 billion is not a grant. It is overwhelmingly concessional loans (low-interest, long-tenor), commercial loans, and guarantees. Only approximately $700 million (~8%) is grant funding. The rest must be repaid. This means South Africa is taking on significant additional sovereign debt to finance its energy transition — a politically explosive reality in a country with a R420 billion+ Eskom debt burden and a debt-to-GDP ratio approaching 75%.

How the Money Flows — Architecture

The JETP is not a single fund. Money flows through multiple channels, creating a coordination challenge:

  1. Bilateral agreements: Each IPG country negotiates its own agreements with South Africa — the US through USAID and DFC, Germany through KfW and GIZ, etc. Each has its own conditions, reporting requirements, and disbursement timelines.
  2. Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs): The World Bank, African Development Bank (AfDB), and Development Bank of Southern Africa (DBSA) provide co-financing and catalytic capital. The World Bank has already approved the $497 million Komati project.
  3. Climate Investment Funds (CIF): South Africa is accessing the CIF's Accelerating Coal Transition (ACT) programme, which provides concessional finance specifically for coal phase-down.
  4. Private sector mobilisation: The JETP includes guarantees and risk-sharing facilities designed to crowd in private investment — the theoretical goal is to leverage the public $8.5 billion into $30–50 billion in total investment. Whether this leverage materialises is the single biggest question about the programme's success.

✅ What the JET Has Actually Done — Achievements & Disbursements

As of mid-2025 (three years after the Glasgow announcement), the JETP has been slow to disburse and implement. The gap between pledges and actual money flowing is substantial — a pattern consistent with international climate finance globally. Key achievements and challenges:

Achievements
  • Komati Repowering Project (World Bank, $497M): The flagship JET project. Construction began in 2023 on the 150 MW solar, 70 MW wind, and 150 MW battery storage at the decommissioned Komati site. A community development programme has been launched. But — critically — the plant has created far fewer jobs than were lost when Komati's coal units shut down (Komati employed ~350 people; the repowering project creates ~50 permanent jobs). This tension — clean energy creates fewer jobs than coal mining and coal-fired generation — is the central challenge of the "just" in JET.
  • JET Investment Plan adopted by Cabinet (November 2023), providing a strategic framework for coordination.
  • JET PMU operationalised within the Presidency, creating institutional capacity that did not previously exist.
  • Skills development programmes launched: Germany's GIZ and the South African Renewable Energy Technology Centre (SARETEC) have trained hundreds of technicians in solar PV and wind turbine maintenance. The National Skills Fund has allocated R800 million to JET-related skills programmes.
  • Municipal Just Energy Transition programme: Pilot projects in Eskom-dependent municipalities (particularly in Mpumalanga) to diversify local economies away from coal, including support for agriculture, tourism, and small-scale manufacturing.
Challenges & Delays
  • Slow disbursement: By mid-2025, of the $8.5 billion pledged, only approximately $1.5–2.0 billion had actually been disbursed or committed to specific projects. The process of turning political pledges into signed contracts, approved loans, and flowing funds has been far slower than anticipated — bogged down by South African bureaucratic capacity, conditions imposed by IPG partners, and the inherent complexity of multi-country, multi-instrument finance.
  • Conditionality disputes: IPG partners, particularly the EU and UK, have insisted on conditions: accelerated coal plant closures (specifically targeting Camden, Grootvlei, Hendrina, and Komati), measurable renewable energy targets, and Eskom restructuring (unbundling into generation, transmission, and distribution). South Africa has pushed back, arguing that it cannot decommission coal plants while the grid is experiencing load shedding — the priority is energy security, not decarbonisation speed. This tension has stalled negotiations on multiple bilateral agreements.
  • The debt question: The JETP is heavily loan-based (~92%). South Africa's fiscal position is already strained. The Treasury has been reluctant to take on significant additional debt, particularly for projects where the economic return is uncertain. This has created tension between the Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment (DFFE), which champions the JET, and National Treasury, which must manage the country's debt sustainability.
  • Coal Minister vs. Climate Minister: A fundamental structural problem: Minister Gwede Mantashe (Mineral Resources and Energy) is a vocal proponent of coal and gas, while Minister Barbara Creecy (Forestry, Fisheries and Environment) is the JET's champion. The two ministers have publicly contradicted each other on coal phase-down timelines, new coal exploration, and the pace of renewable deployment. This intra-cabinet conflict has paralysed decision-making on several key JET deliverables.

🪨 The Coal Problem — Mpumalanga's Existential Question

The JET is a national programme, but its impact is overwhelmingly concentrated in Mpumalanga province — the heartland of South Africa's coal industry. Mpumalanga hosts:

  • 12 of Eskom's 15 coal-fired power stations (the coal belt from Kendal/Kusile in the west to Arnot/Hendrina in the east).
  • ~90% of South Africa's coal mining — both for Eskom and for export (Richard's Bay Coal Terminal).
  • ~120,000 direct coal jobs (mining + power generation) and an estimated 300,000+ indirect jobs dependent on the coal value chain.

The JET Investment Plan envisions all Eskom coal plants being decommissioned by 2050, with the first wave (Komati, Camden, Grootvlei, Hendrina) closing by 2030. This represents the largest concentrated economic restructuring challenge in South Africa since 1994. Mpumalanga's economy is effectively a coal monoculture — and the JET proposes to end it.

What the JET Promises Mpumalanga
💼 Worker Reskilling

Training programmes to transition coal workers into renewable energy jobs, manufacturing, agriculture, and tourism. The challenge: renewable energy creates far fewer permanent jobs per MW than coal. A coal mine that employs 2,000 people might be replaced by a solar farm that employs 20. Reskilling is essential — but it cannot replace the lost jobs at scale. The JET acknowledges this but has not yet solved it.

🏘️ Community Diversification

Investment in Mpumalanga's "post-coal" economy: agriculture (the province has excellent soil and water), tourism (proximity to Kruger National Park, Panorama Route), light manufacturing, and the circular economy (repurposing coal ash and mine waste). The JET Investment Plan allocates R8.3 billion specifically to Mpumalanga economic diversification.

🔄 Plant Repurposing

Following the Komati model, retiring coal plants are to be repurposed as renewable energy hubs — using existing grid connections, land, and water infrastructure. This preserves some of the economic value of the sites and maintains a connection to the energy sector for host communities. Plans exist for Camden, Grootvlei, and Hendrina — but remain at the feasibility stage.

🏛️ The Politics — Why the JET Is So Contested

The JET is not a technical energy programme — it is a political and social transformation programme disguised as an energy transition. The resistance comes from multiple directions:

⛏️ Trade Unions (COSATU & NUM)

The National Union of Mineworkers (NUM) and COSATU (the trade union federation in the ANC alliance) have been the strongest opponents of rapid coal phase-down. Their position: the JET is being imposed by rich countries that built their wealth on fossil fuels and are now demanding that South Africa sacrifice its workers to solve a problem Europe created. NUM's slogan — "Don't let our blue skies be your greenwash" — captures the anger. COSATU supports the JET in principle but demands that no coal plant closes until replacement jobs are guaranteed, retrenchment packages are funded, and affected communities have approved the transition plans. In practice, this means COSATU has blocked or delayed multiple coal closure decisions.

🛢️ The Minerals-Energy Complex

South Africa's political economy has been built on coal for over a century. The "Minerals-Energy Complex" (MEC) — the interlocking network of mining houses, Eskom, Transnet, and the state — is one of the most powerful interest groups in the country. Coal provides ~80% of electricity, ~30% of liquid fuels (via Sasol's coal-to-liquids), and is the country's third-largest export earner (R240+ billion/year). The MEC has actively resisted the JET: Minister Mantashe has publicly advocated for new coal-fired power (the proposed 3,000 MW Coal IPP programme), extended the life of old coal plants, and criticised the pace of renewable deployment. The JET threatens the economic foundation of an entire political class.

🇿🇦 Energy Security vs. Climate

South Africa is experiencing its worst energy crisis in history — Stage 6+ load shedding, an estimated economic cost of R500+ billion per year. The immediate priority for most South Africans — including the Presidency — is keeping the lights on, not reducing carbon emissions. The JET's call for accelerated coal plant closures is in direct tension with the reality that South Africa needs every megawatt it can get. This has led to a compromise: Eskom will delay coal plant closures until sufficient new capacity (renewables + gas + storage) is online. But this delay has frustrated IPG partners and slowed JETP disbursement. The fundamental problem: you cannot close coal plants while load shedding at Stage 6. The JET's decarbonisation timeline may simply be incompatible with South Africa's energy reality.

🌍 The International Dimension

The JETP is a global test case. If it succeeds — if $8.5 billion can catalyse a just, rapid decarbonisation in South Africa — the model will be replicated in Indonesia ($20 billion JETP signed 2022), Vietnam ($15.5 billion, 2022), Senegal, and India. If it fails — if the money doesn't flow, if the conditions are rejected, if the political resistance is too strong — it will undermine confidence in the entire JETP model and in rich countries' commitment to climate finance. South Africa is carrying the weight of global climate diplomacy on its shoulders — and the outcome is uncertain.

🔮 What's Next — The Road Ahead

📋 JETP Implementation Phase (2025–2027)

The remaining JETP funds must be committed to specific projects. The focus: (1) Transmission grid expansion (critical to connect renewable energy from the Northern Cape and Eastern Cape to demand centres); (2) Municipal capacity building (most municipalities lack the technical ability to manage distributed generation and energy trading); (3) Private sector mobilisation (leveraging the $8.5B into $30–50B); (4) Community and worker support programmes. The clock is ticking: the JETP runs through 2027.

🔌 Eskom's Just Energy Transition Strategy

Eskom's internal JET strategy envisions repurposing all coal plants as they retire — solar, wind, battery storage, and, controversially, gas. The utility's 2050 vision is net-zero, with coal phased out entirely. But Eskom's immediate priority is survival: the R420 billion debt, declining EAF (Energy Availability Factor), and unbundling into separate entities. The JET is one priority among many — and it is not always the most urgent one from Eskom's perspective.

🏛️ Presidential Climate Commission Role

The PCC is transitioning from a planning body to an implementation oversight body. It will monitor JETP disbursements, track social outcomes (jobs created/lost, community investment), and hold government and IPG partners accountable. The PCC's credibility — as a genuinely independent voice, not a government mouthpiece — will be critical to maintaining public trust in the JET process.

🇿🇦 Political Will Post-2024 Elections

The 2024 national elections produced a coalition government (Government of National Unity) with the ANC, DA, IFP, and other parties. The DA's entry into national government has shifted energy policy: the DA strongly supports renewable energy and the JET and is pushing for accelerated implementation. However, the coalition dynamics also mean that decisions require negotiation and compromise — making bold, fast moves on coal closures unlikely. The political centre of gravity has shifted toward the JET, but the pace remains slow.

🌍 The Global JETP Model at Stake

South Africa's JETP is being watched by every developing country considering decarbonisation. If the JETP delivers — if the money flows, the coal plants close, and communities are genuinely supported — it will be the proof-of-concept that unlocks billions in additional climate finance for the Global South. If it stalls, if conditionality disputes and political resistance prevent implementation, the JETP model may die with South Africa's experience. The pressure to succeed is not just domestic — it is global.

⚠️ The Unanswered Question

Who pays for the just transition? The JETP's $8.5 billion is a fraction of what is needed. The Investment Plan's R1.5 trillion price tag is overwhelming. South Africa cannot afford it through sovereign debt. The private sector will only invest where returns are sufficient — and the "just" part of the transition (worker support, community diversification) generates no commercial return. The core tension: the transition must be just, but justice costs money that nobody wants to pay. Until this tension is resolved, the JET will remain more aspiration than reality.
