The Transformation Fund

A R100 billion aggregated fund to finance businesses owned and managed by black entrepreneurs — R20 billion a year for five years. It is the dtic's flagbearer transformation instrument, built on the B-BBEE Act rather than on new taxes, and administered through a ring-fenced Special Purpose Vehicle. This page sets out who is involved, exactly where the money comes from and where it goes, the governance and legal scaffolding around it, and which South African industries it will reshape — plus where it is likely to affect sectors not named in the draft.

Overview

The Transformation Fund is an aggregated national fund — not a new tax, and not a new department. Its purpose is to fix a specific and well-documented failure: majority black-owned businesses cannot get affordable finance, cannot get post-investment support, and cannot get into the supply chains that actually pay. Government's instrument of choice is to pool the money that already exists for transformation — corporate enterprise and supplier development spend, multinational equity-equivalent obligations, competition-merger commitments and departmental sector budgets — into one professionally managed vehicle.

The legal foundation is deliberately B-BBEE, not fiscal. Section 7(2) and section 9(2) of the Constitution require the state to promote equality and to adopt measures advancing people disadvantaged by unfair discrimination. Section 2(h) of the B-BBEE Act obliges government to increase the effective economic participation of majority black-owned and managed enterprises — including SMMEs — and to enhance their access to financial and non-financial support. Section 11(2)(b) requires a plan for financing B-BBEE, including incentive schemes. The Fund is presented as the mechanism that discharges those duties.

What the Fund is — and what it explicitly is not

What it is
  • An aggregated fund pulling transformation money into one pool instead of dozens of fragmented ESD programmes.
  • Administered through a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) with a ring-fenced bank account, separately accounted for.
  • A tax-exempt vehicle, so contributions can be redeployed into the market in full rather than eroded by tax.
  • Deployed through debt, grants and equity — including start-up grants and listed shares for broad-based equity structures.
  • Both wholesale and direct lending, so it can fund through intermediaries and deal directly with large transactions.
  • A contributor of non-financial support — business planning, feasibility and environmental impact studies, accreditation, training, export readiness, mentorship.
What it is not
  • Not a new tax. The dtic has stated the Fund imposes no additional tax burden or requirements beyond mechanisms already in the B-BBEE Act.
  • Not repayable. Contributions from measured entities are irrevocable and unconditional; the Fund has no obligation to repay a contributor.
  • Not a sector-specific fund of the kind already in existence — it is designed around key sectors and impact transactions, not a single funding threshold.
  • Not unaccounted. The SPV must table an annual report on funds under management, investments made and performance, and reports to Parliament through the Minister of Trade, Industry and Competition.
  • Not a standalone idea. It sits alongside the NEF, IDC, DBSA, SEDFA and the Black Industrialists programme rather than replacing them.
The design idea in one line. Make contributing to transformation easier and more valuable for a company than running its own ESD programme — one contribution, an immediate B-BBEE Code 400 credit, an income-tax deduction under section 18A, and exemption from donations tax under section 56(1)(h) — and the money aggregates itself.

Who is involved

Three groups have to make this work at the same time: the state (policy, appointment power and seed money), the financial system (fund management, co-lending and distribution) and the private sector money being redirected (corporate ESD budgets, multinational equity equivalents, institutional capital). The draft sets out exactly how the first group is wired into the vehicle, and the second and third have been filling in through public announcements since mid-2025.

The state side

The dtic — custodian and promoter

The Department of Trade, Industry and Competition owns the policy, published the draft concept document, and is named as the institution that would appoint the SPV board and the oversight committee.

  • Minister Parks Tau — publishes the Fund documents, appoints the Board and Oversight Committee, and carries the SPV's reporting line to Parliament.
  • Deputy Minister Zuko Godlimpi — led the delegation briefing the Portfolio Committee on Trade and Industry in June 2025.
  • Ms Susan Mangole, Acting DDG: Transformation and Competition — lead official on the Fund's design and on the public-comment process.
  • DDG: Transformation and Competition — the desk that has engaged the private investment sector directly.
The NEF — implementing partner

The National Empowerment Fund SOC Limited is the operational partner named in the Fund's public communications. It brings the thing the Fund most needs: an existing credit and business-support machine for majority black-owned businesses.

  • Established under the National Empowerment Fund Act 105 of 1998 to promote economic ownership of income-generating assets by historically disadvantaged persons.
  • Authorised Financial Services Provider (FSP 55436) under FAIS, and a licensed credit provider (NCRCP 21442) under the National Credit Act — so it can already lend and advise lawfully.
  • Mr Mzi Dayimane, CEO, is the public face of the NEF's involvement.
  • Brings product know-how from the iMbewu, uMnotho, Women Empowerment, Rural/Township and Strategic Projects funds.

The money side — who else is expected to pay in

Measured entities
Every company with a B-BBEE scorecard and ESD obligations. They become contributors, and receive Code 400 credits, a section 18A deduction and section 56(1)(h) exemption in return.
Multinationals
Firms that cannot meet the ownership element of the Codes. The Codes let them set aside 25% of the value of their South African operations — the EEIP route — which the draft directs into the Fund.
Institutional capital
Retirement funds and asset managers, courted through a high-level engagement hosted by 27four and attended by the Minister and the NEF CEO. This is the most plausible source of the scale the Fund needs.
Other state sources
Sector budgets from national and provincial departments — Tourism, Rural Development, Agriculture, provincial economic development — plus the Competition Commission's public interest commitments from merger conditions, and donors and development banks.

The structural detail that matters most. The Fund is not a trust, not a government department and not a bank. It is an SPV with its own ring-fenced bank account, whose proceeds are separately accounted for and cannot be mixed with the SPV's other operations — and which is tax-exempt, so a rand contributed arrives in the market as a full rand. That combination is what lets the dtic promise contributors an immediate scorecard benefit without asking for a tax increase.

Where the money comes from

This is the most important — and most misunderstood — part of the design. The R100 billion is not appropriated from the fiscus and it is not a new levy. It is assembled from four channels that already exist in law or in corporate budgets, redirected into one pool. Understanding which channel each rand comes from tells you who actually bears the cost, and therefore how likely the Fund is to reach its target.

CHANNEL 1
Enterprise & Supplier Development

The largest expected channel. Companies with a B-BBEE scorecard currently spend on their own ESD programmes. The proposal is to amend the Codes so that a contribution to the Transformation Fund earns B-BBEE points immediately, making the Fund a simpler route than running a programme in-house. ESD money paid across is not repayable to the contributing entity.

Expected to dominate the R100bn
CHANNEL 2
Equity Equivalent Investment Programme

Multinationals often cannot meet the ownership element of the Codes without selling equity. The Codes allow them instead to set aside 25% of the value of their South African operations for transformation. The draft channels EEIP funds into the Fund so they reach a broader set of beneficiaries than each firm could reach alone.

Coverage: 25% of SA operations value
CHANNEL 3
Competition Commission commitments

Public-interest conditions attached to merger approvals — employment, supplier development, local procurement, enterprise funding — become a source of capital for the Fund instead of sitting with individual merger parties.

Merger-condition derived
CHANNEL 4
Government & public entities

Seed capital from national and provincial departments and organs of state — the dtic frames departmental sector funding as seed money intended to attract private contributions, not to carry the Fund. Includes DFIs and state funds such as the UIF, IDC, DBSA and SEDFA.

The honest reading. Because the R100 billion is assembled from money that is already committed to transformation in some form, the Fund's real effect is to change how that money is deployed — not to add R100 billion of new capital to the economy. Whether the total actually grows depends on two things: whether contributors add to their existing spend, and whether the Fund attracts genuinely new institutional capital (pension funds, development banks, offshore investors) that was never B-BBEE money at all. The 27four engagement with retirement fund managers is aimed squarely at the second.

What makes it worth a contributor's while

Scorecard credits

Contributors would receive ESD credits under Code 400 of the Codes — and the draft proposes an amendment so points are earned immediately upon contribution, rather than after years of programme administration.

Tax treatment

Contributions are generally exempt under section 56(1)(h) of the Income Tax Act, 1962 — so no donations tax — and qualify for a deduction under section 18A once a receipt is issued confirming the contribution.

No repayment obligation

Contributions are irrevocable and unconditional, and the Fund has no obligation to repay them. In exchange, the contributor gives up control of how the money is deployed — the central trade-off of the design.

A single counterparty

One Participation Agreement replaces a portfolio of ESD relationships, and the Fund does the sourcing, due diligence and post-investment support that corporates say they find hardest to resource.

What the public comments asked for. After the draft was published, the dtic summarised the inputs it received: clarity on how the Fund will work with existing — and well-functioning — ESD funds; a clear transformation index to measure impact; and a requirement that big corporations complement the money with market access, technical skills development and infrastructure support. Those three asks map directly onto the risks section below.

How the money flows

The mechanics are deliberately simple. A contributor signs one agreement; the money lands in a ring-fenced account; the SPV invests it; whatever comes back is recycled into the next investment. The complexity sits in the governance around the middle step, not in the plumbing.

STEP 1
Participation Agreement

A measured entity, department, donor or corporate decides to contribute and signs a Participation Agreement with the SPV. This regulates the terms of the contribution and the uses to which the SPV may put it in qualifying development initiatives.

STEP 2
Ring-fenced account

Contributions are paid into a bank account in the name of the SPV. Those proceeds are ring-fenced from the SPV's other accounts and operations and separately accounted for. The SPV is tax-exempt, so nothing leaks to tax.

STEP 3
Investment

The fund manager and Investment Committee deploy capital — directly or through intermediaries — into majority black-owned and black women-owned enterprises by way of debt, grants and equity.

STEP 4
Recycling

Where investments earn returns or are repayable, the proceeds are paid back into the Fund and reinvested, creating an additional source of funding for further investments. Over time this is what turns a fixed pool into a revolving facility.

STEP 5
Reporting

Quarterly investment reports to the Board; quarterly reports from the Board to the Oversight Committee; an annual report on funds under management, investments made and performance; and an AGM for all stakeholders. The SPV reports to Parliament through the Minister.

The allocation rules — who gets served

The draft does not simply say "majority black-owned". It layers specific set-asides on top, which is what determines which businesses actually see money.

Designated groups

The distribution rules would mandate that a prescribed amount of available resources be allocated to entities owned by majority black designated groups:

  • Black women-owned businesses — framed in the draft as the critical priority for redressing gender disparities
  • Youth-owned businesses
  • Businesses owned by people living with disabilities
Township and rural ring-fence

A portion of the Fund is set aside exclusively for entities based in township and rural areas. The conditions are strict and testable:

  • The business must be located in a township or rural area
  • It must mainly employ people from those areas
  • Grants and concessionary funding are explicitly contemplated for riskier rural and township businesses — unlike existing ESD funds, which lend only
Black Industrialists

A portion of the distribution is reserved exclusively for entities that meet the definition of Black Industrialists under the Black Industrialist Policy — the manufacturing and value-adding end of the economy, rather than services alone.

Stage and condition of business

All majority black-owned entities qualify regardless of stage: greenfield and brownfield projects, venture capital projects, and distressed clients in need of revitalisation. That last category is unusual — most public funds avoid distressed businesses.

The industries the draft does not name — but which will be affected immediately

This is the part worth flagging. Because the Fund raises capital through intermediaries, invests directly and wholesale, and pays for feasibility studies, environmental impact assessments, accreditations, training and post-investment support, it creates demand in the financial and professional services economy before a single beneficiary is funded.

Banking & DFIs

A R100 billion pool is a wholesale funding opportunity. Banks and development finance institutions become co-lenders, intermediaries and distribution channels — and compete for the Fund's mandate.

Asset & fund management

The SPV intends to register as a Financial Services Provider. That creates a new institutional mandate — the 27four partnership is the template — and pulls pension-fund capital into transformation investing.

Legal, advisory & transaction services

Participation Agreements, due diligence, structuring, Investment Charter and Board Charter compliance. Every contribution and every investment is a transaction that needs professional execution.

Engineering, EIA & feasibility consulting

The Fund funds business planning, feasibility and environmental impact studies directly. That is paid work for consulting engineers and environmental practitioners — and it is what turns an idea into a bankable project.

Assurance, audit & impact measurement

A quarterly reporting cycle, an annual report and a proposed Transformation Index as a benchmark all need measurement infrastructure. This is a new reporting market, and it is what will determine whether the Fund's claims are verifiable.

Training & business development

Business training, financial management training, export readiness, mentorship and the formalisation of informal traders. Skills providers, SETAs and business development services become delivery partners.

The pattern to watch. Nearly every named sector is one where South Africa already has substantial industrial capability but skewed ownership. The Fund's bet is that capital plus market access — not capital alone — is what changes ownership. That is why the draft says access to markets and technical support will be "prioritised and adequately funded above all pillars of the Fund", and why the public comments pushed so hard on requiring large corporates to open their supply chains, not just their cheque books.

The township and rural economy

This is the most distinctive part of the Fund's design, and the part that most clearly separates it from the ESD programmes it is meant to aggregate. A portion of the Fund is set aside exclusively for businesses in township and rural areas — and the test is strict: the business must be located in those areas and must mainly employ people from those areas. That rules out an urban company with a satellite branch.

The reasoning in the document is blunt: means of production and economic activity in South Africa are heavily skewed towards urban areas, and a centralised fund spread evenly is not the same as a fund that deliberately corrects that skew. Rural and township economies are largely informal, which is precisely why they are under-financed — informal businesses cannot service conventional debt.

The six township opportunities the document identifies

Retail & convenience shops

Around 150,000 spaza shops contribute significantly to the economy, and large retailers are moving in with smaller-format stores. Competition is intensifying, and the Fund's interest is in who owns and supplies them — not only who shops there.

Informal services

Fast-food outlets, bakeries, shebeens, hawkers, minibus taxi operators, mechanics and hair salons. These meet essential needs in the community and are the largest single employer bloc in township economies — and the hardest to finance conventionally.

Digital & financial services

Despite high mobile penetration there is a lack of financial infrastructure in townships. The document frames this as an opening for fintech solutions and digital payment systems — improving both safety and convenience.

Entrepreneurship & small business

The general case: training, funding and market access for local entrepreneurs. This is where the Fund's grants and concessionary instruments do the work that loans cannot.

Real estate & housing

Backroom rentals, student accommodation (including commercial properties) and affordable housing. High demand for accommodation in townships, and a sector with room for developers who can get finance.

Agriculture & food production

Urban farming and local food production to improve food security and create employment — supporting small-scale farmers and food producers that operate within township settings.

The five proposed strategies

  • Train, upskill and formalise informal traders through ESD initiatives, in collaboration with relevant government agencies — moving businesses from informal to bankable.
  • Improve profit margins and sustainability of informal traders by using the purchasing power of the more established retailers that the Fund may support. This is the mechanism that makes "market access" concrete rather than rhetorical.
  • Develop small-scale farmers operating in the secondary economy who supply fresh produce, which would potentially minimise value-chain disruptions.
  • Localise ownership of land, retail and production — shifting who owns the assets in a township economy, not just who trades in it.
  • Digitise economic activity in townships as an enabler, rather than treating technology as a separate sector.
Why this is the section that will decide whether the Fund works. Large transactions are easier to write, easier to report and easier to defend — and they are where the headlines come from. The township and rural ring-fence is where the Fund's own stated purpose lives, and it requires the hardest work: small tickets, informal counterparties, grant instruments, and local employment verification. If the township allocation is quietly absorbed into a handful of larger urban deals, the Fund will have failed on its own terms regardless of how much money it raises.

Governance — who is accountable to whom

The Fund's governance model is a deliberate blend of public accountability and private-sector discipline. Policy and appointment power sit with the Minister; strategy sits with a board that is half public and half private; and transaction decisions sit with an investment committee on which private and public representatives both hold voting rights. In principle that is a structure designed so that neither side can capture the Fund alone.

The structure

  • Minister of Trade, Industry and Competition — appoints the Board and the Oversight Committee; holds the reporting line to Parliament.
  • Oversight Committee — 9 members: a Chairperson, three ministers, and five members from business and social groups. Overseas the Board.
  • SPV Board of Directors — 8 members: four public-sector and four private-sector representatives. Sets strategy and oversees the fund manager. Meets at least quarterly.
  • SPV fund manager — the executive that runs the Fund day to day and executes transactions.
  • Investment Committee — four representatives per sector. When Transformation Fund transactions are considered, two private-sector and two public-sector representatives sit on it with voting rights.
  • Parliament — the Portfolio Committee on Trade and Industry receives the SPV's reporting through the Minister.
  • Annual General Meeting — convened by the SPV to report on activities to all stakeholders.
Ring-fencing is the control that matters most. The Fund's proceeds sit in a bank account held in the SPV's name but ring-fenced from the SPV's other accounts and operations, and separately accounted for. Combined with tax-exempt status, that means a contributor can trace a rand from their Participation Agreement into a specific, separately reported investment pool.

  • Investment Charter — regulates all the activities of the Fund, including how transactions are approved.
  • ESG Policy — drafted and approved by the SPV, setting the framework for responsible corporate conduct, aligned to NEMA.
Why the two-tier oversight exists. A single board appointed by a Minister carries concentration-of-power risk. Adding an Oversight Committee above it — with three ministers plus five business and social group members — inserts a second, differently composed layer between the Fund and the executive. Whether it works depends entirely on who is appointed and whether the Committee is willing to challenge the Board. Neither the document nor anything published since sets out an appointment process, a conflict-of-interest policy or a fit-and-proper test.

Legal and regulatory framework

The Fund has to comply with a wide regulatory universe simultaneously. The document splits it into a primary legislative base (what creates the obligation), a functional framework (how the entity must behave), and a supporting environment (the wider small-business and competition law it must work within).

Act / instrumentWhy it applies
Primary legislation — the basis for the Fund
B-BBEE Codes of Good Practice ("the Codes") Set the interpretive principles of B-BBEE and the basis for determining compliance. Compliance with the Codes is the primary basis on which the Fund is established.
Functional legislation — how the entity must operate
National Empowerment Fund Act 105 of 1998 Establishes the NEF trust and its objects — promoting economic ownership of income-generating assets by historically disadvantaged persons, and providing for control, management and dissolution of the trust.
Industrial Development Act 22 of 1940 Promotes the establishment of new industries and industrial undertakings and the development of existing ones — the industrial-policy hook.
B-BBEE Act 53 of 2003, as amended Establishes the legislative framework for B-BBEE; empowers the Minister to issue codes of good practice and publish transformation charters; establishes the B-BBEE Advisory Council.
Public Finance Management Act 1 of 1999 (PFMA) Regulates financial management in national and provincial government, and the responsibilities of persons entrusted with it. Applies given the state's involvement and the SPV's public reporting line.
Companies Act 71 of 2008 Governs the formation and maintenance of the SPV as a company, and sets corporate governance standards.
Financial Intelligence Centre Act 38 of 2001 (FICA) Anti-money-laundering and terrorist-financing duties. The document notes the NEF is an accountable institution under FICA as a money remitter, and that stringent requirements apply to the SPV.
Public Procurement Act of 2024 Consolidates previously fragmented procurement law; section 217(1) of the Constitution requires procurement to be fair, equitable, transparent, competitive and cost-effective. Introduces a tribunal and processes to investigate and charge contraventions.
Supporting environment — the wider small-business and competition framework
National Small Enterprise Amendment Act 21 of 2024 Establishes the Small Enterprise Development Finance Agency (SEDFA) to provide financial and non-financial support to small enterprises; allows the Minister to set criteria for classifying micro, small and medium enterprises.
SMME Block Exemptions Regulations 2024 Previously the Competition Act prohibited certain agreements and practices between SMMEs; these block exemptions now permit them, with prior approval from the Competition Commission. This is what makes collective action by small suppliers lawful.
Financial-sector licensing — because the SPV will itself be a financial institution
Financial Advisory and Intermediary Services Act 37 of 2002 (FAIS) The SPV has undertaken to register and obtain an FSP licence. This is what would position it as an entity of choice for government departments and large private companies wanting partner with SOEs for financial advisory and intermediary services.
Financial Sector Regulation Act 9 of 2017 (FSRA) Establishes the Prudential Authority and the Financial Sector Conduct Authority. As an FSP, the SPV must subscribe to the Treating Customers Fairly principles.
National Environmental Management Act 107 of 1998 (NEMA) Promotes co-operative governance and ensures people's rights are upheld while recognising the necessity of economic development. The SPV has drafted and approved its own ESG Policy under this framework.
Collective Investment Schemes Control Act 45 of 2002 (CISCA) Governs collective investment schemes, in which two or more members of the public pool money or assets. The document notes the SPV has discussed entering this space via savings vehicles for staff and investees — including stokvels — which would require licensing.

Tax treatment of contributions

Section 56(1)(h) — Income Tax Act, 1962

Contributions made to the Transformation Fund will generally be exempt under section 56(1)(h). For a corporate contributor, this removes donations tax as a cost of contributing.

Section 18A — Income Tax Act, 1962

Contributions qualify for a deduction under section 18A upon issuing of a confirmation of receipt in respect of contributions made to the Fund. Practically, the contribution is treated like an approved public benefit donation.

The Transformation Index

The document proposes a Transformation Fund Index as a benchmark to measure and track the Fund's impact — focusing on inclusivity and compliance with B-BBEE objectives. This is a direct response to public-comment feedback asking for a clear way to measure impact, and it is the mechanism that would let an outsider judge the Fund's claims rather than accept them.

The regulatory gap worth naming. The document is explicit that the Fund's design requires an amendment to the B-BBEE Codes — specifically so entities can contribute to the Fund and earn B-BBEE points immediately. Until that amendment is made, the single strongest incentive in the whole design does not exist. Everything else in the structure can be built first; this cannot be worked around.

Timeline — what has actually happened

Announced early in 2025, the Fund has moved through consultation and into capital raising. The pace is the story: the concept document has not been replaced by a final policy document, and the disclosed capital commitments remain a small fraction of the target.

February 2025

President Cyril Ramaphosa announces the establishment of the Fund. The dtic later describes this announcement as the origin point of the initiative.

19 March 2025

Minister Parks Tau publishes the Draft Transformation Fund Concept Document for public comment, with inputs directed to a dedicated dtic email address. The document sets out the R100 billion target, the R20 billion-per-annum capitalisation plan, the funding channels, the investment approach and the proposed governance structure.

24 April 2025

The public commentary deadline is extended — first to 7 May, then to 28 May 2025 — to allow stakeholders more time.

3 June 2025

A delegation led by Deputy Minister Zuko Godlimpi briefs the Portfolio Committee on Trade and Industry on the work done to set up the Fund. The dtic confirms the R100 billion will be sourced from Competition Commission public interest commitments, ESD funds, Equity Equivalent Investments from multinationals, and other government funding initiatives — and states plainly that the Fund imposes no additional tax burden. An oversight committee of nine members from government and the private sector is confirmed.

8 August 2025

The dtic and 27four Investment Managers publicly reaffirm their commitment to the Fund following a high-level engagement hosted by 27four and attended by the Minister, the NEF CEO Mr Mzi Dayimane and the dtic's DDG for Transformation and Competition. Retirement fund managers are the target audience — the first serious attempt to pull institutional capital into the vehicle.

25 February 2026

The dtic convenes all 11 B-BBEE Sector Charter Councils collectively for the first time. The session agrees a three-point action approach — address funding mechanisms, optimise implementation within the current legal framework, and review institutional architecture where systems are not working — and the outcomes are to be consolidated for Cabinet as part of a wider B-BBEE framework review. Data presented shows black ownership at approximately 29%, JSE-listed companies at 31% black ownership, black women's ownership around 12%, management control between 39% and 51%, and transaction values of roughly R600 billion.

12 August 2026

Speaking at the Black Business Council's Annual Summit, Minister Tau reports the Fund's first disclosed capital partnerships: UIF (R500m), DBSA (R250m), Vodacom (R400m) and IBM (R220m) — R1.37 billion in total. He also reports that the IDC facilitated R26.6 billion in transformation funding, of which approximately R5.6 billion went to women entrepreneurs and women-empowered businesses, and describes the Transformation Fund as "South Africa's Transformation Jet Engine".

Still outstanding

No public announcement of the SPV's registration, the appointment of the Board or Oversight Committee, the adoption of the Investment Charter, or the amendment to the B-BBEE Codes that would let contributions earn points immediately. No final (non-draft) Transformation Fund policy document has been published. These are the milestones to watch.

Where that leaves the Fund. Roughly eighteen months from announcement to the first disclosed capital commitments, with the founding document still in draft. That is not unusual for a vehicle of this complexity, but it does mean that any assessment of the Fund today is an assessment of a design — not of a track record.

Industry impact — who gains, and who has to adjust

A fund that aggregates R100 billion of transformation spend does not create a level playing field — it changes which players hold the advantage. The table below separates the clear beneficiaries from the industries whose business model has to change, because that distinction is where most of the practical consequence sits.

Clear beneficiaries
  • Majority black-owned businesses in named sectors — agriculture and agro-processing, mining services and beneficiation, manufacturing, services, infrastructure, tourism, renewables and technology.
  • Township and rural businesses — the only channel in the system that contemplates grants and concessionary funding rather than debt, and the only one with a geographic ring-fence.
  • Black women-owned businesses — a mandated prescribed allocation, and the group the document singles out as the priority for redressing gender disparities.
  • Youth- and disability-owned businesses — designated groups with prescribed allocation rules.
  • Black industrialists — a reserved portion of the distribution, with no funding limit for impact transactions. This is where the largest tickets will go.
  • Early-stage and pre-feasibility projects — the Fund pays for business planning, feasibility studies and environmental impact assessments, which is precisely the stage at which most projects currently die.
  • Distressed businesses — explicitly qualifying for support, which almost no commercial fund will offer.
  • Asset managers and fund managers — a new institutional mandate, with 27four as the early mover.
  • Banks and DFIs — co-lending, wholesale funding lines and distribution roles.
  • Professional services — legal, advisory, engineering, environmental, assurance and business development services.
Industries that have to adjust
  • Large corporates running in-house ESD programmes. The Fund's entire value proposition is that one contribution is easier than administering a programme. Corporate ESD teams, supplier development functions and their internal budgets are directly in the path of that shift.
  • Established ESD intermediaries and third-party fund managers. If ESD spend redirects into a single national pool, the organisations currently managing that money for corporates face a smaller market — this was raised explicitly in the public comments, which asked for clarity on how the Fund works with well-functioning existing ESD funds.
  • Providers that corporate ESD currently funds — training companies, incubators, business development services and existing supplier-development beneficiaries. They may gain from the Fund's larger pool, or lose their direct corporate relationship, depending on how implementation lands.
  • Multinationals relying on the EEIP route. The 25%-of-SA-operations set-aside becomes a contribution to a central fund rather than a self-designed programme — less control, less visibility of their own brand on the outcome.
  • Commercial lenders to SMMEs. A public fund willing to take equity risk, fund distressed businesses and lend concessionally below market pulls the floor out from under high-margin lending to the same borrowers.
  • Competition-affected sectors. Where merger public-interest commitments are redirected to the Fund, the merger parties lose the ability to point to (and control) their own remedies.
  • Businesses that have relied on B-BBEE ownership transactions rather than ESD. The Fund concentrates on productive participation and value-chain entry, not on ownership deals — the emphasis of the whole policy shifts.

Risks and open questions

These are not hypotheticals invented from outside. Most of them were raised in the public comment process itself, and the dtic acknowledged several of them when it briefed Parliament in June 2025. They are the questions the Fund's design still has to answer.

1 · Redirecting ESD money may defund what already works

The Fund's largest channel is corporate ESD spend. Pulling that into one national pool is efficient in theory, but well-functioning ESD programmes build long-term supplier relationships that a central fund cannot replicate. The public comments specifically requested clarity on how the Fund will work with existing, functioning ESD funds — and the answer is not yet in the public domain.

2 · R100 billion is largely money that already exists

Because there is no new tax, the Fund aggregates spend that is already committed to transformation in some form. If contributors simply move existing budgets across, the net new capital is close to zero — the change is in delivery, not volume. The counter is that central management may deploy the same money better; the risk is that the headline figure is read as new money when it is not.

3 · Compliance has been the weak link, not capital

The document's own evidence shows measured entities achieving roughly 44–61% of ESD targets over five years. If the underlying problem is that corporations under-deliver on existing obligations, creating a new destination for the money does not automatically fix the under-delivery. Enforcement has to change too.

4 · Concentration of power in one vehicle

The Minister appoints both the Board and the Oversight Committee. The SPV is tax-exempt, holds a ring-fenced pool, and intends to become a licensed financial services provider that can raise from pension funds. No fit-and-proper test, conflict-of-interest policy or appointment process for either body has been published.

5 · Slow capital formation

Announced February 2025; first disclosed capital commitments R1.37 billion in August 2026 against a R100 billion target. If the pace of the first eighteen months continues, the five-year capitalisation plan slips. This is the difference between a R100bn fund and a much smaller one with a R100bn name.

6 · The township allocation is the easiest to erode

Large urban transactions are simpler, cheaper to process and better for reporting. Small township grants are the opposite. Without published allocation percentages and verified local-employment tests, the ring-fence can be met on paper while the money concentrates elsewhere.

7 · Fronting risk under "majority black-owned"

Fronting is an established problem in B-BBEE ownership structures. A fund deploying R100 billion on ownership-defined eligibility has to verify control, not just shareholding — otherwise the largest transactions may be structured to qualify on paper rather than in substance.

8 · "No funding limit" without a stated ceiling

Removing funding limits is what lets the Fund anchor large industrial transactions — and it is also what allows a small number of very large deals to absorb most of the pool. The document does not set a maximum transaction size or a concentration limit.

9 · Grant funding needs a sunset rule

Grants and concessionary funding for risky township and rural businesses are a genuine improvement over loan-only ESD funds. But the document does not describe graduation criteria — when a business moves from grant to debt — or how grant-dependent beneficiaries become self-sustaining.

10 · Market access is promised but not enforced

The document states that access to markets and technical support will be prioritised above all other pillars — because finance without a buyer does not create a business. But it does not yet impose enforceable procurement commitments on the corporates whose supply chains would have to open. The public comments pushed for exactly this; it remains the least guaranteed part of the design.

The question that decides everything else. Is the Transformation Fund primarily new capital for black-owned business, or a better-managed route for capital that was already committed? The dtic's own framing — no new tax, no new burden, sourced from existing ESD, EEIP, competition and departmental channels — points to the second. That does not make it less valuable: better deployment of existing money is a real gain, and a single R100bn pool can do things forty separate corporate ESD programmes cannot. But it does mean the Fund should be judged on deployment quality, speed and reach — not on the size of its headline number.
