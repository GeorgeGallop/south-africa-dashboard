📄 National Rail Policy (2022)

White Paper on National Rail Policy — Gazette No. 46397, 20 May 2022

The National Rail Policy is the foundational policy document that sets out South Africa's vision for a restructured, competitive, and efficient rail system. Approved by Cabinet in 2022, it marks the most significant rail reform since the formation of Transnet in 1990. Its central thesis: the vertically integrated state monopoly has failed, and the future lies in vertical separation, open access, and independent regulation — with private operators running trains on a publicly owned, independently managed network.

The Problem the Policy Diagnoses

The White Paper opens with a blunt assessment: "South Africa's rail system is in a state of crisis and decline." It identifies several interlocking failures:

Declining freight volumes

General freight volumes fell by over 70% between 1988 and 2022. Rail's share of land freight dropped from ~89% to below 30%. The dominant mode became road — more expensive, more carbon-intensive, and more dangerous.

Chronic underinvestment

Decades of deferred maintenance and capital under-spend left infrastructure in poor condition. Ageing locomotives, deteriorating track, obsolete signalling — the network's reliability collapsed.

Monopoly inefficiency

Transnet's vertical integration — owning infrastructure and operating trains — gave it no competitive pressure. No alternative existed for customers. Service quality, pricing, and investment decisions suffered from the absence of market discipline.

Governance & state capture

The 2010s procurement scandals demonstrated that a state monopoly with weak oversight is vulnerable to systemic corruption. The policy explicitly cites governance failure as a structural problem requiring structural reform, not just personnel changes.

The Three Pillars of Reform

The policy rests on three structural interventions designed to permanently change the incentives governing South Africa's rail system:

1. Vertical Separation

The infrastructure (tracks, signalling, stations) is separated from train operations. A new, independent Rail Infrastructure Manager owns and maintains the network and allocates access to all operators on equal terms. Transnet Freight Rail becomes just one train operator among many — competing for slots, not controlling them.

Modelled on: UK (Network Rail), EU directives, Australian open-access frameworks.

2. Open Access

Any licensed operator — private, public, domestic, international — has the right to apply for train paths on the national rail network. The Infrastructure Manager must grant access on published, non-discriminatory terms. This ends Transnet's de facto monopoly over who runs trains.

First corridor targeted: Durban–Gauteng container line. Over 100 applications received by late 2024.

3. Independent Economic Regulation

A new, arms-length Transport Economic Regulator sets access tariffs, monitors market conduct, resolves disputes, and enforces compliance. This removes pricing and access decisions from political and corporate control and places them with an expert body accountable to Parliament.

Established via the Economic Regulation of Transport Act (2024).

What the Policy Mandates

  • Establish a Rail Infrastructure Manager — a legally and operationally independent entity that owns, maintains, and controls access to the national rail network. Transnet's infrastructure assets are transferred to this entity.
  • Separate Transnet Freight Rail's train operations from infrastructure ownership. TFR becomes a train operating company (TOC) competing with other TOCs for access slots.
  • Create a Transport Economic Regulator with powers to set maximum tariffs for access to rail and port infrastructure, investigate anti-competitive behaviour, and impose remedies.
  • Mandate non-discriminatory access — any qualified operator can apply for slots; decisions must be published with reasons; appeals go to the Regulator, not Transnet management.
  • Establish a Rail Safety Regulator independent of operators, enforcing uniform safety standards across all TOCs.
  • Develop a multi-year Network Statement — a public document listing available capacity, access charges, and technical standards — so operators can plan investments with certainty.
  • Prioritise key freight corridors for early implementation: Durban–Gauteng, Sishen–Saldanha, Hotazel–Ngqura, and the coal line to Richards Bay.

Implementation Roadmap

PhaseTimelineKey Actions
Phase 1 — Legal & Institutional 2022–2024 Pass the Economic Regulation of Transport Act. Establish the Transport Economic Regulator. Begin legal separation of the Rail Infrastructure Manager from Transnet.
Phase 2 — Pilot Open Access 2024–2026 Publish the first Network Statement. Accept and process third-party access applications. First private trains running on the Durban–Gauteng corridor. Concession tenders for priority export corridors.
Phase 3 — Full Separation 2026–2028 Complete transfer of rail infrastructure to the Infrastructure Manager. Full operational independence. Transnet Freight Rail operates as a commercial TOC competing on equal terms.
Phase 4 — Mature Market 2028–2030+ Multiple competing TOCs on the core network. Transparent, regulated access pricing. Rail regains material market share from road. Logistics costs as a percentage of GDP decline.

Why the Rail Policy Matters for the Economy

South Africa's logistics costs are among the highest in the world — approximately 12% of GDP, compared to 8–9% in peer emerging economies. Coal exporters lost an estimated R50+ billion in revenue in 2022–2023 alone because Transnet could not move volumes to port. The Rail Policy's reforms are not ideological — they are an economic necessity. Every percentage point reduction in logistics costs adds billions of rand to GDP and makes South African exports more competitive globally.

The World Bank and National Treasury have both estimated that fixing rail logistics bottlenecks could add 1–2% to annual GDP growth. The Rail Policy is the blueprint for unlocking that growth — by replacing a failing monopoly with a competitive market that attracts private capital, skills, and innovation.

How the Policy and the Act fit together

The National Rail Policy (2022) sets out the what and the why. The Economic Regulation of Transport Act (2024) provides the how — the legal powers, institutional design, and enforcement mechanisms to implement the policy. Together they form a complete reform architecture: policy vision plus statutory teeth.
