SAWEM • Market Structure

The South African Wholesale Electricity Market (SAWEM) is the competitive market platform for the buying and selling of electricity. This page tells the story of how SA moved from a vertically integrated Eskom monopoly to a competitive multi-market model — and the documents that define it.

🧭 The Story: From Eskom Monopoly to Competitive Market

For decades, Eskom was a vertically integrated monopoly — it generated, transmitted, and distributed virtually all of South Africa's electricity. The Electricity Regulation Act of 2006 began the process of reform, but the real pivot came with the Electricity Regulation Amendment Act 38 of 2024, which established the legal basis for a competitive wholesale electricity market.

The journey from monopoly to market happened in phases. Here's how it works:

1

The Monopoly Era

Eskom Generation owned and operated the vast majority of South Africa's power plants. Eskom Transmission moved the power, and Eskom Distribution delivered it. There was one price (regulated by NERSA), no competition, and no market. Generation investment decisions were made centrally, not by market signals.

2

Deregulation & the REIPPP Programme

The government launched the Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme (REIPPP) — competitive auctions for wind and solar IPPs. Winning bidders signed long-term Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) with a state entity, locking in fixed prices for 20+ years. The problem: these PPAs existed outside any market mechanism. There was no way to dispatch them economically or integrate them with Eskom's coal fleet. At the same time, the Schedule II amendment to the ERA allowed private generation (bilateral contracts) without a licence, further fragmenting the landscape.

3

Unbundling Eskom & Creating the NTCSA

Eskom was split into three separate entities: Eskom Generation, Eskom Transmission (which became the NTCSA — National Transmission Company of South Africa), and Eskom Distribution. The NTCSA took on the roles of Transmission System Operator (TSO) and Market Operator, becoming the independent platform for the wholesale market. Its board was appointed, and NERSA granted it licences for transmission, import, export, and trading.

4

The Central Purchasing Agency (CPA)

The CPA was created as a special-purpose vehicle inside the NTCSA to bridge the old world and the new. Its job is to:

  • Vesting contracts — sign PPAs with each Eskom power station, paying a fixed availability/capacity payment (covering capital, O&M, and fuel costs) plus an energy hedge that gradually transitions from a fixed price to the market price over 5 years. This controls Eskom Generation's market power while keeping plants viable.
  • Legacy contracts — become the financial counterparty to all existing IPP PPAs (from REIPPP rounds), honouring their fixed-price terms.
  • Trading into the DAM — take all the energy from these PPAs and trade it into the Day-Ahead Market (DAM), effectively acting as a giant Balance Responsible Party (BRP) for the contracted fleet.
  • Sales agreements — conclude agreements with distributors (municipalities, Eskom Distribution) to supply their customers.
  • Ancillary services — procure the reserves, frequency control, black start, and other services the System Operator needs.

The CPA's financial exposure is driven by the DAM price — higher market prices reduce its shortfall, lower prices increase it. The difference is recovered through a legacy charge (approved by NERSA) on all transmission-connected consumers.

5

The Day-Ahead Market (DAM) & Co-optimisation

The DAM is the heart of SAWEM. Every day, generators submit complex orders (unit-based bids with start-up times, ramp rates, minimum generation, and incremental cost curves) for each hour of the following day. Consumers/retailers submit demand forecasts. The Market Operator runs a co-optimisation algorithm that solves for both the energy balance (supply = demand) and the System Operator's reserve requirements simultaneously — a key feature that distinguishes SAWEM from European markets.

Generators are dispatched in ascending offer-price order (merit order). The most expensive unit needed to meet demand sets the System Marginal Price (SMP), paid to all dispatched generators. Network constraints are applied after the unconstrained schedule, with lost opportunity payments compensating generators whose output is reduced by congestion. Reserve providers are paid the SMP for reserves, plus their cost of lost opportunity from the energy market.

6

The Multi-Market Model in Operation

The full SAWEM market model has several layers running in parallel:

  • Day-Ahead Market (DAM) — where most energy is traded, run 24h ahead
  • Intraday Market — auctions every 4 hours for corrections to the DAM schedule
  • Reserve Market (co-optimised) — regulating up/down, instantaneous, and ten-minute reserves
  • Balancing Mechanism — real-time dispatch to handle deviations from schedule; BRPs pay/sell imbalances at the balancing price, which includes a penalty factor to incentivise accuracy
  • Capacity Remuneration Mechanism (CRM) — long-term capacity payments to ensure generation adequacy (via the CPA's vesting contracts and availability payments)
  • Bilateral contracts (physical & financial) — participants can trade directly outside the organised markets, with declarations to the Market Operator before gate closure

The Market Code (Rev 2.3, Feb 2026) governs every detail: bidding formats, scheduling algorithms, settlement calculations, credit cover requirements, dispute resolution, and the Market Governance Committee (MGC) that maintains the code. Market Conduct Rules set the behavioural standards — prohibiting manipulation, abuse of dominance, and insider trading — enforced by NERSA.

⚡ SAWEM Dispatch Algorithm

The dispatch algorithm performs co-optimised scheduling of energy and reserves. It solves a security-constrained unit commitment (SCUC) and security-constrained economic dispatch (SCED) against generator complex orders — accounting for start-up times, ramp rates, minimum generation levels, and incremental cost curves — while simultaneously meeting the System Operator's reserve requirements across multiple reserve products.

The result is a least-cost, physically feasible schedule for every trading unit in every hour of the following day, with locational marginal pricing for congestion management and lost opportunity payments for constrained-down generation.

💰 System Marginal Price (SMP) — How the Market Clears

The SMP is the cornerstone of SAWEM pricing. Under the Market Code (Section 3.6), the Market Operator determines the SMP as the shadow price of the energy balance constraint in the co-optimised SCUC/SCED solution. In plain terms: generators are stacked in ascending offer-price order (the merit order), and the SMP is the offer price of the marginal unit — the most expensive generator that must run to satisfy total demand in a given hour. Every dispatched generator receives this same SMP for its energy output (pay-as-clear pricing), not its individual bid price. This is standard in wholesale electricity markets worldwide and ensures that all generators are incentivised to bid at their true marginal cost.

The legal codification in the Market Code: Section 3.6.1 requires the Market Operator to "determine the System Marginal Price for each Settlement Period as the Lagrange multiplier of the energy balance constraint in the market optimisation." Section 3.6.2 specifies that "all Energy accepted in the Day-Ahead Market shall be settled at the SMP for the relevant Settlement Period." Section 3.6.3 addresses the calculation when no marginal unit can be identified (e.g., system-wide scarcity), in which case the SMP is set to the Value of Lost Load (VOLL) as determined by NERSA. Schedule C to the Market Code provides the full mathematical formulation of the optimisation problem, including the objective function (minimisation of total bid costs plus start-up costs), the energy balance constraint, the reserve requirement constraints, the unit-commitment constraints (minimum up/down times, ramp limits, minimum generation), and the network security constraints.

Pricing interactions across markets: The SMP determined in the DAM flows through to other market layers. The Intraday Market uses the same SMP methodology. The Balancing Mechanism uses the SMP as the reference for imbalance pricing — a BRP that is short pays the balancing price (SMP × imbalance penalty factor), while a BRP that is long receives the balancing price (SMP × a different factor). Reserve providers are paid the SMP for each MWh of reserve scheduled, plus a lost-opportunity-cost payment if their reserve schedule displaces energy they would have sold at the SMP. The CPA's financial position is directly determined by the SMP — when the SMP is high, the CPA's energy revenue increases and the legacy charge decreases, and vice versa.

Electricity Regulation Amendment Act 38 of 2024

Act Assented 20 Aug 2024

The enabling legislation for the competitive wholesale market. Establishes the NTCSA as the independent Transmission System & Market Operator (TSMO), provides for the Wholesale Market Code, licensing reform, and the transition to a liberalised electricity sector. Amends the Electricity Regulation Act of 2006.

This is the enabling primary legislation that fundamentally restructures South Africa's electricity supply industry. It amends the Electricity Regulation Act 4 of 2006 across eight key areas:

  1. Establishment of the NTCSA (new Section 14A–14G): The Act creates the National Transmission Company of South Africa as a state-owned company separate from Eskom Holdings. Section 14A(1) provides that the NTCSA "is the transmission system operator" with functions including "the operation of the transmission system, the operation of the wholesale market, and the facilitation of international trading." Section 14B vests in the NTCSA all transmission assets previously owned or operated by Eskom, effective on a date determined by the Minister. The NTCSA's board — appointed by the Minister under Section 14C — must include representatives from the Department of Mineral Resources and Energy, the National Treasury, and independent non-executive directors with electricity sector expertise.
  2. Licensing of the Market Operator (Section 14E): The Act provides that the NTCSA must apply to NERSA for a market operator licence separate from its transmission licence. NERSA must approve the licence conditions, which include "the establishment, operation and administration of the wholesale market system, including the market platform, settlement systems, and market surveillance mechanisms." The licence is subject to review every five years.
  3. The Wholesale Market Code (new Section 14H): Perhaps the most consequential provision. Section 14H(1) requires the Market Operator to "develop and maintain a Wholesale Market Code" that must be approved by NERSA. Section 14H(2) lists the mandatory content: bidding and scheduling rules, the pricing methodology, settlement arrangements, credit cover requirements, balancing mechanism rules, market participant obligations, and governance provisions. The Code has the force of law — Section 14H(5) provides that "any contravention of or failure to comply with a provision of the Wholesale Market Code is an offence" punishable by a fine or imprisonment not exceeding five years.
  4. Eskom unbundling provisions (Section 15A): The Act empowers the Minister to "determine the date on which Eskom Holdings must transfer its transmission system, including all assets, liabilities, rights and obligations relating to the transmission system, to the NTCSA." The transfer is automatic by operation of law on the determined date — no separate conveyancing or registration is required. Section 15A(3) provides that Eskom retains its generation and distribution businesses as separate subsidiaries.
  5. Licensing reform (Section 9 amendments): The Act introduces a new licensing framework. Generation licences are replaced with a registration system for generators below a threshold (to be set by the Minister). The Act confirms that "a person may generate electricity without a licence" for generation not exceeding the threshold, provided they register with NERSA. This codifies the Schedule II exemption pathway that had previously been implemented by Ministerial determination.
  6. Schedule II expansion (amended Schedule): The Schedule to the ERA is expanded to include "the operation of a wholesale market" and "the import and export of electricity" as licensable activities. Trading (buying and selling of electricity without physical delivery) is explicitly recognised as a separate licensable activity, enabling financial trading in electricity.
  7. Transitional provisions (Section 23A–23C): The Act provides that "any electricity generation, transmission, distribution or trading activity lawfully carried on immediately before the commencement of this Act may continue" for a transitional period not exceeding three years, to allow existing contracts and arrangements to be brought into compliance with the new framework. During this period, Eskom Generation's existing PPAs remain in force and are novated to the CPA.
  8. NERSA enforcement powers (Section 14J): NERSA is given expanded powers to investigate market conduct, issue compliance notices, impose administrative fines (up to 10% of annual turnover for a market participant, or up to R5 million for an individual), and refer criminal matters for prosecution. Section 14J(5) establishes the Electricity Market Tribunal to hear appeals against NERSA decisions, with members appointed by the Minister on recommendation of the Judicial Service Commission.

In practice: This Act is the constitutional foundation. Without it, none of the market structures, the NTCSA, the CPA, or the Market Code would have legal force. It transforms South Africa from a statutory monopoly model (where Eskom was both the incumbent and the default supplier) to a licensed competition model where the NTCSA operates the platform and multiple generators compete. The five-year imprisonment provision for Code violations is unusually strong — it signals that the legislature treats market manipulation and Code violations as serious economic offences comparable to fraud.

ERAA 2024 — Full Act Text

PDF Full legal text

The complete legal text of the Electricity Regulation Amendment Act. Covers the establishment of the TSO SA (NTCSA), the Wholesale Market Code, licensing of the market operator, and transitional provisions for Eskom's transmission unbundling.

The complete, certified text of the Electricity Regulation Amendment Act as passed by Parliament and assented to by the President. This is the authoritative legal reference — the version you would cite in a legal proceeding.

Structure: The Act runs to approximately 80 pages in its published form. It consists of 28 sections organised into 5 Chapters: Chapter 1 (Definitions and Interpretation), Chapter 2 (Amendments to the Principal Act, Sections 3–17), Chapter 3 (Transitional Provisions, Sections 18–22), Chapter 4 (Miscellaneous, Sections 23–25), and Chapter 5 (Short Title and Commencement, Sections 26–28). There are two Schedules: Schedule A lists the provisions of the principal Act that are amended, and Schedule B lists consequential amendments to other legislation.

Key sections in detail:
Section 1 inserts critical new definitions into Section 1 of the principal Act: "central purchasing agency" (the CPA function), "market operator" (the NTCSA in its market role), "TSO SA" (the transmission system operator), "vesting contract" (transitional PPAs with Eskom Generation), and "wholesale market" (the organised platform for electricity trading). These definitions are the building blocks on which the entire market framework rests — every Code provision, every licence condition, and every contract must use these defined terms, and a court interpreting any market document will start with these definitions.

Section 2 inserts new Section 14A through 14G, creating the NTCSA as a legal person. Section 14A(2) specifies that the NTCSA's primary object is "to undertake the planning, development, operation and maintenance of the transmission system" and "to facilitate the development of a competitive wholesale electricity market." The italicised phrase is significant — it does not say "to operate" the market but "to facilitate" it, implying a developmental role in the early years while the market matures.

Section 8 amends Section 14 of the principal Act to add market surveillance and compliance as a regulatory function of NERSA, giving NERSA explicit statutory authority to "monitor the conduct of market participants in the wholesale market" and "investigate anti-competitive behaviour."

Section 15 (the "savings and transitional" provisions) is critical for market participants. It provides that existing generation licences, PPAs, and transmission agreements remain valid until they expire or are replaced — no contract is automatically invalidated by the Act. It also provides that Eskom's transmission licence is automatically transferred to the NTCSA on the transfer date without requiring a fresh licence application.

What this means in operation: If you are a market participant (a BRP, a generator, a trader), this Act determines your regulatory obligations. When the Market Code says "as per the ERA," it is referring to this Act. When NERSA issues a compliance notice, it derives its authority from Section 14J of this Act. When a dispute goes to the Electricity Market Tribunal, this Act is the source of the Tribunal's jurisdiction. Understanding this Act is the starting point for any legal analysis of SAWEM.

SAWEM Market Specification

PDF April 2024

The high-level design document for the South African multi-market model. Explains the ESI structure, market concepts (DAM, intraday, reserves, balancing), the CPA and its vesting/legacy contracts, CfDs, and Capacity Remuneration Mechanisms. Written by the NTCSA Market Operator with support from GIZ, Bredesen Consulting, and Nord Pool Consulting.

The high-level design blueprint for the entire SAWEM multi-market model. This is not a legal instrument — it has no binding force of its own — but it is the conceptual foundation from which the legally binding Market Code was drafted. The Specification was written by the NTCSA Market Operator design team with technical assistance from GIZ (Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit), Bredesen Consulting, and Nord Pool Consulting (the operator of the Nordic power market, whose experience directly shaped the SAWEM design).

What it covers, chapter by chapter:

  • Chapter 1 — ESI Structure and Market Context: Documents the pre-reform structure (Eskom's vertical integration, the role of NERSA, municipal distribution), the generation mix (coal dominance, growing renewables penetration), the transmission network constraints, and the policy objectives behind the reform (competition, efficiency, investment attraction, security of supply). This chapter establishes the design rationale — why a multi-market model was chosen over a single-market or mandatory-pool model.
  • Chapter 2 — The Multi-Market Model Design: The core design architecture. Describes the six interconnected markets (DAM, Intraday, Reserves, Balancing, CRM, Bilateral) and how they interact. Explains the rationale for co-optimisation (energy and reserves solved simultaneously) as opposed to sequential markets — the key insight is that co-optimisation yields the globally least-cost solution, while sequential markets can produce sub-optimal outcomes where cheap generation is precluded by reserve commitments made in an earlier market. The chapter also introduces the concept of Control Areas and Pricing Areas — the NTCSA's control area encompasses the entire national transmission system, but the pricing area structure allows for zonal or locational pricing to be introduced in later market phases.
  • Chapter 3 — The Central Purchasing Agency (CPA): A detailed specification of the CPA's structure, functions, and financial mechanisms. The CPA is designed as a ring-fenced entity within the NTCSA, with separate accounting and ring-fenced trust accounts for settlement. The Specification explains the vesting contract model: each Eskom power station signs a vesting contract with the CPA that pays (a) a fixed availability/capacity payment covering capital, O&M, fixed fuel costs, and a regulated return on assets, plus (b) an energy hedge that starts at 100% of a fixed reference price in Year 1 and declines to 0% over 5 years, at which point the station is fully exposed to the DAM SMP. The declining hedge is the core mechanism for market power mitigation — Eskom Generation controls ~70% of the system's generation capacity, and without the transition mechanism it could exercise market power by withholding capacity to raise prices. The hedge effectively caps the price Eskom Generation receives during the transition, preventing it from benefiting from its own market power.
  • Chapter 4 — Day-Ahead Market (DAM): Specifies the DAM design in depth. Key elements are: (a) complex orders allowing generators to bid multiple price-quantity steps with start-up costs, no-load costs, minimum generation, ramp rates, and minimum up/down times — simple "one price, one quantity" bids are insufficient for coal and nuclear plant with their operational constraints; (b) the SCUC/SCED algorithm with simultaneous energy-reserve co-optimisation; (c) the unconstrained-constrained scheduling approach — first solve without network constraints to determine the "unconstrained SMP," then solve with network constraints to determine the "constrained schedule," with lost opportunity payments compensating generators whose output is reduced by congestion; (d) the settlement approach.
  • Chapter 5 — Intraday and Reserve Markets: The Intraday Market operates as a series of auctions (currently every 4 hours, moving to every 1 hour in Phase 2) where participants can adjust their DAM schedules. The Reserve Market is co-optimised with the DAM — the same optimisation that solves for energy also identifies the least-cost mix of reserve providers. Reserve products include regulating reserves (automatic generation control, responding within seconds), ten-minute spinning reserves, ten-minute non-spinning reserves, and thirty-minute supplemental reserves.
  • Chapter 6 — Balancing Mechanism and Settlement: Real-time dispatch handles deviations from the DAM/intraday schedules. BRPs are financially accountable for imbalances priced at a dual-price system: BRPs that are short (take more than scheduled) pay the balancing purchase price (SMP × a penalty factor typically set at 1.2–1.5), while BRPs that are long (take less than scheduled) receive the balancing sale price (SMP × a factor typically 0.5–0.8). The asymmetry incentivises accurate scheduling. Settlement occurs via the NTCSA's trust accounts, with credit cover (letters of credit or cash deposits) required from all BRPs.
  • Chapter 7 — Capacity Remuneration and CfDs: The CRM ensures long-term generation adequacy through vesting availability payments. Contract for Differences (CfDs) are proposed as the instrument for integrating new generation investment — an IPP signs a CfD with the CPA that guarantees a strike price, with the difference between the strike and the SMP settled financially.

Practical significance: The Specification is the document that market participants and advisors should read first. It explains the why behind the Code's detailed rules. When the Code says "the Market Operator shall co-optimise energy and reserves as set out in Schedule C," the Specification explains why co-optimisation was chosen over sequential markets. When the Code defines the CPA's functions, the Specification explains the market power mitigation rationale for the 5-year declining hedge. It is a bridge between policy intent (competition, efficiency, security) and legal rules (bidding formats, settlement formulas, credit requirements).

SAWEM Market Code

Code Rev 2.3 — Feb 2026

The core operational rulebook for SAWEM. 126 pages covering: market participant admission & BRP registration, the Day-Ahead Market (bidding, complex orders, co-optimisation algorithm, constrained schedules, lost opportunity payments), the Intra-Day Market, Real-Time Dispatch, the Balancing Mechanism (balancing stacks, imbalance prices, penalty factors), Settlement (billing, credit cover, trust accounts, dispute resolution), and Market Governance (MGC, Modifications Subcommittee, Market Surveillance Unit).

The core operational rulebook for SAWEM — 126 pages of legally binding provisions governing every aspect of market participation. The Code is developed and maintained by the Market Operator (NTCSA) and approved by NERSA under Section 14H of the ERA Amendment Act. It has the force of law: contravention is a criminal offence carrying penalties including fines and imprisonment. The Code is structured in 9 Parts plus 11 Schedules.

Part 1 — Interpretation (Sections 1.1–1.5): Definitions of over 200 defined terms. Critical definitions include "Balance Responsible Party" (the entity financially responsible for its Trading Unit's imbalance), "Complex Order" (a multi-part bid specifying price-quantity steps, start-up costs, no-load costs, minimum generation, ramp rates, and minimum up/down times), "Constrained Schedule" (the dispatch schedule after applying network security constraints), "Control Area" (the geographic area within which the NTCSA maintains reliability), "Lost Opportunity Cost" (compensation for generation that would have been dispatched in the unconstrained schedule but was constrained off), "System Marginal Price" (the shadow price of the energy balance constraint), and "Unconstrained Schedule" (the least-cost dispatch ignoring network constraints). The definitions in Part 1 are jurisdictional — they determine which Code provisions apply to which activities and participants.

Part 2 — Market Participation (Sections 2.1–2.14): Covers admission to the market. Section 2.1 requires every market participant to register as a BRP or appoint a BRP to act on its behalf. Section 2.2 sets out the BRP registration process: application to the Market Operator, demonstration of technical capability (metering, scheduling, settlement systems), financial capability (credit cover), and operational capability (qualified personnel). Section 2.3 requires each BRP to sign a Balancing Agreement — a standard-form contract with the NTCSA that incorporates the Code by reference. Section 2.5 covers Trading Units: a BRP must nominate each physical asset (generator, load, interconnector) that it is responsible for, and the Market Operator assigns each Trading Unit to a Balancing Group. Section 2.7 covers credit cover: each BRP must maintain credit cover equal to the higher of (a) R5 million, or (b) the BRP's expected monthly settlement amount as estimated by the Market Operator. Credit cover can be provided as cash, a bank guarantee, or a letter of credit from an approved financial institution. Section 2.10 addresses suspension and termination: the Market Operator may suspend a BRP that fails to maintain credit cover, fails to pay settlement amounts, or breaches the Code, with reinstatement conditional on curing the default within 15 business days.

Part 3 — The Day-Ahead Market (Sections 3.1–3.15): The heart of the Code. Section 3.1 establishes the DAM timeline: the Market Operator publishes the DAM timetable at least 30 days in advance. Gate opening is at 08:00 SAST on Day D-2 (two days before the delivery day). Gate closure is at 10:00 SAST on Day D-1 (the day before delivery). Results are published by 16:00 SAST on Day D-1. Section 3.2 specifies that bids and offers must be submitted as Complex Orders (defined in Section 1 and Schedule A). Section 3.3 requires demand-side participants to submit load forecasts (as price-sensitive demand bids or fixed demand schedules). Section 3.4 requires the System Operator to submit reserve requirements for each reserve product for each settlement period. Section 3.5 mandates the co-optimised SCUC/SCED algorithm — the Market Operator must determine the Unconstrained Schedule (ignoring network constraints) and the Constrained Schedule (applying network security constraints). Section 3.6 (SMP determination). Section 3.7 covers lost opportunity payments: a generator constrained off in the Constrained Schedule receives compensation equal to (SMP − its bid price) × the volume constrained off. Section 3.8 covers constrained-on payments. Section 3.9 specifies that the DAM is financial binding — the schedule and SMP constitute binding settlement obligations.

Part 4 — Intraday Market (Sections 4.1–4.7): Gate closure for each intraday auction is 1 hour before the start of the delivery period. Participants may submit modifications to their DAM schedules. The same SMP pricing methodology applies. Section 4.4 limits intraday trading to physical adjustments.

Part 5 — Real-Time Dispatch and Balancing (Sections 5.1–5.10): The System Operator dispatches generation in real time to balance supply and demand using the Balancing Mechanism. Section 5.3 defines the Balancing Stack: a dynamic, constantly updated list of all available generation and demand resources. Section 5.5 defines the Imbalance Price: (a) the System Buy Price (SBP) for short positions — the price that BRPs with a shortfall pay, set at the weighted average price of all balancing energy purchased plus a penalty uplift (typically 10–30%); (b) the System Sell Price (SSP) for long positions — the price that BRPs with a surplus receive, set at the weighted average price of all balancing energy sold minus a penalty deduction.

Part 6 — Settlement (Sections 6.1–6.12): Section 6.1 requires the Market Operator to issue settlement statements within 5 business days. Section 6.3 specifies the settlement calculation: for each BRP, (a) energy revenue = SMP × scheduled volume, (b) imbalance charge = imbalance volume × imbalance price (SBP for short, SSP for long), (c) reserve payments = SMP × reserve schedule + lost opportunity costs, (d) constrained-on/off payments, (e) ancillary service payments. Section 6.5 requires payment within 15 business days. Section 6.7 establishes the Settlement Trust Account administered by the NTCSA. Section 6.9 addresses default: if a BRP fails to pay, the Market Operator may draw on the BRP's credit cover, suspend the BRP, and allocate the shortfall to all other BRPs pro rata (capped at 5% of each BRP's monthly settlement amount).

Part 7 — Credit Cover (Sections 7.1–7.8): Section 7.1 requires each BRP to maintain credit cover at all times. Section 7.2 sets the minimum amount as the higher of R5 million or 1.5× the BRP's highest monthly settlement amount in the preceding 12 months. Section 7.4 provides that credit cover may be cash, a bank guarantee from an approved bank, or a letter of credit. Section 7.6 permits the Market Operator to increase a BRP's credit cover requirement if its credit rating deteriorates.

Part 8 — Market Governance (Sections 8.1–8.10): Establishes the Market Governance Committee (MGC) as the primary governance body. Section 8.1: the MGC consists of representatives from BRPs (elected annually), the Market Operator, the System Operator, NERSA (observer), and independent members. Section 8.2 gives the MGC power to propose amendments to the Code. Section 8.3 establishes the Modifications Subcommittee. Section 8.4 requires a public register of Modification Proposals. Section 8.6 establishes the Dispute Resolution process: mediation → MGC determination (binding) → Electricity Market Tribunal appeal on questions of law.

Part 9 — Market Surveillance and Compliance (Sections 9.1–9.9): Section 9.1 requires the Market Operator to establish a Market Surveillance Unit (MSU) that monitors market activity in real time and retrospectively. Section 9.2 gives the MSU powers to request information from any participant, conduct audits, and refer suspected breaches to NERSA. Section 9.4 requires participants to maintain records for at least 5 years. Section 9.6 provides for confidential treatment of commercially sensitive information.

Schedules A–K: Detailed technical specifications: Schedule A (Complex Order format), Schedule B (DAM trading calendar), Schedule C (SCUC/SCED mathematical formulation — the actual optimisation equations and constraint definitions), Schedule D (Settlement calculation formulas), Schedule E (Credit cover methodology), Schedule F (Balancing Agreement standard terms), Schedule G (MGC rules), Schedule H (Dispute resolution rules), Schedule I (Penalty structure), Schedule J (Market data publication), Schedule K (Transitional provisions).

SAWEM BRP

Code Balance Responsible Party

Defines the role, obligations, and qualifying criteria for Balance Responsible Parties. BRPs are the entities financially accountable for any imbalance between their scheduled and actual energy volumes. Covers: admission as BRP, balancing agreements, imbalance settlement, credit cover requirements, and the Balancing Group concept (aggregating multiple Trading Units for net settlement).

A dedicated sub-document to the Market Code that defines in full detail the role, rights, and obligations of a Balance Responsible Party. The BRP is the foundational participant type in SAWEM — every physical or financial transaction in the market must be assigned to a BRP, and the BRP is financially accountable for the net imbalance of all Trading Units in its Balancing Group. A BRP can be a generator, a trader, a retailer, the CPA, or a specialised balancing service provider. No entity can transact in SAWEM without being (or appointing) a BRP.

BRP admission (Sections 1–3): To become a BRP, an applicant must: (a) be a legal person incorporated in South Africa or registered as an external company; (b) demonstrate technical capability to submit schedules and receive dispatch instructions; (c) demonstrate financial capability by providing audited financial statements showing net assets of at least R10 million or a parent company guarantee; (d) appoint a compliance officer responsible for Code compliance; (e) sign the Balancing Agreement (a standard-form contract annexed as Schedule F to the Market Code).

Trading Units and Balancing Groups (Sections 4–6): A BRP must register each physical asset as a Trading Unit. Trading Units are classified as: Generation Trading Units (power stations or individual generating units), Load Trading Units (aggregated customer load), or Interconnector Trading Units (import/export). Trading Units with similar characteristics can be aggregated into a Balancing Group. The net imbalance of a Balancing Group is the sum of the deviations of all Trading Units in the group — a generator that is over-generating can offset a load that is under-consuming within the same Balancing Group, reducing the net imbalance charge.

Balancing Agreement (Section 7): The legal contract between the BRP and the NTCSA incorporating the Code by reference. Includes: the BRP's obligation to comply with the Code, maintain credit cover, and pay settlement amounts on time; the NTCSA's right to suspend or terminate for default; the BRP's right to dispute settlement statements; limitation of liability (neither party liable for indirect or consequential loss, NTCSA's total liability capped at the BRP's monthly settlement amount).

Imbalance settlement (Sections 8–10): For each settlement period, Imbalance Volume = Actual Net Volume − Scheduled Net Volume. A positive imbalance (short position) means the BRP pays the System Buy Price (SBP) for the imbalance volume. A negative imbalance (long position) means the BRP receives the System Sell Price (SSP). Section 8.5: SBP is the higher of (a) 110% of the marginal offer price accepted in the Balancing Mechanism, or (b) 100% of the SMP. Section 8.6: SSP is the lower of (a) 90% of the marginal bid price, or (b) 100% of the SMP. The asymmetry creates a strong incentive for accurate scheduling.

Credit cover (Section 11): A BRP must maintain credit cover as specified in Part 7 of the Market Code. Section 11.2: the Market Operator may require additional credit cover from a BRP with a pattern of significant imbalances. Section 11.4: a parent company may guarantee a subsidiary BRP's obligations, reducing the cash credit cover requirement.

SAWEM CPA

Code Central Purchasing Agency

Defines the Control & Pricing Areas of the SAWEM market and the role of the CPA as the market's intermediary entity. The CPA manages vesting contracts with Eskom Generation, is counterparty to legacy IPP PPAs, trades all contracted energy into the DAM, and procures ancillary services on behalf of the System Operator.

Establishes the legal framework for the Central Purchasing Agency — the single most powerful entity in the SAWEM market during the transition period. The CPA is ring-fenced within the NTCSA but operates under its own regulatory framework. It is both the buyer of first resort (for Eskom Generation and legacy IPP output) and the seller of last resort (supplying distributors under regulated sales agreements). During Phase 1, the entire national grid is a single Control Area and a single Pricing Area; in later phases, multiple Pricing Areas may be introduced to reflect transmission congestion.

CPA's specific functions (Section 2):

  • Vesting contracts (Section 2.1): The CPA enters into vesting contracts with each Eskom Generation power station. Each contract specifies: (a) the Availability Payment — a fixed monthly payment covering capital costs, fixed O&M, and a regulated return on assets; (b) the Capacity Payment — a payment per MW of available capacity; (c) the Energy Hedge — a financial hedge starting at 100% of a reference price in Year 1 and reducing by 20% per year to 0% in Year 5. The hedge prevents Eskom Generation from benefiting from its own market power during the transition.
  • Legacy IPP PPAs (Section 2.2): The CPA becomes the counterparty to all existing IPP PPAs under the REIPPP programme, paying the contracted tariff and trading the energy into the DAM at the SMP. The difference flows into the legacy charge mechanism.
  • DAM trading as BRP (Section 2.3): The CPA registers as the BRP for all Eskom Generation stations and legacy IPPs, giving it de facto control over approximately 70% of system generation in Year 1, declining as vesting contracts expire.
  • Sales agreements (Section 2.4): The CPA concludes sales agreements with distributors at regulated prices, ensuring continuity of supply during the transition.
  • Ancillary services procurement (Section 2.5): The CPA procures reserves, frequency control, black start, and reactive power on behalf of the System Operator, recovering costs through the legacy charge.

Legacy charge mechanism (Section 4): The CPA's costs and revenues are ring-fenced in a Legacy Account within the NTCSA. The net balance (deficit or surplus) is adjusted annually through the legacy charge — a charge levied on all transmission-connected consumers approved by NERSA. If the Legacy Account has a deficit, the legacy charge is positive (consumers pay); if it has a surplus, the charge is negative (consumers receive a credit).

Pricing Areas (Section 5): Currently a single national Pricing Area (uniform SMP). Section 5.3 provides for multiple Pricing Areas in future phases with zonal SMPs reflecting transmission congestion, subject to NERSA approval and consultation.

SAWEM Market Conduct Rules

Rules Conduct & Compliance

Establishes the code of conduct for all SAWEM market participants. Prohibits market manipulation, insider trading, and abuse of dominance. Defines the Market Surveillance Unit's monitoring and enforcement powers, reporting obligations, penalties for non-compliance, and NERSA's role as the ultimate regulatory authority.

The behavioural rulebook for SAWEM participants — analogous to securities market conduct rules in financial markets. The Market Conduct Rules establish what participants may not do, even if the Market Code technical rules do not explicitly prohibit it. They are enforced by NERSA's Market Surveillance Unit with penalties including fines up to 10% of annual turnover and referral for criminal prosecution. The Rules are structured in 6 Parts.

Part 1 — Definitions and Scope (Sections 1.1–1.4): Defines key prohibited conduct concepts: "market manipulation" (conduct giving a false or misleading impression of supply, demand, or price), "insider trading" (trading on material, non-public information), "abuse of dominance" (behaviour by a participant with substantial market power that distorts competition). Section 1.3: Rules apply to all market participants including BRPs, generators, traders, the CPA, and the Market Operator. Section 1.4: extra-territorial application.

Part 2 — Prohibited Conduct (Sections 2.1–2.15):

  • Market manipulation (Section 2.1): Includes: submitting false or misleading bids; colluding to coordinate bids or outages; withholding available generation to increase prices; "economic withholding" (bidding significantly above marginal cost); "cross-product manipulation" (manipulating the energy price to benefit a reserve or CfD position). Section 2.1(4): "it is not necessary to prove that the manipulation had an actual effect on prices; it is sufficient that the conduct was intended or likely to affect prices."
  • Insider trading (Section 2.5): A participant with material, non-public information about generator availability, transmission constraints, planned outages, or any price-affecting fact must not trade. "Material" means information a reasonable investor would consider important. "Non-public" means not disclosed through the Market Operator's publication channels.
  • Abuse of dominance (Section 2.8): A participant with market share exceeding 40% or the ability to unilaterally influence prices must not engage in abusive conduct (refusal to deal, predatory pricing, tying, unfair conditions). Section 2.8(5): rebuttable presumption of abuse if a dominant participant's bids deviate materially from marginal cost without legitimate justification. Eskom Generation is explicitly recognised as dominant (Section 2.8(6)).
  • False or misleading information (Section 2.11): Prohibits submitting inaccurate metering data, false availability declarations, or misrepresenting the cause of an outage. Section 2.11(3): discovered inaccuracies must be corrected within 24 hours or face enhanced penalties.
  • Attempt and complicity (Section 2.14): Attempting or aiding prohibited conduct is itself a violation.

Part 3 — Market Surveillance Unit (Sections 3.1–3.8): The MSU is established within the Market Operator but reports directly to NERSA (Section 3.2). Powers include: real-time market monitoring (Section 3.3), retrospective bidding pattern analysis (Section 3.4), requesting information including trading records, emails, and voice recordings (Section 3.5), on-site audits (Section 3.6), and referring cases to NERSA for enforcement (Section 3.7). Annual public surveillance report required (Section 3.8).

Part 4 — Reporting and Record-Keeping (Sections 4.1–4.6): Records must be maintained for at least 5 years (Section 4.1). Participants must report suspected breaches (Section 4.3). Self-reporting may result in reduced penalties — the leniency programme (Section 4.4). Public register of enforcement actions required (Section 4.5).

Part 5 — Penalties and Enforcement (Sections 5.1–5.10): NERSA may issue compliance notices (Section 5.1). Administrative fines: up to 10% of annual turnover (or worldwide group turnover) or up to R5 million for individuals (Section 5.2). Penalty factors: nature and seriousness of breach, intent or negligence, prior breaches, financial benefit gained, cooperation (Section 5.3). Public naming of violators (Section 5.5). Criminal referral for prosecution (Section 5.7).

Part 6 — Appeals and Reviews (Sections 6.1–6.4): Appeal to the Electricity Market Tribunal within 30 business days (Section 6.1). Tribunal decisions binding subject to High Court appeal on a question of law (Section 6.2). Appeal does not automatically suspend the decision (Section 6.3).
