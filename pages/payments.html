💳 Payments & Open Access

South Africa is opening its national payment system to non-banks — retailers, telecoms and fintechs — so they can hold payment authorisation and settle directly instead of reaching customers only through a sponsoring bank. The first wave lands in 2027.

What just happened

On Friday 16 October 2026, the South African Reserve Bank (SARB) set out the most consequential change to the country's payment system in decades: a new authorisation framework, carried by the National Payment System Bill, that lets non-banks perform payment activities in their own right rather than under a bank's licence or sponsorship.

The change is expected to take effect from mid-2027. In its first phase the framework will allow authorised non-banks to perform seven payment activities on their own account — spanning issuing e-money, acquiring merchants and payment initiation — the activities retailers, mobile operators and fintechs have until now only been able to offer by partnering with a licensed bank.

Reuters reported the opening of the system to retailers, telecoms and fintechs in 2027; the SARB's own briefing the same day confirmed the sequencing, the activities and the parallel overhaul of cash and ATMs.

The principle: regulate the activity, not the institution

The reform moves South Africa from an entity-based model — where regulation follows the type of institution — to an activity-based one, where regulation follows what is actually being done.

Reserve Bank governor Lesetja Kganyago put it plainly when the Bill went out for comment on 1 September 2026: "Similar payment activities should be subject to similar regulatory expectations, whether they are performed by a bank or a fintech. If it walks and quacks like a duck, it is a duck, and it should be treated as one."

That cuts both ways. Widening participation lets non-banks do things that previously required a bank licence — but it does not lower the bar. New participants take on what Kganyago called "the core obligations that are the price of admission to the payment system":

  • governance fit and proper requirements;
  • safety and safeguarding of customer funds;
  • anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing controls;
  • operational risk and resilience obligations;
  • ongoing supervision and reporting.

As Standard Bank's head of interbank and domestic payments, Nthabiseng Mohale, framed it for corporates: "Because you perform the same activity as a bank, whether you're a bank or not, you will be expected to adhere to the same requirements."

The new architecture: PASA out, PayInc and the Reserve Bank in

The governance of the payment system has already been rebuilt underneath the new rules.

  • PASA is gone. On 2 September 2026 the Reserve Bank's recognition of the Payments Association of South Africa as the payment system management body lapsed, ending a 27-year self-regulatory arrangement in which the banks governed their own participation.
  • Functions were split. Card and high-value payments, the committees beneath them, and the licensing, authorisation and registration of payment institutions moved to the Reserve Bank. Low-value payments — electronic transfers, debit orders and instant payments — moved to PayInc, the infrastructure company formerly known as BankservAfrica.
  • PayInc becomes a national payment utility. The Reserve Bank holds 50% of PayInc (the participating banks hold the other half). The intention is to run it as shared, open infrastructure that banks, fintechs and other non-banks can plug into directly, rather than a bank-owned clearing house that outsiders reach only through a sponsor.
  • The PEM programme drives it. The Reserve Bank's Payments Ecosystem Modernisation programme — led by Pradeep Maharaj, with Arif Ismail heading the national payment system department — now sits alongside price stability and financial stability as one of the Bank's top three strategic objectives.

Industry feedback on the draft authorisation framework ran to "multiple hundreds of pages"; the next iteration is expected around the first quarter of 2027, ahead of the Bill's passage.

Why it matters: the money at stake

The reform is aimed squarely at the cost of moving money in South Africa.

  • Merchant card fees of 2–3% are still a sore point for retailers and small merchants — Kganyago noted merchants baulking at card charges in that range.
  • Cash is expensive. Cash still accounts for about 56% of consumer transactions by volume, with roughly R180 billion in circulation and an annual economic cost of about R88.5 billion — some 1.2% of GDP. Consumers earning up to R1 250 a month spend around 5% of their income on cash-related costs, against 1% for those earning R20 000 or more.
  • PayShap is underused relative to ambition. The instant-payment rail handles about 60 million transactions a month; the Reserve Bank's target is 500 million a month by 2029. Officials blame four "Ps" — parity, pricing, promotion and perimeter (where it can be used) — and want transactions below a threshold to be free, with the standardised QR+ code letting even a car guard accept digital payment without fees.
  • Fintech is already large. The Reserve Bank's second fintech scoping study counted around 400 fintechs operating in South Africa.
  • Mobile money shows the size of the prize. MTN Group Fintech's MoMo platform processed 13 billion transactions worth US$330 billion in the six months to end-June 2026 across 14 markets, with 70.8 million active users — but South Africa has no dedicated mobile-money licence, which is precisely the gap the new framework aims to close.

The parallel cash and ATM plan aims to cut the cost of cash — officials say it must fall by at least 30% just for the system to stand still — by pooling cash centres and creating one shared, unbranded ATM network.

Who this changes things for

Incumbent banks

Banks keep their place in the clearing structures and remain the anchor of the system. What they lose is the exclusive right to reach customers directly: the rule-making body they owned and funded has been dissolved, and rule-making authority now sits with the Reserve Bank and with PayInc — which the banks still half-own, a structure critics see as an unresolved conflict of interest.

Retailers

Retail groups have been moving into financial services for years — Pepkor's planned bank, Shoprite's Sixty60 wallet and airtime push, Spar and Pick n Pay's mobile money plays. Direct authorisation would let a retailer acquire merchants and issue e-money itself, capturing margin that currently leaves the store as card interchange.

Telecoms

Mobile operators are the obvious beneficiaries. MTN, Vodacom and Telkom already hold millions of prepaid customers and, in MTN's case, a mobile-money platform across 14 African markets. A local authorisation route — the mobile-money licence South Africa never had — is what opens the door here.

Fintechs and payment providers

Payment initiation, merchant acquiring and e-money issuance are the three activities most directly unlocked. Sponsorship models fade in importance, but the compliance burden — safeguarding, AML, operational risk — follows the activity rather than the licence.

Consumers

The stated aim is lower-cost acceptance and better reach in underserved areas: fewer cash deserts, free low-value ATM transactions, and cheaper digital payments at the kind of merchant that has never justified a card machine.

Other changes riding along

  • Cash and ATMs. The Reserve Bank wants all of the country's roughly 30 600 ATMs white-labelled into one shared, unbranded network, with deposits, withdrawals and balance enquiries at every machine and transactions below a set amount free. About 9 200 ATMs run by independent deployers currently take no deposits. A national cash utility, wholly owned by the Reserve Bank, is targeted for March 2027, with cash centres moving in from mid-2027.
  • Closed-loop schemes. The Reserve Bank does not encourage closed-loop systems but accepts they will exist: they may operate without full authorisation only below R15 million in annual transaction value or one million customers.
  • Third-party payment processors will be limited to two formal beneficiary accounts.
  • Real-time clearing will be sunset as the system standardises on newer rails.
  • ISO 20022 structured addresses. Free-text payment fields give way to structured street, town, city and country fields. Standard Bank kept its 10 October 2026 readiness date; Swift has pushed its industry activation to Q1 2027 because regional readiness was uneven. Missed cut-offs risk delayed or returned payments and extra fees, especially into China, Canada and the UK.
  • Cross-border within the CMA. Balance-of-payments purpose codes across the Common Monetary Area were aligned from August 2026, and a directive requires banks to migrate low-value cross-border payments off the SADC real-time gross settlement system onto TCIB by March 2027.
  • BRICS fast-payment links. Member nations are discussing linkages between their fast payment rails and central bank digital currencies — a longer-horizon thread of the same story.

What could go wrong

  • Whether access actually widens. The open question in the reform is whether the Reserve Bank uses its new authority to admit non-banks at scale — or whether incumbents' half-ownership of PayInc reasserts the old perimeter.
  • Resilience concentration. A payment that once crossed a handful of banks may now start on a phone, run on cloud infrastructure and cross several technology providers in seconds. As Kganyago warned, "a disruption at one technology provider can affect several institutions" and a single cyber incident can undermine confidence far beyond where it begins.
  • Fraud and AML. More participants means more endpoints, more screening obligations and more ways to move illicit funds; activity-based rules only help if supervision keeps pace.
  • Consumer protection in a closed loop. Retailer and telecom wallets that sit below the R15 million / one million customer thresholds avoid full authorisation — a gap that will need watching.
  • Cash desert risk. Pooling cash logistics could cut costs, but if the shared network is not placed where the poorest live, the people most reliant on cash lose access fastest.
  • Legislative timing. Everything after Q1 2027 depends on the National Payment System Bill clearing Parliament intact.
