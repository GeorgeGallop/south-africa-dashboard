History of South African Diamonds

The Diamond Rush (1869-1880s)

The discovery of the 83.5-carat "Star of South Africa" diamond near Hopetown in 1869 triggered one of the most significant economic booms in South African history. The subsequent discovery of diamonds at the Vooruitzicht farm near Kimberley in 1871 led to a massive influx of prospectors and miners. The Kimberley region became the epicenter of global diamond mining, with the famous "Big Hole" eventually becoming one of the world's largest open-pit mines.

Cecil Rhodes & De Beers (1871-1902)

Cecil Rhodes (1853-1902) arrived in South Africa at age 16 and entered the diamond fields at Kimberley in 1871. Starting with a water pump rental business that supplied the mines, Rhodes gradually bought up mining claims with funding from the powerful Rothschild banking family. By 1888, Rhodes and businessman Barney Barnato merged their operations to form De Beers Consolidated Mines with £200,000 capital (approximately £28.5 million in today's money).

De Beers' strategy was revolutionary: rather than simply maximizing production, they controlled supply to maintain high prices. In 1889, Rhodes negotiated a strategic partnership with the London-based Diamond Syndicate, agreeing to regulate output and maintain prices. By 1890, De Beers controlled approximately 90% of the world's diamond supply, establishing a monopoly that would persist for over a century.

During the Second Boer War (1899-1902), Rhodes personally moved to Kimberley to ensure the company's survival during the siege. He mobilized De Beers' resources to manufacture shells, fortifications, and even an armoured train to defend the city. Rhodes died in 1902 at age 48, leaving behind a diamond empire and the Rhodes Scholarship, funded by his estate.

The Oppenheimer Era (1926-1982)

Ernest Oppenheimer, a German immigrant to Britain who had founded the Anglo American mining company, joined De Beers' board in 1926 and became chairman in 1929. Oppenheimer's most famous quote encapsulates De Beers' philosophy: "The only way to increase the value of diamonds is to make them scarce, that is to reduce production."

During Oppenheimer's 28-year tenure (1929-1957), De Beers extended its monopoly globally, controlling 80-85% of the world's rough diamond distribution throughout the 20th century. The company maintained prices even during the Great Depression by purchasing and stockpiling diamonds. Oppenheimer pioneered the use of diamond drills, enabling the Free State Gold Rush of the 1930s.

Harry Oppenheimer succeeded his father (1957-1982) and expanded De Beers to operate in Canada, Australia, Namibia, Zambia, and Tanzania. During his leadership, De Beers accounted for 10% of South Africa's GNP and 30% of the country's exports by 1973.

Marketing Revolution: "A Diamond is Forever" (1947)

Perhaps De Beers' most enduring legacy is not geological but marketing. In 1947, copywriter Frances Gerety coined the slogan "A Diamond is Forever" for N.W. Ayer & Son advertising agency. In 2000, Advertising Age magazine named it the best advertising slogan of the 20th century. This campaign transformed diamonds from a speculative commodity into symbols of love and commitment, fundamentally reshaping consumer demand.

Decline of the Monopoly (2000-Present)

De Beers' dominance began eroding in 2000 following two major pressures: discoveries of diamonds in Canada and Australia (outside De Beers' control), and growing international concern about "conflict diamonds" or "blood diamonds" financing armed conflicts in Africa. In response, De Beers stopped purchasing from outside suppliers and embraced the Kimberley Process Certification Scheme (2003).

By 2011, the Oppenheimer family sold its 40% stake in De Beers to Anglo American for £3.2 billion ($5.1 billion), ending an 80-year family dynasty. De Beers' market share continued declining from 90% in the 1980s to just 29.5% by 2019. The emergence of lab-grown diamonds in recent years poses an existential threat to the natural diamond market, with lab-grown stones now costing approximately 90% less than mined diamonds (compared to only 10% less in 2018).

Notable South African Diamonds
  • Star of South Africa (1869): 83.5 carats; triggered the diamond rush
  • Cullinan Diamond (1905): 3,106 carats rough; largest rough diamond ever discovered at Premier Mine (Pretoria)
