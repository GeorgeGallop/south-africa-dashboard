🎓 Agent-Based Computational Economics

The science of growing economies from the bottom up — and how you will model South Africa's economic evolution as it feeds into the electricity market.

🎭 What Is Agent-Based Computational Economics?
💬 Layman's Introduction

Traditional economics works like a photograph: it takes a snapshot of the economy at a moment in time and assumes it is in equilibrium — supply equals demand, everyone has optimised, nothing is changing. Agent-Based Computational Economics (ACE) works like a movie: it starts with a cast of heterogeneous agents (households, firms, banks, governments), each following plausible behavioural rules, and then presses play. You watch the economy grow and evolve over time — with booms, busts, innovations, inequalities, and structural changes that emerge from the interactions, not from any equilibrium assumption.

If CAS theory is the physics of complex systems, ACE is the engineering — the practical methodology for building computational economies that evolve, adapt, and produce realistic macro patterns from micro behaviours.

Agent-Based Computational Economics was formally defined by Leigh Tesfatsion at Iowa State University in the 1990s. She describes it as “the computational study of economic processes modelled as dynamic systems of interacting agents.” The key word is processes — ACE focuses on how economies evolve over time, not on finding a static equilibrium.

📚 Leigh Tesfatsion, founder of ACE (2006):
“The goal of ACE is to understand how process rather than end state drives economic outcomes. Instead of assuming that agents are perfectly rational and that the economy is in equilibrium, we start with agents who have bounded rationality, follow simple rules, and learn from experience. We then study the evolving economy that results from their interactions.”
📚 How ACE Differs from Traditional Economics
DimensionTraditional EconomicsACE
Core method Solve for equilibrium: find prices where supply = demand. Algebra and calculus. Grow the economy from initial conditions: simulate interactions over time. Computation.
Agents Representative agent (one “typical” consumer, one “typical” firm). Homogeneous. Heterogeneous population with realistic distributions of income, productivity, preferences.
Rationality Perfect rationality: agents have all information and compute the optimal action instantly. Bounded rationality: agents have limited information, use heuristics, learn from feedback.
Time Comparative statics: compare before and after a shock; the path between them is ignored. Dynamics: the path is the whole point. Booms, busts, transitions, and phase changes unfold step by step.
Equilibrium Assumed. The economy always tends toward a unique, stable equilibrium. Not assumed. May emerge, may not. Multiple equilibria, cycles, and chaos are possible outcomes.
Emergence Ignored or treated as “market failure.” Central. Macro patterns like business cycles, inequality, and technological change emerge from micro interactions.
Validation Match a few aggregate statistics (GDP, inflation, unemployment). Match distributions, moments, correlations, and stylized facts — not just averages.
📄 A Brief History of ACE

1990s — The founding: Tesfatsion creates the first ACE research group at Iowa State. Early models focus on artificial stock markets (Santa Fe Institute) and trade networks. The key insight: even simple agents can produce realistic financial time series (volatility clustering, fat tails) that equilibrium models cannot.

2000s — The maturity: ACE models tackle macroeconomics directly. The “Krugman model” of economic geography, Delli Gatti's models of financial fragility, and the “EURACE” project — the first large-scale agent-based macro model of the European economy with ~50 million agents.

2008 — The wake-up call: The global financial crisis was not predicted by any mainstream equilibrium model. ACE models, which naturally produce crises as emergent outcomes, gained credibility. The Bank of England, the IMF, and the European Central Bank began investing in ACE models.

2020s — The integration: ACE is now mainstream. “Agency-based” macro models (like the IMF's “Agent-Based Integrated Assessment Model”) are used alongside DSGE models. Your SAWEM project is part of this tradition — applying ACE methodology to the electricity market embedded in a real economy.

■ How ACE Shows Economic Evolution Over Time
💬 The “Generative” Approach

The goal of ACE is not to predict the future. The goal is to understand how the economy works by growing it from the ground up. The motto, from Joshua Epstein at the Brookings Institution: “If you didn't grow it, you didn't explain it.” This means: you define the agents, their rules, and their environment, press play, and see what happens. If the simulated economy reproduces the patterns you see in the real world (business cycles, income distributions, trade patterns), then you have a candidate explanation for how those patterns arise.

The Standard ACE Workflow
①
Design Agents
Define types (households, firms, banks, govt), their attributes, decision rules, learning algorithms, and interaction topology.
↓
②
Set Initial Conditions
Calibrate agent populations to real-world data (income distributions, firm sizes, productivity, demographics). Initialize the environment (prices, institutions, infrastructure).
↓
③
Simulate Forward
Run the model period by period: agents interact, markets clear (or don't), learning updates, new agents enter, old agents exit. Collect all micro and macro data.
↓
④
Analyse Emergent Patterns
Compare simulated macro and micro patterns to real-world data. Do you see realistic business cycles, inequality, growth, structural change? If not, revise the agent rules.
📊 The Key Types of Economic Evolution You Can Show
1. Structural Transformation

How the sectoral composition of the economy changes over time — agriculture → industry → services. In SAWEM context: the shift from an energy-intensive industrial economy (mining, smelting) to a services-and-knowledge economy changes the load shape, peak demand, and electricity intensity of GDP.

Look for: declining share of industrial electricity consumption as % of total, rising share of commercial and residential.
2. Technological Diffusion

How new technologies spread through the economy over time — the classic S-curve. In SAWEM: rooftop solar adoption, EV adoption, industrial heat pump adoption, smart meter penetration. Each follows a logistic diffusion curve with different saturation levels and speeds.

Look for: S-curves of adoption, heterogeneity in adoption timing by income/sector/region, network effects (my neighbour has solar → I am more likely to adopt).
3. Inequality Dynamics

How the distribution of income, wealth, and access evolves — and how it feeds back into economic activity. In SAWEM: energy affordability is an inequality issue. Low-income households spend a larger share of income on electricity. If SMP rises, inequality worsens, which may trigger political responses (subsidies, tariff restructuring).

Look for: Gini coefficient over time, energy burden (electricity cost as % of income) by decile, fuel poverty indicators.
4. Business Cycles & Crises

Endogenous booms and busts that arise from the interaction of agents — not imposed as external shocks. In SAWEM: investment cycles in generation capacity (boom when prices are high, bust when overcapacity depresses prices). Financial stress cycles at Eskom (debt-driven crises). Load-shedding cycles.

Look for: spectral analysis of GDP, SMP, and investment time series; duration and frequency of recessions/crises; power-law distribution of crisis sizes.
5. Institutional Evolution

How market rules, norms, and regulations emerge and change as the economy evolves. In SAWEM: the transition from a vertically integrated monopoly (Eskom) to a competitive wholesale market (SAWEM) is itself an institutional evolution. Your model can simulate whether the market needs a capacity mechanism and what form it should take.

Look for: emergence of new trading practices, changes in market concentration, regulatory adaptation to new technologies.
6. Spatial & Regional Evolution

How economic activity shifts across regions over time. In SAWEM: the location of new renewable generation (Northern Cape wind, Eastern Cape solar) shifts economic activity away from coal-dependent Mpumalanga. This has profound implications for employment, migration, and infrastructure planning.

Look for: regional GDP shares, employment shifts, migration patterns, congestion rent evolution across transmission corridors.
📊 The “Generative” Test — Epstein's Framework

Joshua Epstein (1999, 2006) formalised the ACE approach with his generative social science framework. The question is not “Can the model fit the data?” but “Can the model generate the data from plausible micro rules?” The test has four steps:

  1. Identify a macro-empirical regularity you want to explain (e.g., the fat-tailed distribution of electricity price spikes, the S-curve of solar adoption, the 10-year investment cycle in generation capacity).
  2. Propose a micro-level mechanism that could generate it (e.g., profit-seeking agents with imperfect information, learning delays, and heterogeneous costs).
  3. Implement the mechanism in an ACE model and run it. Check if the macro regularity emerges.
  4. If it does not, revise the mechanism. If it does, you have a candidate explanation — the macro pattern is generated by the micro rules, not imposed.

The generative test is the standard to which your SAWEM model should be held. It is not enough to say “the model produces SMP data.” The question is: can your model generate the real-world SMP distribution, price spike frequency, and volatility clustering from plausible bidding rules and learning algorithms?

■ What Data to Assess — Observations of an Evolving Economy
💬 The ACE Data Framework

In an ACE model, you have a god's-eye view: you can record every variable for every agent at every time step. The challenge is knowing what to look at. The following framework organises the data into levels, from the micro (individual agents) to the macro (system-level patterns) to the emergent (the patterns that only appear when you study the whole system over time).

📊 Level 1: Aggregate Macroeconomic Time Series

These are the standard macro indicators — what every economist looks at first. In your SAWEM ACE model, these are the outputs of the OPF + macro ABM loop.

GDP & Components:
  • Total output (real GDP) by sector
  • Consumption, investment, government spending
  • Trade balance (exports − imports of electricity-intensive goods)
  • Source in SA: StatsSA GDP releases, SARB Quarterly Bulletin
Energy Economics:
  • Average SMP (annual, quarterly, monthly)
  • Total electricity consumption (TWh/yr)
  • Electricity intensity of GDP (kWh/R)
  • Energy cost as % of GDP
  • Source in SA: Eskom Annual Reports, DMP, StatsSA
Labour & Demographics:
  • Employment by sector (coal mining, RE, manufacturing, services)
  • Unemployment rate (official + expanded)
  • Population growth, urbanisation rate
  • Source in SA: StatsSA Quarterly Labour Force Survey, Census
Fiscal & Financial:
  • Government revenue & expenditure (including Eskom bailouts)
  • Eskom debt, municipal arrears
  • Carbon tax revenue
  • Investment in generation capacity (R/yr by technology)
  • Source in SA: National Treasury Budget, Eskom Annual Report
📊 Level 2: Distributional & Structural Data

This is where ACE diverges from traditional macro. The distribution matters as much as the average. These data reveal the “texture” of the economy that standard models miss.

What to MeasureHow to Measure ItWhy It Matters for SAWEM
Income distribution Gini coefficient, Palma ratio, income shares by decile, full distribution plotted as histogram Determines residential electricity affordability, solar adoption rates by income group, political pressure for tariff reform
Wealth distribution Net worth distribution, asset ownership by type (housing, financial, business) Determines capacity to invest in rooftop solar, EVs, and battery storage without financing
Firm size distribution Number of firms by employee count, revenue, electricity consumption. Power-law tail? Industrial load composition: few large firms (mining, smelting) vs many small firms (services). Affects demand response potential
Regional disparities GDP per capita by province, employment by province, electricity access by province Gauteng (economic centre) vs Mpumalanga (coal) vs Northern Cape (RE) vs Eastern Cape (poor). Transmission needs, equity considerations
Energy access & poverty % of households with electricity, energy burden (% of income spent on electricity), cooking fuel type Basic needs: ~3 million SA households still lack electricity. Energy burden >10% is considered fuel poverty
Labour market structure Formal vs informal employment, skill distribution, unionisation rates, wage distribution Just Transition: coal worker retraining, new RE job creation, geographical mismatch of skills
Sectoral composition Share of GDP and employment by sector, Herfindahl index of economic concentration Determines overall electricity intensity and demand growth trajectory
📊 Level 3: Emergent & Relational Data

These are the patterns that only appear when you track the system over time and study the relationships between variables.

Autocorrelation & Persistence:
  • How long does a shock to SMP persist? (autocorrelation function)
  • Are high-price states “sticky”? (regime-switching tests)
  • Is there long memory in demand growth? (Hurst exponent)
Cross-Correlations & Lags:
  • SMP → IPP investment: what is the lag? (2–4 years?)
  • GDP → electricity demand: what is the elasticity? Is it stable or changing?
  • Carbon tax → emissions: what is the response lag?
Volatility Clustering:
  • Are large SMP changes followed by large SMP changes? (ARCH/GARCH effects)
  • Does volatility increase as the energy transition progresses? (expected: yes, as renewables increase)
  • Is there a relationship between price level and price volatility? (leverage effect)
Tail Behaviour & Extreme Events:
  • Are SMP spikes power-law distributed? Or exponential?
  • What is the return period of a “load-shedding event” (unserved energy > threshold)?
  • Are extreme events becoming more or less frequent over the transition?
📊 Level 4: Counterfactual & Policy Metrics

The ultimate purpose of the ACE model is to answer “what if” questions. These metrics compare different simulation runs:

■ Applying ACE to the South African Economy
💬 Why SA Is a Perfect ACE Case Study

South Africa is not a “typical” economy — and that is exactly why ACE is the right tool. SA has extreme inequality, a dual economy (formal/informal), a dominant state-owned electricity monopoly in transition, high unemployment, and enormous structural change ahead (the Just Energy Transition). These features are precisely what traditional equilibrium models handle poorly and what ACE models handle naturally. You cannot assume a “representative agent” in SA — the differences between a Soweto household, a Sandton executive, a Mpumalanga coal miner, and a Northern Cape farmer are the engine of the system, not noise.

🇧🇪 South Africa's Key ACE-Relevant Features
FeatureDataHow It Enters the ACE Model
Extreme inequality Gini = 0.63 (highest in the world). Top 10% earn 65% of income. 55% of population lives below poverty line. Income distribution determines residential electricity demand, solar adoption rates, and energy burden. Low-income households are price-sensitive but cannot easily invest in alternatives.
Dual economy ~35% unemployment (expanded). ~25% of workers in informal sector. Formal sector is capital-intensive (mining, finance); informal is labour-intensive (retail, services). Formal firms are connected to the grid and pay SMP + surcharges. Informal firms may have no connection or illegal connections. The formal/informal split affects load forecasting and demand response potential.
Eskom dominance & fragility Eskom ~R450bn debt. EAF ~55% (2018–2024). Load shedding in every year since 2008. Unbundling into Gen, Tx, Dx underway. Eskom Corporate is a key agent with its own financial dynamics (debt service, tariff applications, bailout requests). Its health determines the tariff path, which affects all other agents.
Just Energy Transition ~90% of electricity from coal. JET P pledge of $8.5bn. ~120,000 direct jobs in coal value chain in Mpumalanga. RE IPP programme since 2011. The central scenario dimension: how fast does coal retire? Where do new jobs come from? What happens to coal communities? How does transmission expansion affect RE deployment?
Municipal crisis ~R60bn owed by municipalities to Eskom. Many municipalities are bankrupt. Load shedding and non-payment create a death spiral. Municipal agents have financial constraints that affect their ability to pay Eskom, invest in infrastructure, and manage load. Municipal failure feeds back into Eskom's finances.
Rooftop solar boom ~4,000 MW of rooftop solar installed 2020–2025. Driven by load-shedding and falling PV costs. Mostly high-income households and commercial. Technology diffusion S-curve with income-dependent adoption rates. Reduces daytime grid demand (duck curve), erodes Eskom revenue, creates grid stability challenges.
Regional concentration Gauteng ~35% of GDP. Mpumalanga ~50% of coal generation. Northern Cape ~80% of best solar resource. Eastern Cape & KZN have high unemployment and low electrification. Geography matters for transmission, employment impacts of transition, and political economy of energy policy.

📌 What to Research Next

You mentioned you have ideas for this and want to research the framework. Here is a reading path tailored to building an ACE model of the South African economy:

  1. Start here — the ACE methodology: Leigh Tesfation, Agent-Based Computational Economics: A Constructive Approach to Economic Theory (Handbook of Computational Economics, Vol. 2, 2006). This is the foundational chapter — it explains exactly what you are doing.
  2. The generative approach: Joshua Epstein, Generative Social Science: Studies in Agent-Based Computational Modeling (Princeton, 2006). The “if you didn't grow it, you didn't explain it” philosophy in action.
  3. Macro ACE models: Delli Gatti et al., Agent-Based Macroeconomic Modeling (Cambridge, 2018). The state of the art in building whole-economy ACE models with financial sectors.
  4. Energy & the economy: The IMF's Agent-Based Integrated Assessment Model (2023) — an ACE model of climate, energy, and the economy. Directly relevant to what you are building.
  5. South African data: Download the StatsSA Income and Expenditure Survey (IES) and the Quarterly Labour Force Survey (QLFS) microdata. These are the most important datasets for calibrating your household agents.
  6. South African energy economics: CSIR energy research reports, Eskom's System Operator data, the IRP2025, and the National Treasury's Just Energy Transition Implementation Plan.
  7. Firm dynamics: The World Bank Enterprise Survey for South Africa gives you the firm size distribution, productivity, and electricity constraints that should calibrate your firm agents.
