🧻 Municipal Sewage Crisis

The Collapse of Wastewater Treatment — Raw Sewage in Rivers, Failed Green Drops, and the Poisoning of South Africa's Water Systems

South Africa's wastewater treatment system is collapsing in plain sight, and the consequences are flowing into the country's rivers, dams, and oceans. The Green Drop report — the national audit of wastewater treatment — shows that more than 60% of the country's sewage treatment works are in a critical or worse condition, with hundreds of plants discharging raw or partially treated sewage into watercourses every day. Sewage spills have turned stretches of the Vaal River, Jukskei River, and parts of the Umgeni into open sewers. This is not only an environmental catastrophe — it is a public health emergency and a direct threat to South Africa's already-scarce drinking water supply, because the same rivers that carry untreated sewage are often the source of municipal drinking water downstream.

Where It Started — The Origins of the Sewage Crisis

The sewage crisis shares its roots with the water crisis, but it has its own specific history:

1. Post-Apartheid Backlog & Urbanisation

Apartheid built wastewater treatment capacity for the white minority and formally excluded townships from sewerage networks. After 1994, the state connected millions of households to sanitation for the first time — but the treatment works and bulk sewers to process that waste were never built at the same pace. Rapid urbanisation outpaced the expansion of treatment capacity. Plants designed for 10,000 people were receiving the sewage of 60,000. The excess simply bypassed treatment and flowed into rivers.

2. Deferred Maintenance & Operator Loss

Wastewater treatment is a 24/7 biological process — if pumps, aerators, and clarifiers are not maintained, the process fails within days, not years. Municipal budget squeezes in the 2010s cut maintenance first. Qualified wastewater treatment plant operators left the public sector (many emigrating to Australia, the UK, and the Gulf). By the 2020s, plants across the country were being run by staff without licences, with broken aeration blowers, and with chemical dosing systems that had not worked in years. The result was plant after plant discharging what was effectively raw sewage.

3. Corruption & Contract Fraud

Wastewater infrastructure became a prime target for municipal corruption. Bilge contracts, inflated capital projects, and "maintenance" tenders awarded to connected companies that never did the work became endemic. The Auditor-General repeatedly flagged billions of rand in irregular expenditure on sanitation projects. When the money for a new oxidation pond or pump station is stolen (or paid to a company that delivers nothing), the sewage keeps flowing — untreated.

What Made It Worse — The Accelerators

  • The Green Drop audit itself was weakened then ignored: The Green Drop certification programme was effectively suspended between 2014 and 2022, removing the accountability mechanism that had driven improvement. When the audits resumed, the collapse was revealed in full. The absence of auditing for nearly a decade meant the failure developed silently.
  • Population growth & densification without network expansion: New informal settlements and densification of existing townships added huge volumes of sewage to networks that were already at or beyond capacity. Connecting more people to a failing system makes it fail faster — and illegal connections of raw sewage to stormwater drains became a widespread shortcut that bypassed treatment entirely.
  • Power outages (load shedding): Wastewater plants rely on continuous electricity for pumps and aeration. Load shedding — particularly the severe 2022-2023 stages — caused repeated plant shutdowns and sewage spills. Many plants lacked backup generators, and where generators existed, they were often stolen or unmaintained. SEWS (sewage spills) skyrocketed during load shedding: each blackout triggered spills that took days to stop.
  • Climate-driven flooding: The 2022 KwaZulu-Natal floods — intensified by climate change — overwhelmed failing sewage infrastructure, washing raw sewage and debris into rivers and the ocean. Climate events turned a chronic problem into acute crises.
  • The "too big to fail" elephant — Rand Water & the Vaal: The Vaal River system — the industrial heart of South Africa — receives effluent from dozens of municipalities and industrial plants. By 2024-2025, the pollution was so severe that the Department of Water and Sanitation admitted the Vaal was in a state of crisis. Untreated sewage from Emfuleni (the municipality that hosts Vanderbijlpark and Vereeniging) was a major contributor, flowing into the river that supplies drinking water to millions downstream.
The Bloemhof Dam crisis (2025): In one of the most dramatic symbols of the failure, the Vaal River's sewage pollution travelled downstream to Bloemhof Dam, which became choked with an massive algae bloom — effectively a sewage-fed green soup. Municipalities drawing drinking water from Bloemhof had to suspend abstraction. Raw sewage, travelling hundreds of kilometres from upstream municipalities, had turned a major drinking water reservoir into an unusable body of water. The water crisis is not just about the quantity of water; it is about the quality — and sewage is poisoning the little water South Africa has.

Where We Are Now — Rivers Running with Sewage

🌊 The Vaal River

The Vaal — Gauteng's industrial spine and a drinking water source for millions — is now chronically polluted. Multiple sewage plants in Emfuleni, Midvaal, and Sedibeng discharge partially treated or raw sewage. The Department of Water and Sanitation declared the Vaal in crisis in 2025, with the pollution directly causing the Bloemhof Dam algae disaster. The river that was once the pride of the PWV region is now a public health and ecological emergency.

🏞️ The Jukskei & Apies Rivers (Gauteng)

The Jukskei, running through northern Johannesburg, is one of the most polluted urban rivers in the world — a combination of treated and untreated sewage, industrial effluent, and informal settlement runoff. The Apies River in Pretoria (Tshwane) carries similar loads. These rivers flow into the Crocodile River, which feeds the Hartbeespoort Dam — which itself suffers catastrophic, decades-old pollution. The chain of sewage is national: Johannesburg's rivers feed the system that eventually reaches Limpopo and Botswana.

🌍 The Umgeni & eThekwini (KZN)

The 2022 floods exposed and worsened the collapse of eThekwini's sewage infrastructure. The Umgeni River, which supplies Durban's drinking water, has seen repeated sewage spills as treatment works (including the massive Northern Works and the Amanzimtoti works) failed under load and damage. Durban's beaches have periodically been closed due to sewage contamination. The economic impact on tourism and public health is enormous.

🌿 Rivers & Dams Across the Country

The crisis is not confined to the big rivers. In the Free State, Mpumalanga, the Eastern Cape, and the North West, dams routinely show high E. coli counts and algae blooms linked to sewage. Fish kills, unusable bathing waters, and farmers refusing to irrigate with river water have become routine. Green Drop compliance of 13% is a national catastrophe dressed in a statistic: 87% of the country's sewage treatment works are failing in some way.

Who Is Going to Fix It — The Reform Agenda

Sewage is, in some ways, harder to fix than water supply: it is invisible until it spills, biologically unforgiving, and requires skilled 24/7 operators — precisely the capacities municipalities have lost. The reform agenda is therefore even more centralised and interventionist than for water:

The National Water Services Authority & Intervention Powers

The Department of Water and Sanitation's plan to create a National Water Services Authority — modelled on the National Water and Sanitation of South Africa (NWSSA) proposal — would allow national government to take over failing municipal wastewater systems directly. Section 63 of the Water Services Act already allows DWS to "take over" services; the reform agenda is to make this routine rather than exceptional for municipalities in Green Drop/Blue Drop crisis.

Regional Water Utilities (RWUs)

Government policy is moving toward establishing Regional Water Utilities to operate water and sanitation across groups of municipalities — combining the revenue base and technical capacity of several entities into one competent operator. Rand Water's model is the template. If implemented, RWUs would take over the operation of hundreds of failing plants and manage them as professional utilities, not as politicised municipal departments.

National Prosecuting Authority & Asset Forfeiture

Fixing sewage is also a crime-fighting problem: the officials who stole the maintenance budgets and the contractors who took money and delivered nothing must be prosecuted. The NPA's priority on corruption cases — backed by asset forfeiture — is a necessary condition for restoring the integrity of wastewater contracts. Without prosecutions, the same looting will repeat in any new utility structure.

Private Operators & Long-Term Concessions

There is growing (still contested) support for private-sector operation of wastewater works under municipal concessions — a model used successfully in parts of the Western Cape and internationally. Private operators bring qualified staff and enforceable performance contracts. The political objection — "privatisation of public services" — is the main barrier, but the financial and technical reality may force the door open for professional management, whether public, private, or hybrid.

The honest assessment: No single actor will fix the sewage crisis alone. It will require: (1) national intervention and utility takeovers in the worst municipalities, (2) a decade of capital investment (R200+ billion) to rebuild treatment capacity, (3) a professionalisation of operations — whether through regional utilities, private operators, or both, and (4) criminal accountability for the corruption that diverted the money. On the current trajectory, Green Drop compliance will not recover materially before 2030, and rivers like the Vaal will remain polluted for many years after that. The cost of not fixing it is far higher: downstream drinking water contamination, disease, and the irreversible damage of South Africa's water systems.
