How it started

Operation Vulindlela (isiZulu: "open the way") was announced by President Cyril Ramaphosa in his address to a joint sitting of Parliament on 15 October 2020, in the depths of the COVID-19 recession. It was established in October 2020 as a joint initiative of the Presidency and National Treasury.

Crucially, it was not another reform plan. Rather than developing new policy, it was created to implement priority reforms that had already been approved but had stalled — a direct response to years of "policy paralysis" where good reform intentions never made it into execution.

The intervention is coordinated by a dedicated Operation Vulindlela Unit working from the centre of government (the Presidency's Project Management Office, with the Treasury's support). The unit monitors progress, identifies blockages, and actively helps departments resolve delays — a delivery-driven model rather than a paper exercise.

  • Why it was needed: South Africa's economy grew by only ~1% per year on average for a decade before 2020, with unemployment persistently above 30% and collapsing network industries (notably Eskom and Transnet) constraining the whole economy.
  • Objective: Reduce input costs, lower barriers to entry, increase competition, and create new opportunities for investment.
  • Name: "Vulindlela" means "open the way" — signalling the removal of blockages that have trapped the economy in low growth.
How it works

Operation Vulindlela is a government-wide delivery mechanism. A small team at the centre of government tracks 19 priority structural reforms, convenes the relevant departments, and escalates blockages to the highest political level (Cabinet and the President) when reforms stall.

Each focus area is driven by sector-specific structures — task teams, crisis committees and implementing agencies — that report into the Operation Vulindlela Unit. Its focus areas are the network industries — the bedrock of economic growth and essential to a globally competitive economy — plus the visa regime:

Focus area Why it matters Lead structure(s)
⚡ Electricity Ending load shedding, breaking Eskom's monopoly, enabling private generation, and moving to a competitive electricity market. Eskom Restructuring Task Team (ERTT), National Energy Crisis Committee (NECOM)
🚛 Freight Logistics Reversing the collapse of rail and ports; opening the rail network and terminals to private participation. National Logistics Crisis Committee (NLCC)
💧 Water Fixing water-use licensing and water services so businesses and farms can get water approvals quickly. National Water Crisis Committee (WATERCOM), Water Partnerships Office
📶 Digital Communications Auctioning high-demand spectrum, completing the digital migration, and cutting mobile data costs. ICASA, State IT Agency, SITA digital public infrastructure programmes
🛂 Visa Regime Attracting scarce skills and growing tourism through eVisa expansion and visa waivers. Department of Home Affairs, Trusted Employer Scheme
Phase 1 (2020–2025): What has been achieved

Phase 1 concentrated on energy, water, freight logistics, telecommunications and the visa system. The headline results:

Sector Achievement
Electricity The licensing threshold for private electricity generation was raised and then removed entirely. The confirmed renewable energy investment pipeline grew from ~4,000 MW (March 2022) to more than 36,000 MW — representing well over R220 billion in new private investment. The ERTT drove the split of Eskom into generation, transmission and distribution entities to enable a competitive electricity market.
Freight Logistics The Freight Logistics Roadmap was adopted (overseen by the NLCC); private sector participation was introduced in container terminals, and open access to the freight rail network was legislated. Eleven private train operating companies were brought in to run trains on Transnet's network.
Water The water-use licence backlog was cleared. Licence processing times fell from ~300 days to 90 days, with 70%+ of licences now processed within 90 days. The new online Water Use Licence Application and Authorisation System is expected to unlock R43 billion to GDP per year over five years.
Digital Communications Ended a decade-plus delay in auctioning high-demand spectrum; completed the switch-off of analogue signal for frequencies above 694 MHz. Mobile data costs declined.
Visa Regime More than 300,000 outstanding visa applications were cleared; the eVisa system was expanded from 14 to 34 countries; visa waivers cover 135 countries with negotiations underway to add 12 more.
Phase 2 (May 2025 onwards): What is happening now

President Ramaphosa announced the second phase of Operation Vulindlela in May 2025. It continues the flagship reforms while expanding into new areas:

  • Local government — improving municipal governance and financial viability.
  • Water services — moving beyond licensing to the delivery of water itself.
  • Metropolitan rail — addressing commuter rail through the wider rail reform agenda.
  • Spatial inequality and housing — tackling apartheid spatial legacy (spatial integration).
  • Digital transformation of government — modernising the state's own systems.

Current progress (as per the 2025/26 Q4 report from the Presidency and Treasury):

Work stream Status
Electricity 60% on track, 20% delayed, remainder facing material obstacles — distribution restructuring across 167 licensed distributors is the hardest part.
Freight logistics 60% on track, 40% delayed — institutional and operational constraints at Transnet continue to limit the pace of reform.
Water 100% on schedule — WATERCOM established, National Water Action Plan rolled out.
Digital infrastructure 80% on track — digital public infrastructure (MyMzansi, MzansiXchange) accelerating.
Local government 75% on track — Metro Trading Services Reform Programme, White Paper on Local Government.
Spatial integration & housing 60% on track, 40% delayed or facing roadblocks.
Visa & immigration Between completed and on track — Trusted Employer Scheme Phase II launched, ETA expansion.
Challenges and criticisms
  • Growth is still slow: Six years on, economic growth remains subdued at around 1% annually, with unemployment still above 30%.
  • Transnet is the weak point: Analysts (e.g. Oxford Economics Africa) say the electricity reforms contrast with much slower progress in logistics, where institutional and operational constraints at Transnet continue to limit the pace and scale of reform.
  • Distribution is hard: Overhauling electricity distribution into financially and operationally sustainable entities involves 167 licensed distributors and significant institutional change.
  • "Work-in-progress, not a turning point": Analysts credit Vulindlela with unlocking reform pathways and improving the policy framework, but describe it as a work-in-progress story rather than a decisive turning point.
What is projected for the future

If the reform agenda is sustained, the payoffs are substantial:

  • Electricity: The 36,000 MW+ private generation pipeline, once operational, is expected to end load shedding structurally, lower energy costs, and unlock industrial expansion. Foundations are being laid for a competitive electricity market with a competitive transmission company.
  • Water: The online licensing system is projected to add R43 billion to GDP per year for the next five years.
  • Freight logistics: Private participation in rail and ports is expected to lift rail volumes, take trucks off the roads, and improve the efficiency of South Africa's export corridors.
  • Investment and confidence: The Presidency reports "higher levels of investment and confidence" in the economic trajectory, with foundations being laid for private sector participation in logistics and water.
  • South African Reserve Bank modelling (historically cited for the reform agenda) has estimated that structural reforms could raise GDP growth by around 1 percentage point per year, adding roughly R400 billion+ to GDP over a decade if fully implemented.

The key risk: implementation. The 2026 SA-TIED independent review ("Opening the road, driving change") and analysts agree that the programme's success ultimately depends on execution discipline across departments, political commitment through election cycles, and the pace at which state-owned companies (especially Transnet) can be turned around.

Timeline at a glance
  • 15 Oct 2020 — President Ramaphosa announces Operation Vulindlela to a joint sitting of Parliament.
  • 2021 — Operation Vulindlela Unit established; 19 priority reforms identified across energy, water, freight, telecoms and visas.
  • 2022 — Licensing threshold for private generation raised (100 MW), then removed entirely in subsequent months.
  • 2023 — National Logistics Crisis Committee (NLCC) established (June); Freight Logistics Roadmap adopted.
  • 2023–24 — Spectrum auction concluded; water licence backlog cleared; 300,000+ visa backlog cleared; rail open access legislated; National Water Crisis Committee (WATERCOM) established.
  • May 2025 — Phase 2 announced: adds local government, water services, metropolitan rail, spatial inequality, and digital government.
  • 2026 — Q1 2026 report: ERTT Phase I completed (independent transmission entity endorsed); WATERCOM operationalised; Trusted Employer Scheme Phase II launched; 11 Rail Access Agreements concluded; independent SA-TIED review published.
