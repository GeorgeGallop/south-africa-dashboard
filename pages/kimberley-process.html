💎 The Kimberley Process

South Africa's Global Legacy — The Certification Scheme That Set Out to Break the Link Between Diamonds and War

The Kimberley Process Certification Scheme (KPCS) is the international mechanism created to prevent "conflict diamonds" — rough diamonds used by rebel movements to finance wars against legitimate governments — from entering the mainstream diamond trade. Launched in 2003 and named after the South African city where it was negotiated, the scheme requires participating countries to certify that their rough diamond exports are conflict-free. It was South Africa's response to a moral catastrophe in Africa — and it turned the country's most famous city into the standard-bearer for ethical diamonds. More than 80 participating governments, covering roughly 99% of global rough diamond production, now operate under its rules.

Part 1: The Problem — Conflict Diamonds & the Wars They Fueled

In the 1990s, the world woke up to a brutal reality: diamonds were funding some of Africa's deadliest wars. Rough diamonds are small, extremely valuable, and easy to smuggle — ideal currency for insurgencies that had no other way to buy weapons. The conflicts this financed were not minor skirmishes; they were among the most destructive wars in modern African history.

Angola (1992-2002) — UNITA

The rebel movement UNITA (National Union for the Total Independence of Angola) controlled the diamond-rich Cuango Valley and funded its 27-year insurgency by smuggling rough diamonds worth an estimated US$3-4 billion between 1992 and 1998. The UN imposed diamond sanctions on UNITA in 1998, but enforcement was weak. UNITA's diamonds bought weapons that killed hundreds of thousands of Angolans.

Sierra Leone (1991-2002) — RUF

The Revolutionary United Front (RUF) financed its brutal campaign — famous for amputating civilians' hands — largely through diamonds from the eastern Kono district. RUF diamonds, often traded through Liberia and smuggled to Antwerp, were worth an estimated US$25-125 million per year at the peak. The Sierra Leonean conflict killed over 50,000 people and displaced more than 2 million.

Liberia — Charles Taylor's Diamond-for-Guns Trade

Liberian warlord-turned-president Charles Taylor used diamonds (many smuggled from Sierra Leone) to buy weapons from Eastern Europe — the infamous "blood diamond" trade documented in the 2000 UN Fowler Report. Taylor's trade was a linchpin of the entire West African conflict system, funding both his own forces and the RUF. He was later convicted by the Special Court for Sierra Leone for, among other things, arming rebels in exchange for diamonds.

DRC — The Great African War

The Democratic Republic of Congo's catastrophic 1998-2003 conflict — sometimes called "Africa's World War," which killed an estimated 5+ million people — was partially sustained by the exploitation of Congolese mineral wealth, including diamonds, by a web of armed groups and foreign armies.

The turning point: The release of the film Blood Diamond (2006) and, more importantly, the work of NGOs Global Witness and Partnership Africa Canada in the late 1990s, forced the industry and governments to confront the trade. Global Witness's 1998 report "A Rough Trade" and Partnership Africa Canada's 2000 report "The Heart of the Matter" documented how conflict diamonds were flowing into the legitimate market via Antwerp, London, and Tel Aviv. At the peak, conflict diamonds were estimated at 4% of world diamond production — enough to destabilise entire regions.

Part 2: Why Kimberley? — The South African Response

South Africa's role in creating the Kimberley Process was pivotal and deeply symbolic. As the birthplace of the industrial diamond industry — and home of De Beers, the very company that had built the modern diamond trade — South Africa had both the moral authority and the economic stake to lead.

1. Security Council Sanctions & the Un Resolution

In 1998-2000, the UN Security Council imposed sanctions on UNITA (Angola) and on Sierra Leone's rough diamond exports, and the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 55/56 (December 2000) calling for a global certification scheme. South Africa, a non-permanent Security Council member at the time, helped drive the process forward.

2. The Kimberley Negotiations (May 2000 - November 2002)

The first ministerial meeting on conflict diamonds was hosted by South Africa in Kimberley, May 2000. The choice of venue was deliberate: the city's history — the site of the 1871 diamond rush and the origin of the modern diamond industry — was a reminder that diamonds built South Africa, and that the trade could be made to serve rather than destroy. Negotiations continued in Brussels, London, Luanda, and Gaborone, culminating in the Interlaken Declaration and the formal adoption of the KPCS in November 2002.

3. A Tripartite Model — Government, Industry, Civil Society

The KP's most innovative feature was structural: it is a tripartite arrangement. Governments (80+), the diamond industry (via the World Diamond Council), and civil society (Global Witness, Partnership Africa Canada, and others) all sit at the table as equal decision-makers. This was unprecedented in international commodity regulation and gave the scheme its credibility — NGOs and the industry effectively policed each other.

Part 3: How the Kimberley Process Works

The KPCS operates on a simple but powerful principle: no certificate, no trade. Every export of rough diamonds from a participating country must be accompanied by a tamper-proof Kimberley Process Certificate, and only shipments with valid certificates may be imported by other participants.

1

Mining

Rough diamonds are mined by licensed producers. Countries must ensure no rebel-mined diamonds enter the legitimate chain by controlling their own mining areas.

2

Export

Each export is sealed in a tamper-proof container with a KP Certificate issued by the exporting state's designated authority, recording carat weight, value, and origin.

3

Import

The importing country must verify the certificate, match it to the shipment, and issue a reciprocal certificate confirming import. Shipments without certificates are refused.

4

Trading & Cutting

Once certified, stones enter the legitimate cutting and trading centres (Antwerp, Mumbai, Tel Aviv, Dubai). The system relies on chain-of-custody documentation from mine to market.

Scope and limits of the scheme:
  • Covers: Rough diamonds only — the validity of the certificate ends once the stone is cut and polished.
  • Does not cover: Polished diamonds, gemstones other than diamonds, or artisanal mining within a country's own borders unless linked to rebel financing.
  • Enforcement: Participants meet annually in plenary; the Chair rotates. Problems are addressed through peer review, "review missions," and ultimately suspension of non-compliant members.

Part 4: The Kimberley Process in Action — Successes and Failures

Since 2003, the KP has transformed the rough diamond trade. Conflict diamonds fell from ~4% of global trade to well under 1% — a genuine humanitarian achievement. But the scheme has also faced serious tests and criticism:

✅ The Successes
  • End of the Sierra Leone/RUF trade: The RUF's diamond income collapsed after the KP and UN sanctions; the war ended in 2002.
  • Angola: UNITA lost its diamond-financing ability, hastening the end of its 27-year insurgency.
  • Liberia: Was suspended from the KP in 2003, re-admitted in 2007 only after Charles Taylor's departure and reforms.
  • Systemic change: 80+ countries now operate certified, audited controls. Almost all global production flows through the system.
  • Illicit flows reduced: Industry and NGO estimates agree the share of conflict diamonds is now a fraction of 1%.
⚠️ The Failures & Criticisms
  • Zimbabwe's Marange field (2006-2011): Military seizure of alluvial diamond fields, killings of miners by security forces, and exports that bypassed KP rules. Civil society called the KP's weak response a "complete failure." A compromise eventually allowed controlled exports — criticised by NGOs as capitulation.
  • Venezuela (2016): Suspended after failing to document the origin of its diamonds; the KP could not prevent smuggling-linked corruption there.
  • Central African Republic (2013): Exports suspended after the Seleka rebellion; the KP's management of the crisis was slow and incomplete, with artisanal diamonds still leaking out.
  • Narrow definition of "conflict": The KP defines conflict diamonds only as those used by rebel movements against "legitimate governments." It does not cover state-perpetrated abuses (e.g., Zimbabwe), human rights abuses by governments, or diamonds from areas where governments themselves commit atrocities (e.g., CAR after 2013).
  • No teeth: Critics argue the KP relies on voluntary compliance and has no independent enforcement or inspection capacity; the Russia veto dynamic (see below) has further paralysed it.
The modern existential challenge: Since 2022, the Kimberley Process has faced a gridlock crisis over Russia. Russia — the world's largest rough diamond producer (through Alrosa) — has blocked any expansion of the KP's mandate, and Western participants, under sanctions, have pushed to exclude Russian diamonds or broaden the definition of "conflict diamond." This standoff has frozen reform: the G7's separate "Russian diamond ban" (effective 2024) effectively bypasses the KP to stop Russian stones, which many view as a death knell for the KP's relevance as the global standard. Meanwhile, lab-grown diamonds (now ~20% of the market by value, and far cheaper) challenge the entire premise of the natural diamond trade the KP was designed to police.

Part 5: South Africa's Role and Interest

South Africa is not merely the namesake of the Kimberley Process — it is a founding member, an active participant, and a major beneficiary. South Africa's relationship with the KP is multi-layered:

  • Founding host and first chair: South Africa hosted the inaugural 2000 Kimberley meeting and has chaired and co-chaired the plenary over the years, using its position as a legitimate, well-governed producer to set the agenda.
  • Producer and exporter: South Africa produces roughly US$1-2 billion in rough diamonds annually (Cullinan, Venetia, Finsch, Koffiefontein, and marine operations). Its exports require KP certificates issued by the South African Diamond and Precious Metals Regulator (SADPMR), which enforces the scheme domestically.
  • The moral link to the industry's history: The KP's legitimacy rests on the idea that diamonds can be a force for good. South Africa — the country where De Beers invented the modern diamond economy — symbolically owns that narrative, and its own history (from the rush to the monopoly to democracy) is the counter-example to the blood diamond story.
  • Leadership in reform attempts: South Africa has supported efforts to strengthen the KP — including broadening the definition of conflict diamonds, improving artisanal mining governance, and transparency measures — though it has had to balance these against opposition from Russia and other large producers.
  • Economic stake: South Africa's diamond industry contributes directly and indirectly to the economy through mining, cutting, and the broader luxury supply chain. A credible, trusted certification system is in South Africa's direct commercial interest — it protects the premium that "clean" diamonds command.

Part 6: The Future — Who Fixes the Kimberley Process?

The KP is at a crossroads. Its original mission — stopping rebel-financed wars — has been substantially achieved. But its future relevance depends on whether it can adapt. The key actors and the realistic paths:

1. The G7 & the Russian Diamond Ban

The G7's 2024-2025 "Russian diamond ban" — with traceability requirements using blockchain (the "Chorus" and "Tracr" systems) — is effectively creating a parallel, stricter certification regime outside the KP. If the G7 persists, the world may end up with two systems: a strict Western one and a looser KP for everyone else. The realistic future is that the G7/traceability systems, not the KP, will set the authenticity standard going forward — which would effectively bypass and hollow out the KP.

2. Civil Society and the NGOs

Global Witness, Partnership Africa Canada (now IMPACT), and others have kept the pressure on. Some of the most prominent NGOs have concluded the KP cannot reform itself and have reduced their engagement — a damning signal. IMPACT and others continue to push for an expanded mandate (human rights, environmental, and broader "conflict" definitions). Whether the NGOs stay or leave will determine whether the KP retains any independent credibility.

3. The Diamond Industry & Lab-Grown Disruption

De Beers, Alrosa, and the World Diamond Council face an existential threat from lab-grown diamonds. Natural diamond producers increasingly market "natural, certified, ethical" stones as a premium product — which makes the credibility of the certification system (KP or its successor) more commercially important than ever. Industry pressure for a credible, enforceable, traceable system is therefore likely to grow, even if it means abandoning the KP brand.

4. South Africa's Leadership Opportunity

South Africa is uniquely placed to broker a new consensus: it is a trusted democratic producer, a founder of the KP, and an emerging voice among African producers. If the KP is to be reformed rather than replaced, South Africa and its fellow SADC producers (Botswana, Namibia) are the most credible convenors. A "Kimberley Process 2.0" — modernised, with blockchain traceability, a broadened conflict definition, and real enforcement — remains possible if a coalition of African producers, the G7, and the industry can agree. Whether that happens is the open question for the next five years.

The honest assessment: The Kimberley Process did what it was created to do — it largely ended the use of diamonds to finance rebel wars, a genuine humanitarian achievement that saved countless lives in West and Central Africa. But it was designed for the conflicts of the 1990s, and it has struggled to evolve. Its future now depends on whether it can be modernised fast enough to stay ahead of the G7 traceability systems, the lab-grown revolution, and the credibility crisis left by Zimbabwe, CAR, Venezuela, and the Russia standoff. Either the process is reformed from within — most likely led by South Africa and the African producers — or it will be quietly superseded by stricter private and G7-backed certification systems, and the Kimberley name will survive only as a historical footnote in the city where the modern diamond trade began.
