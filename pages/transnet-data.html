How the Company Makes Money, What Went Wrong, and Where It Stands Now — With Charts

Transnet is South Africa's freight logistics backbone — but it is also a company in crisis. This page tells that story with data and charts: how it earns revenue (rail tariffs, port terminal handling, port "landlord" fees, pipeline fees), what went wrong (a collapse in volumes driven by state capture, underinvestment, cable theft and poor maintenance), and where it stands now (revenue up but still loss-making; volumes recovering off a very low base; a historic reform programme underway).

How Transnet Makes Money — The Business Model

Transnet is a vertically-integrated state monopoly with five revenue-generating divisions. It earns money by charging for access to and use of national freight infrastructure.

Largest Stream
🚂 Rail tariffs (TFR/TRIM)

The biggest revenue source. Charges mining houses and manufacturers per tonne-km to move bulk commodities — coal, iron ore, manganese, chrome — plus containers and general freight. Rail pricing is regulated (was self-set; now set via the TRIM Network Statement and, soon, the Transport Economic Regulator).

Landlord Fees
🏗️ Port authority fees (TNPA)

TNPA is the "landlord" of the 8 commercial ports. It charges ship dues, port tariffs, berth and quay lease fees for the use of channels, breakwaters, berths and harbour infrastructure — regardless of who operates the terminals.

Handling Fees
📦 Terminal handling (TPT)

Transnet Port Terminals charges cargo-handling fees for loading/unloading containers, bulk and break-bulk at the terminals. This is the monopoly being opened to private operators (ICTSI at Durban, etc.).

Pipeline Tariffs
🛢️ Pipeline transport

Transnet Pipelines (~3,000 km) moves crude oil, refined products and gas from Durban inland. It charges regulated pipeline tariffs — critical to national energy security.

Revenue Mix — How the R78 billion is earned (FY2024/25, approximate split)

Transnet's revenue is dominated by rail freight, with ports and terminals the next largest. The split reflects a company whose fortunes are tied to bulk commodity exports — which is why the collapse in mining volumes hit it so hard.

What Went Wrong — The Volume Collapse

Transnet's business model depends on moving bulk commodities at scale. When volumes collapsed, so did the foundation of the business.

Rail Freight Volumes (Mt per annum) — the collapse & recovery

Rail volumes fell from the 226 Mt peak (2017/18) to under 150 Mt — a third of the business gone — while market demand kept growing to ~280 Mt. Trucks absorbed the gap. The dotted line shows the 2030 target of 250 Mt.

Richards Bay Coal Exports (Mt) — the export engine stalled

Coal is South Africa's highest-value bulk export. Throughput at Richards Bay Coal Terminal collapsed from ~76 Mt (pre-2018) to ~47 Mt (2023) before recovering to 52.08 Mt in 2024. Each lost million tonnes is hundreds of millions in lost export revenue.

1. State capture (2010s)

The Zondo Commission documented how the Gupta-Zuma era looted Transnet — the ~R50bn locomotive procurement scandal (1,064 locomotives, ~R16bn overpriced) being the centrepiece. Procurement was rigged, competent engineers sidelined, and the board captured.

2. Underinvestment & maintenance backlog

Decades of deferred maintenance and capital under-spend left the network decaying — ageing locomotives, poor track, obsolete signalling. Private operators cannot run fast/heavy trains on track that isn't maintained.

3. Cable theft & vandalism

Organised cable theft and vandalism crippled lines, rendering corridors inoperable. Security is a precondition for any reliable operation — public or private.

4. COVID & locomotive availability

COVID-19 exacerbated pre-existing problems, and chronic locomotive availability failures meant trains weren't running even where track was sound. The result: hundreds of billions in lost mining export revenue and the shift of freight to roads.

The Balance Sheet Strain — Debt & Guarantees

Debt trajectory (R billion) + state guarantees
What this means

Debt ballooned past R130 billion, forcing repeated government guarantees — R51 billion in 2025 alone. Debt service is a major drag on a company already unable to fund its own maintenance. The balance sheet, not the network, became Transnet's most urgent constraint.

Where They Are Now — The Reform Story

The crisis forced a structural response: not privatisation, but breaking up the monopoly and opening access.

The structural reform underway
  • Vertical separation — TRIM (the Rail Infrastructure Manager) now owns/manages the network, separate from train operations. 11 private train operating companies signed Rail Access Agreements in May 2026.
  • Open access & third-party operators — rail and ports are being opened to competitive private operators; ICTSI is already partnering on the Durban Container Terminal.
  • Independent regulation — the Economic Regulation of Transport Act (2024) creates the Transport Economic Regulator to set access tariffs and enforce non-discriminatory access, taking pricing out of Transnet's hands.
  • Target — rail volumes recovering toward 250 Mt by 2030, and terminal operations opened to private participation under the landlord port model (see Terminal Operators).
The honest picture

Transnet is simultaneously generating more revenue and losing more money, carrying an unsustainable R130bn+ debt load, and moving a third less freight than at its peak — while economy-wide demand has grown. The recovery is real but off a very low base. The structural reform (separation, open access, independent regulation, private terminal operators) is the answer to a problem that financial rescue alone cannot fix. Whether the 250 Mt target is hit by 2030 depends on operators executing and the network being maintained and funded — but the governance architecture is now in place.
