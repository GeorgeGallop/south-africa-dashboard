🔑 The key to "working at night": CSP stores heat, not electricity

A normal solar panel (PV) makes electricity directly from sunlight, so when the sun goes down the power stops. A CSP plant instead uses mirrors to make heat, and that heat can be banked in molten salt — a liquid "thermal battery" kept in insulated tanks.

During the day the plant produces electricity and charges the salt tanks. In the evening, when the mirrors go dark, the plant simply switches to drawing stored heat out of the tanks and keeps the turbine spinning — for up to 12 hours at Redstone, meaning it can run through the whole night and into the next morning.

⚙️ How a CSP plant works (with storage)

☀️ Day cycle — collect heat, make power, charge the tank
  1. Mirrors (parabolic troughs or heliostat field + tower) track the sun and concentrate direct sunlight onto a receiver pipe or tower-top receiver.
  2. The concentrated light heats a heat-transfer fluid — synthetic oil (~390°C) in trough plants, or molten salt directly (~550°C) in tower plants like Redstone.
  3. Some hot fluid goes to a heat exchanger that boils water into steam, spinning a steam turbine → electricity.
  4. The remaining hot fluid is used to melt salt; the hot salt (~550°C) is pumped up into the hot salt tank — this is the plant "charging" its battery.
🌙 Night cycle — discharge the battery
  1. Sun sets; the mirror field stops collecting. No more solar heat is coming in.
  2. Hot molten salt is drawn from the hot tank and pumped through a heat exchanger.
  3. The salt's stored heat boils water → steam → the turbine keeps running exactly as during the day.
  4. Cooled salt flows back to the cold tank (~290°C), ready to be re-melted by tomorrow's sun.
  5. Generation continues until the hot tank runs out — hours of storage versus minutes of battery for a typical PV plant.
The two-tank molten-salt storage loop
Still confused? Think of it like a thermos flask full of liquid lava. Trough/tower plants literally heat salt until it melts, pour the hot liquid into an insulated tank, and use that stored heat hours later to boil water. The "storage hours" number (e.g. 4.5h, 9h, 12h) is how many hours the plant can run at full power from the tank alone.

📍 Why the Northern Cape?

  • CSP needs direct normal irradiance (DNI) — bright, focused, cloud-free sunlight. The Northern Cape has ~2,900 kWh/m²/year, among the highest on Earth (comparable to the Atacama Desert and the Sahara).
  • Dry climate = few clouds and rain days, so the sun is predictable, which makes storage scheduling easier.
  • Large, flat, sparsely populated land for mirror fields.
  • Existing 400 kV transmission corridors from solar/wind zones connect plants like Redstone, Kathu, and Ilanga to load centres (Gauteng, Western Cape).

🏛️ Role in the grid

Dispatchable, not intermittent

Because generation is decoupled from moment-to-moment sunshine (heat can be stored), CSP can be told to generate at specific times — especially the 17:00–21:00 evening peak when PV fades but demand stays high. This is why producers are willing to pay more per MWh than for raw PV.

Economically complementary to PV

Plain PV + batteries give minutes-to-hours of storage; CSP gives hours to overnight from a cheaper, longer-lived thermal medium. CSP fills the "shoulder" of the evening ramp, reducing reliance on OCGTs (open-cycle gas turbines) and load-shedding in the evening.

Grid services

With spinning turbines and inertia, CSP can provide services batteries struggle with — inertia, voltage support, and frequency response. Storage also allows "load shifting": charge at midday, discharge at night.

Cost & development

CSP is capital-intensive (mirror fields, tanks, towers) and historically more expensive per MWh than PV. It was procured under REIPPPP bid windows 1–4; Redstone's completion (2025) is the first new plant since 2019. The cost premium is justified by its dispatchability.

⚠️ Honest limitations

  • Cloudy days — bad weather means the tank can't fully recharge, so night output depends on the previous day's sun. You can't store what the mirrors didn't collect.
  • Storage is finite — a 4.5h plant can cover the evening peak, but only a 12h plant (Redstone) can promise "all night."
  • Water & dust — mirrors need regular cleaning (desert dust washes off) and some plants need cooling water; troughs often use air-cooled condensers to save water.
  • Cost — per-MWh cost remains above PV, so the value proposition is dispatchability, not raw cheapness.
  • Land & lead time — huge mirror footprints and multi-year construction.
