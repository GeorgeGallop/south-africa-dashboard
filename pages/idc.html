Industrial Development Corporation of South Africa

The state's development finance institution — funding factories, mines, energy and African industry since 1940

The IDC is South Africa's largest development finance institution by assets. It is wholly owned by the government. The Minister of Trade, Industry and Competition holds the shares. Unlike Eskom or Transnet, it does not run a national grid or a rail network — it lends and takes equity in businesses that are supposed to industrialise the country. The book at 31 March 2025 was about R93 billion across 636 clients.

History & origins
  • 1940: The Industrial Development Corporation is established by statute to build manufacturing capacity during wartime shortages and to reduce import dependence. From the start it is a state financier of industry, not a commercial bank.
  • 1951: IDC founds Foskor to mine phosphate at Phalaborwa — still the group's largest operating subsidiary. Through the apartheid decades the IDC is used to seed strategic industries (chemicals, textiles, metals, import-substitution plants) that private capital would not fund on development terms.
  • 1990s: After 1994 the mandate is restated as industrial development plus jobs, black economic empowerment and regional investment. The IDC becomes the state's main tool for taking equity in mines, steel, auto, agro-processing and later renewables when a project needs a patient balance sheet.
  • 2000s–2010s: The book expands across the rest of Africa (Mozal aluminium in Mozambique, mines and infrastructure in Ghana, Uganda, the DRC). At home the IDC is a recurring shareholder in distressed industrial names — Scaw, Foskor, Hulamin, Columbus Stainless — the "clean and fix" book.
  • 2024: sefa (Small Enterprise Finance Agency), previously an IDC subsidiary, is transferred out to the dtic / Sedfa from 1 October 2024. Small-business retail credit leaves the group; IDC is left as a mid-to-large industrial financier.
  • 2025–26: Portfolio ~R93 billion; 88% in South Africa, 12% on the continent. Profit drops to R1.8 billion as impairments and a thinner dividend year follow the R7 billion FY2024 result. The board remains aligned to the NDP, the Medium Term Development Plan and dtic masterplans.
What the IDC actually does

The IDC does not print money and it is not a grant office. It raises capital, then puts it into firms as term loans, mezzanine and equity. Over 94% of the book is tagged to industrial-policy sectors: manufacturing, mining, energy, agro-industry and infrastructure. The developmental test is jobs, localisation and black ownership; the commercial test is whether the loan is repaid and the equity is worth something.

Debt (76% of the book)

Senior and subordinated loans to industrial projects. This is the core product: multi-year funding that a commercial bank will not hold alone, often clubbed with DFIs or commercial lenders.

Equity (24% of the book)

Shareholdings taken to get a project built, or converted from debt in a restructuring. IDC says it does not want to control firms forever; in practice Foskor, Scaw and others have sat on the balance sheet for years.

FY2025 funding was on the order of R13–16 billion approved/disbursed depending on the line item, supporting about 15,700 jobs. The Rest of Africa book is spread across 16 countries, concentrated in Southern Africa (~R5.4 billion), East Africa (~R2.8 billion) and West Africa (~R1.9 billion).

Group structure

The legal parent is the IDC of South Africa Limited. The mini-group used for funding operations is IDC plus three wholly owned treasury/funding vehicles: Findevco, Konoil and Impofin. Operating subsidiaries and associates sit around that core. sefa is no longer in the group.

Who it answers to — and who it is not

The Republic is the only shareholder. The dtic is the shareholder department. There is no listed IDC ordinary share. Private firms sit in the portfolio (Hulamin, Scaw, BAIC, Exxaro via Eyesizwe); they do not own the Corporation.

IDC is not the Land Bank (agriculture), not the DBSA (public infrastructure), not sefa/Sedfa (small enterprise), and not the PIC (pension-fund asset manager). Those are the other state balance sheets people confuse it with. IDC's job is industrial projects — factories, mines, energy plants, agro-processing — in South Africa and, secondarily, the rest of the continent under AfCFTA-aligned regional value chains.

The constraint

A development financier that takes equity in sick industrial companies ends up owning them. Foskor, Scaw and the textile plants are the exhibit. Impairments were 30.6% of the mini-group book at FY2025; the IFRS non-performing loan ratio was 41.7%. That is the cost of the mandate: the IDC is asked to fund things commercial banks will not, then criticised when those things do not pay.

Gearing was 33.4% — still room on the balance sheet, but not infinite. Profit collapsing from R7.0 billion to R1.8 billion in one year is a reminder that a large equity book (Foskor fair-value swings, dividends) makes earnings lumpy. The strategic question for the rest of the decade is whether IDC stays a patient industrial shareholder or is forced into a faster "fix and exit" so capital can rotate into energy, autos and African manufacturing.
