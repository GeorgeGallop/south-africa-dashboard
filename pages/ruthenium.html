Ruthenium (Ru)

South Africa supplies ~85–94% of primary ruthenium
Overview Platinum Palladium Rhodium Ruthenium Iridium Osmium
What is it?

The lightest PGM (atomic number 44). A hard, brittle, silvery metal used as a chemical catalyst, in hard-disk coatings, chip resistors, and electrochemical anodes — not in car exhausts.

Where is it found?

Recovered from Bushveld PGM ore, especially UG2, as a minor metal in the 6E basket. Africa accounts for ~99% of mined ruthenium (South Africa ~95%, Zimbabwe the rest).

What is it used for?

Chemical process catalysts, hard-disk drive media, thick-film chip resistors, chlor-alkali and other electrochemical anodes, and emerging data-centre / AI-related electronics.

Supply Chain

Separated at the precious-metals refinery after the major PGMs. Sold as sponge or compounds to electronics and chemical companies, largely in Japan, China, the US and Europe. Recycling is limited compared with autocatalyst PGMs.

What ruthenium is

Ruthenium is one of the two "minor" industrial PGMs that the auto industry barely uses. It is harder and more brittle than platinum, melts at 2,334°C, and is an excellent catalyst for several bulk-chemical reactions. In electronics it is used in nanometre-scale coatings: a few atomic layers of ruthenium on a hard-disk platter enable perpendicular magnetic recording, which is how modern HDDs store data.

In the Bushveld, ruthenium (with iridium and rhodium) is concentrated in the UG2 reef. Northam has noted that at Zondereinde, platinum, rhodium, iridium and ruthenium make up about 75% of the UG2 metal split. Output cannot be increased without mining more PGM ore. Heraeus / SFA (Oxford) estimate that five companies — Valterra Platinum, Sibanye-Stillwater, Northam, Impala Platinum and African Rainbow Minerals — produce about 80% of the world's ruthenium, and South Africa as a whole about 94% of primary supply.

The metal was an afterthought in the PGM basket for decades. That changed as chemical demand set records, hard-disk coatings recovered, and China treated ruthenium as a critical mineral. Johnson Matthey says the 2025 market deficit was nearly 300,000 oz — about a quarter of annual consumption — and the price hit an all-time high around $1,275/oz by year-end. The Minerals Council average for 2025 was US$731/oz (+67%).

What it is used for

Johnson Matthey puts 2025 demand at 1.27 million ounces (~39 tonnes):

  • Chemical (~567 koz, ~45%): Catalysts for acetic acid and related processes, ammonia and specialised organic synthesis. This book hit a new high in the mid-2020s as Chinese chemical capacity grew.
  • Electrical and electronics (~413 koz, ~33%): Hard-disk drive media (Seagate, Western Digital, Toshiba and their Asian supply chains), thick-film chip resistors, and other electronic pastes. HDD demand rebounded with data-centre builds even as SSDs took the consumer slot. Northam has explicitly linked new industrial demand to AI and data storage.
  • Electrochemical (~135 koz): Dimensionally stable anodes for chlor-alkali (chlorine and caustic soda) and related electrolytic processes. Ruthenium oxide coatings on titanium anodes are an industry standard.
  • Other (~150 koz): Superalloys, some fuel-cell research, jewellery alloying in tiny amounts, and strategic stockpiling (Johnson Matthey does not count all strategic buying in official demand).

Unlike platinum, palladium and rhodium, there is no autocatalyst recycling engine behind ruthenium. End-of-life recovery from electronics and chemical catalysts exists but is smaller and slower, which is why mine-supply shocks pass straight into the price.

Who buys it

  • Electronics: HDD makers and their Japanese, Korean, Chinese and US materials suppliers. Chip-resistor manufacturers in Japan, Taiwan and China.
  • Chemical companies in China, Europe and the US buying process catalysts — often via Heraeus, Johnson Matthey and other precious-metals houses rather than direct from the mine.
  • Electrochemical equipment makers (anode coaters such as De Nora and peers) serving chlor-alkali plants worldwide.
  • Trade routes: South African exports of unwrought iridium, osmium and ruthenium (HS 711041) go first to Japan, Hong Kong, the United States, China and the United Kingdom. OEC data for 2025 put Japan as the top destination (about ZAR 7 billion), then Hong Kong and the US. South Africa's share of global exports in this combined category is around 49%.

Chinese strategic purchasing and export-control policy have become part of the price. Johnson Matthey flagged Chinese export controls on ruthenium and strategic buying as amplifiers of the 2025 squeeze. Industrial consumers in Japan, Europe and the US are therefore not just buying metal — they are buying insurance against a single-country mine source and a tightening Chinese policy overlay.

Production and consumption

Johnson Matthey does not publish a country split for ruthenium the way it does for platinum, but primary supply was 972,000 oz in 2025 (~30 tonnes), down from 1.13 Moz in 2024 when South African producers released refined inventory. Demand of 1.27 Moz produced a 293,000 oz deficit. African Development Bank figures for 2023 put South African mine production at 28.5 tonnes — 95% of the world — with Zimbabwe 1.3 tonnes.

Sibanye-Stillwater alone reported 266,000 oz of ruthenium in 2024 from its South African PGM operations. USGS US imports of ruthenium were 9,780 kg in 2024 and an estimated 14,000 kg in 2025. Producer stock sales that papered over earlier deficits are running down, which is why 2025 was the year the shortage showed up in the price.

The future

Ruthenium is one of the few PGMs whose demand story is not hostage to the internal combustion engine. Chemical catalysts, data storage and chip components scale with industrial production and computing, not with vehicle powertrains. AI and hyperscale data centres that still use high-capacity HDDs for cold storage are a real, if cyclical, bid. Johnson Matthey expects the market to stay in deficit in 2026.

The constraint is supply. South African PGM mining is shrinking, UG2-heavy shafts are not being replaced one-for-one, and there is no large non-African ruthenium mine to open. Recycling will grow but from a low base. That combination — concentrated supply, industrial demand, critical-mineral politics — is why fabricators and governments now treat ruthenium as strategic rather than as a cheap basket leftover.

Risks run both ways. Chemical demand can drop with a Chinese industrial slowdown (Johnson Matthey already forecasts chemical ounces down in 2026). HDD coatings can be thrifted. But the structural picture for South Africa is favourable: every remaining UG2 ounce carries a ruthenium credit the market is newly willing to pay for.
