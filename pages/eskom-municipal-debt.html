⚡ Eskom's Municipal Debt Crisis

The R119 Billion That Municipalities Owe Eskom — Why It Matters, What's Being Done, and Who Will Pay

Municipalities are the single biggest debtor to Eskom. By the end of 2025 they owed the utility R110.5 billion for electricity — a figure that has since climbed toward R119 billion and keeps rising. The money is not a rounding error: it is roughly 60% of Eskom's annual fuel bill, it forces Eskom to borrow instead of invest, and it undermines the entire municipal electricity distribution model. Every billion rand of unpaid municipal debt is a billion rand that does not get spent on keeping Eskom's generators running, its grid maintained, or its workforce paid. This page sets out the scale and trajectory of the debt, who owes it, why it exists, what Eskom, the Treasury and Parliament are doing about it, and what the realistic outcomes are.

The Numbers — How Big Is This Debt, Actually?

South African municipalities act as resellers of electricity: they buy bulk power from Eskom, distribute it through municipal networks, and bill households and businesses. The model only works if municipalities collect more from their customers than they owe Eskom. When collection collapses, the municipal owes Eskom but still has to pay salaries and maintain networks — so the Eskom debt balloons. The trajectory is the alarming part. Municipal arrears to Eskom have risen relentlessly:

  • March 2025: R94.6 billion
  • December 2025: R110.5 billion (+17% in nine months)
  • Mid-2026: ~R119 billion and rising (Eskom chairman Mteto Nyati confirmed the figure is still climbing)
Why the "overdue" number matters more than the headline: Not all of Eskom's R110+ billion is "arrears" — but a large share is. SCOPA was told that R94 billion was overdue in early 2025, and that Eskom had R8.9 billion of revenue it could not even recognise on its balance sheet because municipalities could not pay (up from R6.3 billion in 2023 and projected to double). Debt that Eskom knows it will never collect is not an asset — it is a hole in the balance sheet.

Cases like Kai !Garib (Northern Cape) illustrate the extreme: a municipality of ~85,000 people with more than R1 billion of debt — mostly to Eskom — against annual revenue of under R400 million. It cannot pay its own staff, cannot treat water, and cannot service its Eskom bill.

Where It Started — The Origins

1. The Municipal Electricity Model Itself

Municipalities are Eskom's distribution agents, buying bulk power and reselling it. Historically, electricity was the most profitable municipal service — the surplus cross-subsidised water, refuse and rates. That made the electricity account the cash cow of local government. But it also meant that when municipalities mismanaged electricity revenue, the damage hit everything else. The model gave municipalities enormous exposure to Eskom bills with no independent incentive to pay on time.

2. The Non-Payment Legacy

The anti-apartheid rent and service boycotts of the 1980s normalised non-payment, and the culture survived democracy — especially in townships. Political parties courted votes by tolerating non-payment. By the 2000s, many municipalities simply stopped enforcing disconnections. With no culture of payment and no enforcement, the electricity account became the natural place for the shortfall to accumulate.

3. Failed Billing & Revenue Systems

Municipal billing systems have been a historical disaster. When meters are not read, bills are wrong or not issued, and customers rightly refuse to pay. When the system cannot track who owes what, collection is guesswork. Johannesburg's Phakama billing collapse and Tshwane's Project Kantar scandal are the best-known examples — but hundreds of municipalities have never had a functional billing system. You cannot collect a debt you cannot calculate.

4. Structural Under-Investment & Load Shedding

Load shedding from 2008 onward did double damage: it reduced the electricity municipalities could sell (their revenue), while Eskom's own financial crisis meant it had neither the money nor the will to enforce collection against its largest customers. Municipal maintenance of distribution networks was deferred for years, making illegal connections and losses worse.

5. Corruption and Revenue Leakage

The Auditor-General has documented how municipal officials and councillors stole or diverted electricity revenue — through fraud, ghost staff, inflated contracts, and collusion with illegal connections. In many municipalities, a large share of the cash that was collected never reached the bank account, let alone Eskom. Revenue leakage of 30-50% of billings has been flagged repeatedly.

6. The "Government Itself Doesn't Pay" Factor

Municipalities in turn struggle to collect because national and provincial departments — schools, hospitals, police stations — owe municipalities R60+ billion. When the state doesn't pay its own bills, municipalities have a perfect excuse not to pay theirs. The debt chain runs from the fiscus to municipalities to Eskom — and everyone in the chain blames the next.

What Made It Worse — The Escalation (2023-2026)

  • The debt relief programme was designed, then mostly failed: National Treasury launched the Eskom Municipal Debt Relief programme in 2023: municipalities that signed up would have historical debt written off over three years (33.3% per year) provided they paid current bills and improved collection. Of the 71 municipalities that signed up, more than 61 failed to meet the conditions. "Persistent defaulters" are now being expelled from the programme. The incentive that was supposed to fix the problem was largely ignored by the municipalities it was designed for.
  • Eskom's financial crisis made the debt existential: Eskom itself was bankrupt by the early 2020s, kept alive by the R254 billion Eskom Debt Relief Act (2023) — subsequently revised by Treasury to R230 billion with support extended to 2028/29. Every rand of uncollected municipal debt made Eskom more dependent on the fiscus and on tariff increases (which hit paying consumers hardest).
  • Treasury moved to the stick: In 2025-2026, Treasury withheld R13.5 billion in equitable share grants from 69 municipalities that passed unfunded budgets and defaulted on obligations to Eskom, water boards, SARS, the Auditor-General and pension funds. Those 69 municipalities collectively owe R27.4 billion. The condition for release: sign credible repayment agreements with creditors.
  • Eskom began formal enforcement: In March 2026, Eskom initiated a formal public-consultation process against 14 municipalities, legally clearing the way to reduce, interrupt or terminate electricity supply at bulk points. This was the first time Eskom moved systematically from threatening to acting. Joburg was issued a formal notice in May 2026 as its arrears reached R5.2 billion.
  • The supply-interruption catch-22: Cutting supply to a defaulting municipality punishes the residents who did pay, and destroys the municipality's ability to collect revenue (no power means no business rates). Eskom has so far used targeted interruption at bulk points and negotiated repayment plans rather than full blackouts, but the threat is now real and repeated.
The 61-of-71 failure rate is the single most important recent fact: A relief programme that gave municipalities three years of debt write-off in exchange for simply paying current bills and fixing billing failed for 86% of participants. This proves the core diagnosis: the problem is not that municipalities cannot pay — it is that they will not, and that the political and administrative systems meant to force them cannot do so. Any fix that assumes municipalities will voluntarily collect and pay has already been shown to fail.

Why It Matters — What the Debt Does to Eskom & the Country

💳 The Balance Sheet Hole

Debt that will never be repaid is not an asset — it is a liability. Eskom's municipal arrears undermine its ability to show a clean balance sheet, to raise debt at reasonable rates, and to justify its tariff path. The R8.9 billion of "unrecognisable" revenue grows every year. Eskom chairman Mteto Nyati said in 2026 that dealing with municipal arrears is "critical to its long-term financial sustainability."

⚙️ Less Money for Generation & Maintenance

Every billion not collected from municipalities is a billion Eskom must borrow or receive from the fiscus. It means less spending on power station maintenance, grid upgrades, and energy availability — the very things that caused load shedding. The municipal debt crisis directly contributed to the crisis of generation availability that blacked out the country between 2008 and 2024.

🔁 A Direct Subsidy from Paying Customers

When municipalities don't pay Eskom, Eskom recovers the shortfall through higher tariffs charged to everyone else — households, business, and industry that do pay. Non-paying municipalities are therefore effectively subsidised by paying customers. This transfers wealth from well-managed municipalities (like Cape Town) and private users to the most mismanaged municipalities in the country.

🏙️ Undermining the Municipal Distribution Future

Eskom's long-term plan is to exit municipal distribution and let municipalities (or new entities) take over. But if municipalities cannot pay for the power they already distribute, the case for handing them more responsibility collapses. The distribution-agency model (DAA) that Eskom is negotiating explicitly conditions it on municipalities fixing payment discipline — because Eskom refuses to keep subsidising failed distribution.

How It Is Being Addressed — The Full Toolkit

No single measure can fix R119 billion of municipal debt. The response is a toolbox of interlocking mechanisms, several launched only in the last two years:

1. The Eskom Municipal Debt Relief Programme (2023-2026)

Structure: municipalities commit to paying current consumption in full and improving collection; in exchange Eskom/Treasury writes off historical debt in thirds over three years (Feb 2025 SCOPA framework). Status: 61+ of 71 participants failing; persistent defaulters being removed. Treasury is shifting from "give relief" to "terminate relief for those who fail" — the conditionality is finally being enforced.

2. Ring-Fencing Municipal Electricity Revenue

Eskom's central demand: all municipal electricity revenue must be ring-fenced — paid into a dedicated account used only for paying Eskom and maintaining the electrical network, not for general municipal spending. This stops the age-old practice of municipalities spending electricity cash on salaries, vehicles, and tenders, and then telling Eskom they can't pay. This is the single most consequential reform on the table.

3. Prepaid Metering as the Collection Mechanism

Prepaid electricity meters force payment in advance — you cannot use power you haven't paid for. Eskom and Treasury are pushing municipalities toward universal prepaid and smart metering, and Treasury has launched a new conditional grant for smart prepaid meter rollout. Prepaid does not eliminate the historical debt, but it structurally prevents new debt from accumulating on the consumption side.

4. Supply Interruption as the Enforcement Backstop

Eskom's March 2026 formal consultation against 14 municipalities creates a legal pathway to reduced/interrupted supply at bulk points — the ultimate enforcement tool. Eskom says it will use it in a graduated way (limiting supply, not blacking out entire towns), but the credible threat of interruption is now real. It has already pushed several municipalities into repayment agreements and is restoring the principle that power has to be paid for.

5. Treasury Leverage — Withholding Equitable Share Grants

Treasury withheld R13.5 billion from 69 municipalities that passed unfunded budgets and defaulted on creditors, and is conditioning release on signed repayment agreements. Linking national grant money to payment discipline attacks the root incentive: municipalities that won't collect from their own citizens now face losing the money that pays their own staff.

6. The Distribution Agency Agreement (DAA) & Municipal Debt Elimination

Eskom's proposed Distribution Agency Agreement with financially-distressed municipalities gives Eskom or an appointed agent control over billing and revenue collection — taking collection out of the municipality's hands entirely. Municipalities that refuse are facing the supply-interruption route. The DAA is effectively Eskom's way of saying: if you can't collect, let us collect — or lose supply.

Who Will Actually Fix It?

The honest answer is that the fix will come from enforcement, not goodwill. The actors and their roles:

Eskom Board & Management

Under chairman Mteto Nyati and CEO Dan Marokane, Eskom has gone from pleading to enforcing: formal consultation against 14 municipalities, terminated relief for persistent defaulters, and the DAA push. Eskom's improved financial position (FY2024/25 profit ~R10bn; operating profit R46.3bn; revenue R183.7bn) finally gives it the power to act rather than beg. This is the single biggest change: a financially stronger Eskom is politically able to cut off non-payers.

National Treasury

Treasury holds the biggest levers: the R230bn Eskom support package, the debt-relief programme, the equitable-share withholdings, and the new prepaid-metering grant. Treasury's 2025 Budget Review signals it is shifting from unconditional support to strict conditionality. Treasury also has the constitutional power to intercept and redirect provincial allocations to pay municipal bills — the "state pays itself" lever that has barely been used.

Parliament (SCOPA) & the NPA

SCOPA has publicly endorsed Eskom's debt-elimination proposal and is pressuring CoGTA and Treasury to implement it. The NPA and SIU are the accountability end: prosecuting the officials who stole municipal electricity revenue is what breaks the corruption cycle. Without convictions and asset forfeiture, new debt will replace old debt as fast as it is written off.

The Municipalities Themselves (and Voters)

Ultimately the money is in the hands of municipal residents, businesses, and the officials who manage collection. Municipalities that reform — like those that sign DAA agreements, roll out prepaid meters, and enforce credit control — can recover revenue sharply (the metros that enforce collection exceed 90% recovery). The 2026 local elections, fought heavily on service delivery, are the voters' chance to remove the leaders who presided over the collapse.

The honest assessment: The debt will not be "repaid" in full — a large share will ultimately be written off, one way or another, because the municipalities that owe it cannot and will not pay. The real question is whether the write-off happens with conditions (municipalities that reform get relief; those that don't get cut off) or as a bailout (everyone gets forgiven, and the debt simply reappears). The difference is the entire ballgame. On the current trajectory — with Eskom enforcing, Treasury withholding, and prepaid meters rolling out — the most likely outcome over the next 3-5 years is: harsh enforcement against the top 14, conditional relief for the reform-minded, and a slow, painful restructuring of municipal electricity that ultimately transfers distribution to stronger hands. The alternative — continued tolerance of non-payment — is what caused load shedding, and it is no longer politically survivable.
