🏛️ Vesting Contracts & Price Formation

How Eskom's legacy tariffs, the SAWEM Day-Ahead Market SMP, IPP PPA strike prices, and the Central Purchasing Agency interact — and why the "price of electricity" depends on which lens you're looking through.

🤔 The Price Confusion — Four Different Prices, One Grid

There isn't one "price of electricity" in South Africa. There are at least four, and they're all correct — they just measure different things.

Price Level (2025/26 est.) What It Really Is
🏛️ Eskom Regulated Tariff
(Megaflex Peak)		 ~R 6.85 / kWh
684.59 c/kWh		 The bundled, NERSA-approved price paid by Eskom's direct customers. Covers generation, transmission, distribution, legacy costs, subsidies, and a regulated return. This is the "retail-ish" price that appears on your bill. It reflects Eskom's average cost of service, not the marginal cost of any specific power plant.
📊 DAM System Marginal Price (SMP)
SAWEM market clearing		 ~R 1.20–3.50 / kWh
est. from Eskom coal costs		 The price determined by the marginal unit in the Day-Ahead Market. All dispatched generators receive the SMP (pay-as-clear). Set by the most expensive generator needed to meet demand in each hour — typically an Eskom coal plant running part-loaded, or an OCGT at peak. Not a retail price. It covers only the marginal energy cost, not the bundled fixed/legacy costs in the regulated tariff.
🌱 IPP PPA Strike Prices
REIPPP contract prices		 Wind: ~R 0.50–0.80 / kWh
Solar PV: ~R 0.40–0.70 / kWh		 Fixed-price, 20-year Power Purchase Agreements awarded through competitive REIPPP auctions. These are contract prices, not market prices. A wind farm with a strike price of R 0.70/kWh gets paid R 0.70/kWh regardless of what the SMP is. The CPA (as counterparty) takes the volumetric and price risk — if the SMP is below the strike, the CPA pays the difference; if above, the IPP pays the CPA (via a CfD settled through the legacy charge). The IPP's marginal cost is near-zero (wind/sun are free), but its PPA price reflects the full cost of capital, construction, 20-year O&M, and a return.
💼 Eskom Vesting Contract Price
Transition hedge		 Year 1: fixed reference price
→ Year 5: 100% SMP		 A transitional mechanism preventing Eskom Generation from exercising market power. Each Eskom station signs a vesting contract with the CPA that includes a declining energy hedge: in Year 1, the station receives a fixed reference price for 100% of its output (hedging against the risk of bidding its true marginal cost into the DAM). By Year 5, the hedge declines to 0%, and the station is fully exposed to the SMP. The hedge percentage declines by 20% per year. This is the core market power mitigation mechanism.
The critical insight: The SMP is not the price Eskom's customers pay. It's the wholesale energy-only price in the DAM. The regulated tariff bundles generation capacity, transmission, distribution, legacy costs, electrification subsidies, environmental levies, and a regulated return on assets on top of energy costs. When you see "electricity costs R 6.85/kWh" and "the DAM SMP is R 1.50/kWh", both numbers are correct for what they measure.

⚠️ The Central Problem: Eskom Sets the Marginal Price

South Africa faces a unique structural challenge: the same company that owns 90% of generation capacity will also be the de facto price setter in the market.

🏭
Eskom's Dominance in the Merit Order

Eskom Generation controls ~44 GW of coal, nuclear, pumped storage, and OCGT capacity — roughly 90% of system generation.

In a competitive DAM, generators bid their marginal cost. Eskom's coal fleet has marginal costs ranging from ~R 0.40/kWh (Matimba, efficient) to ~R 2.50/kWh (older, inefficient stations like Camden or Grootvlei). OCGTs (Ankerlig, Gourikwa) bid at ~R 3.50/kWh+ (diesel).

📈
The Marginal Unit Sets Everyone's Price

Under SAWEM's pay-as-clear mechanism (standard for wholesale markets), every dispatched generator receives the SMP — not its own bid price. If Eskom's most expensive coal unit is the marginal plant setting the SMP at R 1.50/kWh, then:

  • Cheap coal plants (marginal cost R 0.40) get inframarginal rent of R 1.10/kWh
  • Wind IPPs (PPA strike R 0.70) get SMP + a CfD top-up from the CPA
  • Solar PV (marginal cost ~R 0.00) gets the full R 1.50/kWh SMP
  • The CPA collects/bears the difference between SMP and PPA strike prices
🔴
Market Power Risk: Economic Withholding

A dominant generator can raise the SMP by economic withholding — bidding some capacity above its true marginal cost. If Eskom bids 2 GW of its fleet at R 5.00/kWh instead of R 0.80/kWh, and those units end up marginal, the SMP rises from R 0.80 to R 5.00 for everyone.

The Market Conduct Rules (Section 2.1(4)) prohibit this: "it is not necessary to prove that the manipulation had an actual effect on prices; it is sufficient that the conduct was intended or likely to affect prices." Eskom Generation is explicitly recognised as dominant (Section 2.8(6)). But detection is difficult — distinguishing "strategic bidding" from "genuine cost-reflective bidding" requires detailed plant-level heat-rate and fuel-cost data.

🛡️
The Vesting Hedge: Market Power Mitigation

The vesting contract's declining hedge is the primary mitigation tool. The CPA document (Section 2.1) specifies: each vesting contract includes "an Energy Hedge — a financial hedge starting at 100% of a reference price in Year 1 and reducing by 20% per year to 0% in Year 5."

This means: in Year 1, Eskom receives a fixed price for all its output — it has no incentive to bid high to manipulate the SMP, because its revenue is fixed regardless. As the hedge declines, Eskom gradually gains exposure to the SMP, but by Year 5, the market is expected to have sufficient non-Eskom competition (IPPs, imports, demand response) to discipline pricing.

Source: SAWEM Specification, p. 7

"Eskom Generation's market power and the honoring of the contractual terms of the PPAs negotiated with IPPs as part of the transition to a market environment" are the two central challenges the CPA and vesting contracts are designed to address.

— SAWEM Market Specification, Chapter 2: ESI Structure

🔧 How the Vesting Contract Mechanism Works

1
Step 1: Eskom Generation signs a Vesting Contract with the CPA

Per the Market Code definition (Section 1, "Vesting Contract"): "a contract or other financial arrangement between the NTCSA and an Eskom generator for the sale of a specified amount of electricity at a price determined by the Regulator as a mechanism to facilitate the transition to a competitive market."

2
Step 2: The CPA receives Availability Payments

The CPA pays each Eskom station a fixed Availability Payment covering capital costs, fixed O&M, and a regulated return on assets. This is the "capacity" component — it keeps the plant available even if it never runs. Plus a Generation Capacity Charge for reserving plant capacity.

3
Step 3: The CPA applies the Energy Hedge

The CPA determines the hedged portion of generation. In Year 1: 100% of output at a fixed reference price. The hedge declines by 20% per year. The unhedged portion is exposed to the DAM SMP: if the SMP is above the station's marginal cost, the station profits; if below, the station loses money and has an incentive to improve efficiency.

4
Step 4: Eskom bids into the DAM as a normal BRP

Eskom Generation bids its plant's marginal costs into the Day-Ahead Market like any other participant. The CPA registers as a BRP for all Eskom stations. The Market Operator runs the co-optimised SCUC/SCED algorithm, determines the SMP, and dispatches units in merit order.

5
Step 5: Settlement — the CPA nets everything

The CPA pays Eskom the Availability Payment + the hedge settlement + the actual SMP revenue from the DAM. The CPA also pays IPPs their PPA strike prices and sells the energy into the DAM. Any net deficit (or surplus) flows through the Legacy Charge Mechanism to all transmission-connected consumers, approved by NERSA.

Source: SAWEM Specification, Chapter 5 — Central Purchasing Agency

"Each vesting contract specifies: (a) the Availability Payment — a fixed monthly payment covering capital costs, fixed O&M, and a regulated return on assets; (b) the Capacity Payment — a payment per MW of available capacity; (c) the Energy Hedge — a financial hedge starting at 100% of a reference price in Year 1 and reducing by 20% per year to 0% in Year 5."

— SAWEM CPA Document, Section 2.1: Vesting Contracts

🌬️ The Wind & Solar Paradox: $0 Marginal Cost, Paid R 0.70/kWh

Your intuition is correct: a wind turbine's marginal cost is effectively zero (the wind is free). Yet REIPPPP PPAs pay R 0.50–0.80/kWh. How does this work, and where does the money come from?

💸
The PPA Price ≠ Marginal Cost

A PPA strike price covers the full levelised cost of the project over 20 years:

  • Capital cost recovery — building a wind farm costs ~R 15–20 million/MW upfront
  • Financing costs — debt service at SA interest rates (12%+)
  • O&M over 20 years — turbine maintenance, land leases, staff, insurance
  • Return on equity — typical 12–15% IRR for infrastructure
  • Decommissioning — end-of-life removal costs
Analogy: Your house's "marginal cost" of heating on a cold day is zero once it's built. But the price you pay on your bond includes construction, financing, and maintenance. Same logic applies to a wind farm.
🏦
Who Pays the Difference?

The difference between the PPA strike price and the SMP is settled through the Legacy Charge mechanism. Here's the flow in two scenarios:

When SMP < PPA Strike (most likely):
Wind PPA = R 0.70/kWh, SMP = R 0.60/kWh
The CPA pays the IPP R 0.70/kWh. It sells into the DAM at R 0.60/kWh. Loss: R 0.10/kWh, recovered through the Legacy Charge on consumers.
When SMP > PPA Strike (possible in future):
Same wind PPA = R 0.70/kWh, SMP = R 1.50/kWh (OCGT on the margin)
The CPA pays the IPP R 0.70/kWh. It sells into the DAM at R 1.50/kWh. Profit: R 0.80/kWh, credited back through the Legacy Charge.
The CPA absorbs all volumetric and price risk. It is legally obliged to take-or-pay the entire output of each IPP at the strike price, regardless of whether the energy is needed or what the SMP is. This is the volumetric exposure you identified — Eskom (via the CPA) bears the risk that wind doesn't blow or that the SMP collapses. The PPA counterparty (the bank lending to the IPP) requires this certainty: they won't finance a project whose revenue depends on an hourly wholesale price that didn't exist when the PPA was signed.
📉
The Merit Order Effect: Renewables Lower the SMP

When wind or solar is generating, they bid at near-zero marginal cost into the DAM. In the merit order stack, they displace the most expensive coal or OCGT plants. This pushes the SMP down — which is good for consumers buying at the SMP, but bad for baseload coal plants and for the CPA's legacy charge position (because a lower SMP means a bigger gap between PPA strike prices and the SMP).

🔗
CfD Structure vs. Physical PPA

The SAWEM Specification (Chapter 7) proposes moving from physical PPAs to Contracts for Difference (CfDs) for future procurement. Under a CfD:

  • The IPP sells its energy directly into the DAM at the SMP
  • The CPA settles the financial difference: if SMP < Strike, the CPA pays the IPP the difference; if SMP > Strike, the IPP pays the CPA
  • The IPP is exposed to basis risk (its actual output vs. the CfD reference volume) — it must manage its own volumetric risk through the Balancing Mechanism
  • This aligns incentives: the IPP has an incentive to generate when the SMP is high (valuable energy) and can be curtailed when the SMP is low (not valuable)

🏦 The Legacy Charge: Who Foots the Bill?

"The CPA's costs and revenues are ring-fenced in a Legacy Account within the NTCSA. The net balance (deficit or surplus) is adjusted annually through the legacy charge — a charge levied on all transmission-connected consumers approved by NERSA."

— SAWEM CPA Document, Section 4: Legacy Charge Mechanism
What flows through the Legacy Account:
Inflows (Credits)Outflows (Debits)
SMP revenue from selling Eskom + IPP output into the DAM Availability Payments to Eskom Generation (vesting contracts)
CfD settlement receipts from IPPs (when SMP > strike) PPA payments to IPPs at contracted strike prices
Ancillary service charge revenue Ancillary service procurement costs
Legacy charge collected from consumers Legacy charge refunds to consumers (if surplus)
The scale of the exposure:

As of 2025, REIPPPP has allocated ~6,200 MW of renewable capacity. At a weighted average PPA strike of ~R 0.90/kWh and assuming a DAM SMP of ~R 1.20/kWh, the CPA would be in surplus on the energy component (selling above the PPA price). However:

  • The Availability Payments to Eskom's coal fleet (44 GW × R 200–400/kW/year) will dominate the Legacy Account — these are the fixed costs of keeping coal plants available, whether they run or not
  • When renewable penetration rises and the SMP falls (merit order effect), the CPA's SMP revenue declines, potentially swinging the Legacy Account into deficit even as physical electricity becomes cheaper
  • The Legacy Charge thus becomes a de facto carbon transition tax — consumers pay for the fixed costs of coal capacity that runs less frequently, plus the premium for renewable PPAs

⚠️ This is the fundamental tension: The SMP may fall as renewables enter (good for short-term energy costs), but the Legacy Charge must rise to cover the fixed costs of coal plants that are no longer running (bad for total system costs). The total cost of electricity (SMP + Legacy Charge + network charges) may not fall — it may even rise during the transition, even though the "headline" SMP is lower.

🔑 The Bottom Line

1️⃣
Eskom's regulated tariff (R 6.85/kWh peak) is a bundled average-cost price — it covers everything: generation, grid, legacy costs, subsidies, returns. It is not a market price.
2️⃣
The DAM SMP will be set by Eskom's coal fleet marginal costs because Eskom controls ~90% of generation. The vesting hedge mitigates (but does not eliminate) the market power risk.
3️⃣
IPP PPA strike prices (R 0.40–0.80/kWh) are levelised-cost prices, not marginal-cost prices. The CPA absorbs the difference between these and the SMP through the Legacy Charge.
4️⃣
The Legacy Charge ensures that the total cost of the system (fixed coal costs + IPP premiums + SMP revenue) is recovered from consumers. The headline SMP may fall, but the all-in cost to consumers may not.
