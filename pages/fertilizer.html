What fertilizer is

A fertilizer is any material applied to soil or leaves to supply plant nutrients. Commercial mineral fertilizer is built around three macronutrients, written on every bag as an N-P-K grade (for example 28-0-0 limestone ammonium nitrate, or 12-32-16 MAP-based blend):

  • N Nitrogen — drives leaf growth and yield. Applied as urea (46% N), limestone ammonium nitrate / LAN (~28% N), ammonium sulphate, calcium ammonium nitrate, or as MAP/DAP which also carry phosphorus.
  • P Phosphorus (quoted as P2O5) — root development, energy transfer, early growth. From phosphate rock via phosphoric acid into MAP, DAP, single/triple superphosphate.
  • K Potassium (quoted as K2O) — water regulation, drought and disease tolerance. Almost entirely muriate of potash (KCl) plus some sulphate of potash (SOP) for chloride-sensitive crops such as potatoes, tobacco and fruit.

Secondary nutrients (sulphur, calcium, magnesium) and micronutrients (zinc, boron, copper, manganese) ride along in blends or as specialty products. Organic fertilizers — manure, compost, guano — recycle nutrients already in the farm system; they do not replace the imported N and K that commercial grain farming needs at scale.

What it is used for

Plants cannot make N, P or K from sunlight. Liebig’s law of the minimum still holds: the scarcest nutrient caps the yield. South African soils in the summer-grain belt are typically low in plant-available phosphorus and often acidic; nitrogen is leached or taken off with every harvest; potassium is removed in grain and sugarcane.

BFAP and Stellenbosch University (published in SA Grain, September 2025) allocate South African NPK use roughly as:

  • ~73% field crops — maize first, then sugarcane, wheat, soybeans, sunflower, canola.
  • ~15% horticulture — citrus, deciduous fruit, table grapes, nuts (macadamia NPK use jumped ~195% from 2013–15 to 2021–23).
  • ~12% intensive pastures, mainly dairy in the Western Cape, Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal.

Maize still takes the largest single slice. Soybean expansion is the main reason phosphorus and potassium use has grown faster than nitrogen: soybeans fix their own N but take more P and K, and they displaced about 900 000 ha of cereal area while oilseeds added ~1.1 million ha over two decades. Fertilizer is also a cash-flow item — Grain SA has repeatedly flagged that urea and MAP are among the largest variable costs on a Free State maize budget, so a 2022-style price spike cuts application rates the next season.

How it is made

Nitrogen — Haber–Bosch ammonia

Almost all synthetic N starts as ammonia (NH3). Nitrogen from air is combined with hydrogen over an iron catalyst at high pressure (Haber–Bosch, 1909). The hydrogen almost always comes from steam-reforming natural gas or coal-derived synthesis gas — which is why urea prices track energy prices. From ammonia the plant can make:

  • Urea — NH3 + CO2 → urea. Highest N analysis, cheapest to ship, 100% imported into South Africa.
  • Nitric acid → ammonium nitrate / LAN / CAN — Omnia’s Sasolburg nitric-acid complex and Sasol’s LAN route.
  • Ammonium sulphate — often a by-product of other chemical plants; large import volumes from China.
Phosphorus — rock + sulphuric acid

Phosphate rock (apatite) is digested with sulphuric acid to make phosphoric acid, then ammoniated to MAP (monoammonium phosphate) or DAP (diammonium phosphate). Foskor is South Africa’s only integrated producer of that chain. Sulphur for the acid is imported; ammonia for MAP is imported or bought from Sasol.

Potassium — mined, not manufactured

Potash is a mineral (sylvite, carnallite) mined in Canada, Russia, Belarus, Germany, Israel/Jordan (Dead Sea) and Chile (SOP). South Africa has no commercial potash deposit in production, so every tonne of KCl and SOP is imported. Blenders in Durban, Endicott and Cape Town then mix N, P and K straights into crop-specific NPK grades.

Who makes it — world and South Africa

Global producers

The world market is a handful of energy-rich and rock-rich states. Nitrogen is dominated by China, Russia, the Middle East (Saudi Ma’aden / SABIC, Qatar, Oman) and the US (CF Industries). Phosphate is Morocco/OCP, China, the US (Mosaic) and Russia. Potash is Nutrien and Mosaic (Canada), Uralkali / Belaruskal, K+S (Germany), ICL (Israel) and SQM (Chile SOP). Trading houses (Yara, ETG, Gavilon, ICH) sit between those plants and farm gates.

South African value chain

The Competition Commission’s Fresh Produce Market Inquiry (final non-confidential report, January 2025) describes four layers:

  1. Raw materials — Foskor (phosphate rock and phosphoric acid), Sasol (ammonia, LAN), Omnia (nitric acid, ammonium nitrate, calcium nitrate), plus sulphur and imported ammonia into Richards Bay.
  2. Straights — MAP/DAP (Foskor plus imports), urea (imports only), LAN (Sasol), KCl/SOP (imports only).
  3. Importers and blenders — Kynoch/ETG and Yara are the two largest dedicated importers; Omnia both manufactures and imports; co-ops and traders (NWK, Senwes, Profert, Gavilon, SQM, ICH) fill the rest.
  4. Retail — co-op silos, merchant depots, and on-farm delivery into the Free State, North West, Mpumalanga and Western Cape.

Broad-market share bands in that inquiry (2019–2022) put Kynoch, Foskor and Omnia each in the low- to mid-teens percent in some years, with a long tail of “others”. Those bands are ranges, not a league table — blending and imports shift year to year with the maize plantings.

Where South Africa makes it

Domestic manufacturing is a phosphate-and-nitrogen story. Phosphate rock is mined at Phalaborwa (Limpopo) and railed ~800 km to Richards Bay (KwaZulu-Natal) for acid and MAP. Nitrogen chemistry sits on the Sasolburg/Secunda coal-chemicals complex. Everything else is blending at the ports and inland depots.

Where it is imported — products, partners, ports

South Africa is a net importer of mixed and straight fertilizers even though it mines phosphate and makes ammonia. Urea is not manufactured locally at all. Potash is not mined. MAP still has to be topped up because Foskor has often run well below nameplate (IDC: MAP averaged ~59% of capacity over 2013–2022).

Import value was $824 million in 2023 after a twenty-year peak of more than $1.5 billion in 2022 when Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and gas prices sent urea, DAP and potash to records. World Bank Pink Sheet annual averages: urea $700/t in 2022 (from $229 in 2020), potash $863/t, DAP $772/t. By 2024 those had halved; 2025 prices firmed again (urea $423, DAP $685).

Five-year average import mix, 2019–2023 (TradeMap / Agrekon 2025), tonnes of product:

  • Urea ~875 000 t — ~40–42% of all fertilizer imports by value and volume over a decade.
  • Potassium chloride ~369 000 t
  • Ammonium sulphate ~286 000 t
  • MAP ~262 000 t
  • LAN ~119 000 t, CAN ~72 000 t, SOP ~67 000 t, DAP only ~11 000 t (MAP is the local P grade).

Nitrogen products were 56% of import value and 64% of import volume in that window. Volume grew only ~2% a year; value grew ~18% a year — a price story, not a volume explosion.

Partners. Saudi Arabia (urea, MAP), Russia (MAP, urea, KCl), Qatar, China, Germany and Oman are the standing list in the BFAP/SA Grain write-up. Those four plus China supplied 57% of import value in 2019–2023. World Bank WITS (HS chapter 31, 2025) puts the latest year at $1.09 billion, led by Russia ($363m), China ($167m), Saudi Arabia ($132m), Oman ($76m), Germany ($50m) and Qatar ($46m). Chile appears for SOP.

Ports. A PtX Hub / industry compilation of five-year average import tonnes: Durban 81%, Cape Town 15%, Richards Bay ~1%, with small residual volumes at Ngqura/Gqeberha and the Mananga (Eswatini) land border. Maydon Wharf is the physical centre of the urea and potash trade.

Exports are much smaller and mostly SADC: Zambia, Zimbabwe, Namibia, Botswana and Eswatini took 78% of export value in the BFAP window. Peak export value was $662 million in 2022 (price), while peak export volume was 2015. South Africa is the region’s blender and transshipper, not a bulk urea exporter.

Projections to the early 2030s

BFAP’s baseline (normal weather) is not a boom in fertilizer tonnes. The story is mix, not volume:

  • Nitrogen stays around 560 000 t. Cereal hectares ease slightly as marginal maize struggles against global prices; soybeans keep some of the area they already took, and soybeans do not need much bagged N.
  • Phosphorus and potassium have been the growth nutrients (P +6.3% a year and K +4.4% a year from 2004 to 2024). That pace slows as the soybean land grab matures, but horticulture (trees coming into bearing, especially macadamias) still lifts P and K demand.
  • Foskor is the swing factor on local P. A sustained recovery at Phalaborwa and Richards Bay cuts MAP imports; another Transnet or acid-plant outage does the opposite. IDC has already recorded a decade of sub-capacity MAP.
  • Green ammonia is the longer-dated option to delink N from imported gas and from Sasol’s coal-chemicals complex. Several coastal projects are scoped on the Green Hydrogen & Ammonia page. None of them is a urea plant in the ground today — until one reaches FID, urea remains an import.
  • Risks that cut use, not just prices: a dry El Niño season, a weak maize price, and rand weakness against dollar-priced urea. 2016 (57 kg/ha) is the recent drought print on the intensity chart.

Policy overlay: fertilizer is registered under the Fertilizers, Farm Feeds, Agricultural Remedies and Stock Remedies Act 36 of 1947. Fertasa is the industry association and runs the SARS import/export portal used by members. Smallholder support programmes create a second, government-tendered demand channel that Who Owns Whom flags as a growth kicker if it is actually funded.
