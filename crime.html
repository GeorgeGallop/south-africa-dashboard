🔒 Crime & Economic Sabotage

How Organised Crime, Theft, and Corruption Undermine South Africa's Infrastructure and Economy

South Africa faces a crisis of economic crime that goes far beyond street-level robbery. Organised syndicates are systematically stealing the country's infrastructure — copper from rail lines and power grids, steel from bridges and factories, cables from telecommunications networks. At the same time, state capture — the systematic looting of state-owned enterprises through corrupt procurement — drained hundreds of billions of rand from Eskom, Transnet, and other public institutions. These are not separate problems. They are connected manifestations of the same underlying weakness: the inability of the state to protect its own assets from organised theft, and the capture of state institutions by the very people who were supposed to guard them. Together, they constitute one of the largest drags on South Africa's economic growth.

The Three Faces of Economic Crime in South Africa

When people think of crime in South Africa, they typically think of violent street crime — muggings, carjackings, house robberies. But the most economically damaging crimes are different. They are less visible, less reported, and far more consequential for the country's long-term prosperity. They fall into three categories:

1. Infrastructure Theft

Organised syndicates systematically steal copper, steel, aluminium, and other metals from public infrastructure — rail catenary wires, Eskom transmission cables, Telkom copper lines, municipal water pipes, manhole covers, bridge railings. This is not opportunistic theft by individuals. It is industrial-scale organised crime, with supply chains that extend from the theft site to scrap metal dealers to export markets. The damage extends far beyond the replacement cost of the metal: a stolen section of rail overhead wire stops trains for days; a stolen transformer kills power to a factory for weeks; a stolen manhole cover costs a life when someone falls in.

2. State Capture & Procurement Fraud

From roughly 2013 to 2018, during the Zuma-Gupta era, state-owned enterprises were systematically looted through corrupt procurement. The Zondo Commission documented how Eskom's coal contracts were inflated by R16+ billion, how Transnet's locomotive tender (1,064 locomotives from CRRC/CSR) was inflated by billions more, and how politically connected intermediaries extracted kickbacks at every stage. The total cost is estimated at R500+ billion across all SOEs — not counting the secondary damage from plants that weren't maintained because the money was stolen, or trains that couldn't run because the procurement was corrupt rather than functional.

3. Illegal Mining & Resource Theft

South Africa has an enormous illegal mining economy — an estimated R7+ billion per year in gold alone. Abandoned mine shafts are taken over by armed syndicates (the "zama zamas") who extract gold, diamonds, chrome, and coal illegally. This is not artisanal poverty-driven mining; it is organised crime, often controlled by international syndicates with links to the formal gold trade. Illegal mining damages legitimate operations, pollutes water systems, funds other criminal activities, and represents a direct drain on the formal economy's tax base and export revenue.

How Infrastructure Theft Works — The Economics of Metal Crime

Infrastructure theft is not random vandalism. It is a rational, market-driven criminal enterprise that operates according to clear economic logic. Understanding that logic is essential to understanding why it is so hard to stop.

The Supply Chain of Stolen Metal

1

Theft

Organised crews with vehicles, tools, and sometimes insider knowledge strip metal from infrastructure — rail wires, power cables, transformers, pipes.

2

Intermediate Buyers

Small-scale scrap dealers buy the metal, often knowing it is stolen. They strip insulation, cut it into untraceable pieces, and sell it up the chain.

3

Scrap Consolidators

Larger dealers aggregate scrap metal from multiple sources. At this point, the metal is indistinguishable from legitimate scrap. Paperwork is often falsified.

4

Export

Much of the stolen metal is exported — primarily to China and India — as "scrap copper" or "recycled metal." Once it leaves South Africa, it is gone forever.

Why it follows the copper price: The global copper price is the single best predictor of infrastructure theft incidents in South Africa. When copper prices spike (as they did in 2021–2022, driven by the electric vehicle and renewable energy booms), theft incidents surge. When prices fall, theft declines — not because syndicates stop operating, but because the profit margin narrows and they shift to other metals (steel, aluminium). This price-sensitivity proves that infrastructure theft is not mindless destruction — it is a calculated business that responds to market incentives. Theft is most intense precisely when copper is most valuable, which is also when Transnet and Eskom can least afford to lose it.

The replacement cost multiplier: A metre of copper overhead wire might be worth R500–1,000 as scrap. But replacing it costs 5–10× that amount — you have to shut down the rail line, bring in specialised crews and equipment, tension the new wire to precise specifications, and test it before reopening. And that's assuming you catch the theft quickly. If a train hits a section where the wire has been cut, the damage to the locomotive's pantograph and the train's schedule can multiply the cost by orders of magnitude. The R2+ billion Transnet estimates for cable theft disruption is almost certainly an undercount, because it doesn't include the lost export revenue from trains that couldn't run.

State Capture: When the Guards Become the Thieves

Infrastructure theft steals physical assets. State capture steals the money that was supposed to maintain them. Together, they form a pincer movement that has left South Africa's public infrastructure hollowed out — physically stripped of metal by criminal syndicates, and financially stripped of maintenance budgets by corrupt insiders.

The Zondo Commission (2018–2022) documented how this worked at Eskom and Transnet:

  • Eskom coal contracts: Coal supply agreements were awarded to Gupta-linked companies at massively inflated prices — sometimes 2–3× the market rate. The overpayment was split between the politically connected supplier and the Eskom official who approved it. The Zondo Commission identified over R16 billion in irregular coal procurement. The coal itself was often of inferior quality, damaging the power stations and reducing their output.
  • Transnet locomotives: The 2014 contract for 1,064 locomotives from CRRC and CSR (Chinese state-owned manufacturers) was inflated by billions. Regiments Capital, a Gupta-linked advisory firm, was paid facilitation fees. The locomotives that were delivered — many of the 21E class electrics and various diesel units — were overweight, over-specified for South African conditions, and came with maintenance contracts that locked Transnet into expensive long-term obligations.
  • Governance collapse: The looting was not possible without the systematic weakening of governance. Boards were stacked with political appointees. Procurement rules were bypassed. Whistleblowers were silenced or fired. Independent oversight was neutralised. The result was that billions of rand that should have gone to maintaining power stations, rail lines, and port equipment instead went into private pockets — while the infrastructure that depended on that money decayed.
The connection between state capture and infrastructure theft: These two forms of economic crime feed each other. State capture stripped the maintenance budgets that would have repaired stolen infrastructure. Infrastructure theft makes the consequences of state capture more visible — when the wires are stolen and there's no money to replace them, the connection between corruption and service failure becomes undeniable. A country with strong governance might suffer from infrastructure theft but could fund repairs. A country with state capture suffers from both — the assets are stolen, and the money to replace them was stolen too.

What Is Being Stolen — A Sector-by-Sector Breakdown

⚡ Eskom / Electricity Grid

Transmission line conductors (copper and aluminium), substation copper busbars, transformer windings, overhead earth wires, and even the steel lattice towers (cut down for scrap). Eskom estimates it loses R2–3 billion per year to cable theft alone. The indirect cost — power outages, load shedding exacerbated by stolen infrastructure, factories that can't operate — is multiples higher. In some areas, entire substations have been stripped of copper, rendering them inoperable for months.

🚂 Transnet / Rail Network

Overhead catenary wire (copper contact wire and copper catenary wire), signalling cables, substation equipment, track components (steel rails, fishplates, bolts), and even steel bridge components. Transnet estimates R2+ billion per year in direct disruption costs. Thousands of kilometres of electrified line have been dieselised or abandoned due to wire theft. See the Cable Theft deep-dive for the full story.

📡 Telecoms / Telkom

Copper telephone lines — thousands of kilometres stolen from Telkom's legacy network. Telkom has largely abandoned copper in favour of fibre and wireless, partly because theft made the copper network uneconomical to maintain. Mobile network tower batteries are also a major target — thieves steal the backup batteries (lead-acid and lithium) from cell towers, causing network outages during load shedding when the towers rely on battery power.

🏗️ Municipal & Construction

Manhole covers, streetlight poles, water pipes (copper and brass fittings), aluminium window frames from schools and hospitals, copper roofing from public buildings, electrical wiring from housing developments under construction, guardrails and fencing from highways. The theft is so pervasive that many municipalities have replaced metal covers with concrete alternatives — which are less functional but not worth stealing.

Why It Persists — The Enablers

Given the staggering economic cost — R50+ billion per year from infrastructure theft alone, R500+ billion from state capture — why hasn't it been stopped? The answer lies in a web of interconnected failures:

Scrap Metal Regulation Is Broken

South Africa attempted to ban scrap metal exports in 2022 to stem the flow of stolen copper and steel. The ban was partially effective but faced legal challenges from the scrap metal industry (which includes both legitimate recyclers and criminal fronts). Enforcement is weak — it is easy to smuggle scrap metal across borders or to misdeclare exports as something else. Without effective controls on the scrap metal trade, there will always be a ready buyer for stolen infrastructure.

Law Enforcement Is Overwhelmed

The SAPS (South African Police Service) is under-resourced, under-trained, and often compromised. Dedicated units for infrastructure crime (like the former "Copperheads" unit) were established and then disbanded or starved of resources. Prosecution rates for infrastructure theft are extremely low — syndicates operate with near-impunity because the probability of being caught and convicted is negligible. The risk-reward ratio heavily favours crime.

Global Demand Is Insatiable

Copper is essential for electrification, renewable energy, and electric vehicles — global demand is projected to double by 2035. This drives prices higher and makes theft more profitable. South Africa is not unique in suffering infrastructure theft — it happens everywhere copper prices are high and governance is weak. But the combination of abundant copper infrastructure (one of the world's most electrified rail networks), weak enforcement, and high global prices makes South Africa an especially attractive target.

The Economic Impact — Beyond the Replacement Cost

The direct cost of stolen metal — R2 billion here, R3 billion there — understates the true economic damage by an order of magnitude. The real cost is in what the infrastructure enables, not what it costs to replace:

  • Lost exports: When cable theft shuts down the coal line to Richards Bay, the cost is not the stolen copper — it's the 76 million tonnes of coal that can't reach the port, the R50+ billion in lost export revenue, and the mining jobs that disappear because the product can't be shipped.
  • Factory closures: When a substation is stripped of copper and a factory loses power for weeks, the cost is not the transformer — it's the production that stops, the workers sent home, the contracts lost to competitors in other countries.
  • Investment deterrence: When international investors see that South Africa cannot protect its own rail lines from copper thieves, they ask a simple question: why would I invest capital here if the infrastructure it depends on can be stolen? This is the hardest cost to quantify but arguably the most damaging — the investment that never arrives because the risk of infrastructure failure is too high.
  • Public safety: Stolen manhole covers kill people. Stolen guardrails cause fatal accidents. Stolen signalling cables cause train collisions. These are not economic externalities — they are the direct human cost of a system that cannot protect its own infrastructure.
The way forward: No country has ever completely eliminated infrastructure theft, but countries have reduced it to manageable levels. The formula is well-understood: (1) regulate the scrap metal supply chain so that stolen metal cannot easily be sold, (2) invest in security — drones, cameras, private guards, community tip-lines, (3) use theft-resistant materials where possible — aluminium catenary wire instead of copper, concrete covers instead of metal, and (4) prosecute the organisers, not just the low-level thieves. South Africa has attempted all four, but none at sufficient scale. Until the probability of being caught, convicted, and imprisoned for infrastructure theft is high enough to deter organised syndicates, the theft will continue — because the economics of stealing copper remain overwhelmingly positive for the criminals.

Economic crime in South Africa is not a law-and-order problem — it is an economic problem with law-and-order consequences. The country's infrastructure was built on the assumption that the state could protect its assets. That assumption no longer holds. Fixing it requires not just more police, but a fundamental restructuring of the scrap metal market, the prosecution system, and the incentives that make infrastructure theft the most profitable crime in the country.
