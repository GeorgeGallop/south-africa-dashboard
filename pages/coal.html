Coal

Major energy source and export commodity
What is it?

Fossil fuel used primarily for electricity generation and steelmaking.

Where is it found?

Mpumalanga (Witbank, Highveld coalfields), Waterberg, and KwaZulu-Natal coalfields.

What is it used for?

~77% of South Africa's energy needs; ~95% of national electricity generation via Eskom; steel production, chemicals, and liquid fuel synthesis (Sasol).

Supply Chain

Mined (open-pit & underground) → Transported by rail (Transnet) → Power stations or exported via Richards Bay.

History of South African Coal

Early Coal Use (Iron Age - 1869)

Coal's use in southern Africa dates back to the Iron Age (300–1880 AD), when charcoal (charred wood, not true coal) was used to smelt iron and copper. However, large-scale commercial coal mining did not begin until the mid-19th century following the discovery of significant coal deposits. The vast coal reserves found in the Karoo Supergroup's Ecca deposits (formed during the Permian period, 280-250 million years ago) would eventually become one of the world's largest coal reserves, containing over a third of all coal reserves in the Southern Hemisphere.

The Eskom Era Begins (1923-1960s)

The systematic development of South Africa's coal industry paralleled the growth of the electricity sector. In 1923, the government established the Electricity Supply Commission (ESCOM), later known as Eskom, under the leadership of Hendrik van der Bijl. Eskom's Electricity Act required that it could only sell electricity at cost and was exempted from tax, enabling rapid infrastructure expansion.

Eskom completed its first coal-fired power stations in 1928: the Congella Power Station in Durban and the Salt River Power Station in Cape Town. In 1935, the Witbank power station (128 MW) opened in partnership with the Victoria Falls and Transvaal Power Company, specifically to provide power to the mining industry. By 1948, Eskom bought out the Victoria Falls and Transvaal Power Company for £14.5 million (equivalent to £2.55 billion in 2017), consolidating its monopoly over electricity generation.

Rapid Expansion (1960-1990): The "Six-Pack" Era

From 1960 to 1990, Eskom embarked on an aggressive expansion program to fuel South Africa's rapid economic growth. The company's installed capacity ballooned from just 4,000 MW to 40,000 MW over this three-decade period. In parallel, Eskom built a nationwide 400 kV high-voltage transmission network to distribute coal-generated power across the country.

During the 1970s and 1980s, Eskom constructed a standardized fleet of large "six-pack" coal-fired power stations (so named for their six large generator units each). These stations achieved remarkably low electricity generation costs through economies of scale. Key plants included:

  • Arnot Power Station (Mpumalanga, 1975): 2,352 MW
  • Duvha Power Station (1980-1984): 3,600 MW
  • Kriel Power Station (1976-1979): 3,000 MW
  • Matla Power Station (1979-1983): 3,600 MW
  • Lethabo Power Station (1985-1990): 3,708 MW
  • Tutuka Power Station (1985-1990): 3,654 MW
  • Kendal Power Station (1988-1992): 4,116 MW (world's largest coal-fired plant at the time)

This period also saw Eskom construct the Koeberg Nuclear Power Station (1984, 1,860 MW) in the Western Cape, partly to provide alternative generation capacity and support South Africa's nuclear program.

Coal's Economic Dominance (1995-2015)

By 1995, South Africa had become a global coal powerhouse, producing over 255 million tonnes annually. The industry employed 135,000 workers at its 1981 peak, though this declined to 80,000 by 2015 due to mechanization. The five largest coal mining companies—Anglo American, South32, Sasol Mining, Glencore Xstrata, and Exxaro—accounted for approximately 85% of all production.

Coal became deeply embedded in South Africa's economy: in 2004, the coal mining industry generated R39 billion in gross income and directly employed 50,000 people. By the 2000s, coal represented approximately 77% of South Africa's energy needs, with 92% of all coal consumed on the African continent sourced from South Africa. The country emerged as the 5th largest exporter of coal globally, with 30% of its production exported (primarily via Richards Bay).

Sasol and Synthetic Fuels (1950s-Present)

A unique aspect of South Africa's coal use is its conversion into liquid fuels. Sasol Mining pioneered coal-to-liquid (CTL) technology, operating the Secunda CTL facility which converts coal into synthetic fuels. By the 2000s, approximately 35% of South Africa's liquid fuel supply was derived from coal through this process—a critical technology during the apartheid era's international fuel embargo.

The Crisis Era Begins (2007-Present)

In 2007, South Africa faced its first major electricity supply crisis. Demand exceeded coal-fired generation capacity, forcing Eskom to introduce load shedding (rolling blackouts). This was a watershed moment: former President Thabo Mbeki later admitted in December 2007 that the government's decision to deny Eskom's budget requests in the late 1990s during the attempted privatization was a strategic error that destabilized the economy.

Medupi and Kusile: The Curse of Cost Overruns (2007-2025)

In response to the 2007 crisis, Eskom launched construction of two massive new "six-pack" coal stations: Medupi (Limpopo, 4,764 MW) and Kusile (Mpumalanga, 4,800 MW). These projects were meant to be completed within a decade but have become synonymous with South African infrastructure failure.

The projects have been plagued by:

  • Massive corruption: R139 billion (US$9.13 billion) allegedly stolen by 11 contractors during construction
  • State capture: The Gupta family secured coal supply contracts worth R11.7 billion (2014-2017) through improper channels
  • Consultant overcharging: Gupta-owned Trillian Capital Partners and McKinsey made R1.6 billion in questionable fees
  • Extreme delays: Originally scheduled for completion by 2015-2019, Medupi only came online in 2023 and Kusile remains incomplete
  • Ballooning costs: Original R233 billion budget exceeded by multiples due to construction delays and rework

Finance Minister Pravin Gordhan stated in 2020 that corruption and cost overruns at Medupi and Kusile caused a fourfold increase in electricity prices—a catastrophic impact on South African consumers.

Renewable Energy Transition and Climate Agreements (2021-Present)

At the COP26 Climate Conference in Glasgow (November 2021), South Africa announced a major pivot: a deal worth $8.5 billion in climate finance from developed nations to accelerate its transition away from coal-dependent electricity generation. The country committed to a Just Transition program to support coal mining communities and workers during the shift to renewables.

However, execution has been slow. Eskom's Integrated Resource Plan calls for 6,000 MW of new solar PV capacity and 14,400 MW of new wind power by 2030. As of 2025, Eskom is planning pilot renewable energy facilities and green hydrogen production—signals of a long-term shift away from coal, though the transition faces resistance from mining unions (NUM and Solidarity) concerned about job losses.

The Health and Environmental Cost

Coal power's human cost is significant. A 2017 study estimated that over 2,000 South Africans die annually from health conditions caused by Eskom's coal-fired power plants (Eskom disputed this, claiming only 330 deaths in 2023). A 2023 study focusing only on PM2.5 particulates estimated 800 deaths per year.

Eskom is the world's largest emitter of sulfur dioxide in the power industry, releasing 1.6 million tons into the air in 2019. The company emits 42% of South Africa's total greenhouse gas emissions and generates approximately 45% of all electricity consumed on the African continent—making it a critical energy source but also a major climate and health liability.

Mpumalanga Province, home to most coal-fired power stations, has been identified as having "the dirtiest air in the world" in some years due to combined emissions from coal power plants and Sasol's synthetic fuel facilities.

Load Shedding Crisis (2019-2024)

The failure to complete Medupi and Kusile on time, combined with aging coal plant breakdowns and maintenance backlogs, led to severe rolling blackouts starting in 2019. Load shedding reached Stage 6 in December 2019 (the highest level reached at that time), with plans to escalate further. During the worst periods in 2023, South Africa experienced Stage 6 blackouts during winter, with daily power cuts lasting up to 12 hours—contributing an estimated 2% loss to the country's GDP.

Former Eskom CEO André de Ruyter reported that Eskom was losing roughly R1 billion (US$53.9 million) per month due to theft and corruption, attributing much of the power crisis to criminal syndicates operating within the utility. He also alleged involvement of senior ANC politicians in the theft, eventually leading to his resignation in February 2023 under pressure.

The Future: Just Transition or Coal Persistence?

South Africa faces a critical juncture. The COP26 agreement commits the country to moving away from coal, with climate finance supporting renewable energy investments. However, the pace of change is uncertain given:

  • Resistance from unions (NUM, Solidarity) protecting coal mining jobs
  • Aging coal plant infrastructure requiring replacement
  • Eskom's severe financial distress limiting investment in renewables
  • Political will to truly decarbonize facing competing priorities
  • Renewable energy infrastructure still being built (solar and wind are intermittent)

By 2025, South Africa remained heavily dependent on coal despite international climate commitments. The Just Transition concept— supporting mining communities economically during the shift away from coal—remains more rhetoric than reality for most coal-dependent regions like Lephalale and Witbank.
