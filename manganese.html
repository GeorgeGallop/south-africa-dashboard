History of South African Manganese

Discovery and Early Chemistry (1774-1830s)

Manganese was first isolated as a distinct element in 1774 by Swedish chemist Johan Gottlieb Gahn, who reduced pyrolusite (manganese dioxide) with charcoal. But it took decades before its crucial role in steelmaking was understood. In the 1830s, metallurgists discovered that adding manganese to molten steel removed sulfur and oxygen impurities, dramatically improving steel quality. This single application — manganese as a steel purifier — would eventually drive 90% of global demand and make large deposits strategically priceless.

The Kalahari Giant (1875-1920s)

Manganese deposits were first reported in South Africa's Northern Cape in 1875, but the scale of the find only became apparent in the early 1900s. What became known as the Kalahari Manganese Field — centred near the town of Hotazel in the Northern Cape — proved to be the largest manganese deposit on Earth. In the 1920s, SAMANCOR (South African Manganese Corporation) began the first commercial mining operations, exporting ore through Port Elizabeth to global steel mills.

Post-War Expansion (1950s-1980)

The post-World War II steel boom drove rapid expansion of South Africa's manganese sector. Production grew from under 1 million tonnes in the 1950s to around 5 million tonnes by 1980. Assmang joined SAMANCOR as a major producer, developing the Black Rock mine. By 1980, South Africa had become the world's largest manganese exporter. The Sishen-Saldanha railway line — built primarily for iron ore in the 1970s — also benefited manganese exports, providing bulk rail capacity to the deep-water port at Saldanha Bay.

Post-Apartheid Liberalisation (1990s-2000s)

The end of apartheid and South Africa's re-integration into the global economy sparked a new phase of expansion. BHP Billiton acquired a major stake in SAMANCOR in 2003, and in 2013 South32 was spun off from BHP to take over its manganese and other assets. South Africa remained consistently the world's largest producer through this period, with production reaching ~7.5 million tonnes by the early 2020s.

The EV Battery Opportunity (2016-Present)

The most significant development for manganese's future is the rise of electric vehicle batteries. Lithium-manganese-oxide (LMO) and lithium manganese iron phosphate (LMFP) cathodes are gaining traction as cheaper alternatives to the nickel-rich chemistries. Manganese is abundant, cheap, and improves battery safety and lifespan. This has opened a potential second market for manganese beyond steel — high-purity manganese sulphate for battery manufacturing. South Africa, with its vast reserves, is well-positioned to supply the battery-grade manganese the energy transition requires.

Infrastructure Constraints (2017-2024)

The story of recent manganese production is as much about logistics as geology. Transnet's rail underperformance has been a persistent constraint: locomotive shortages, theft of infrastructure, and maintenance backlogs on the Sishen-Saldanha and other lines have limited export capacity. At various points, millions of tonnes of manganese have sat stockpiled at mines because trains could not carry ore to ports. This has cost the sector billions of rands in lost revenue and forced mining companies to consider private rail alternatives — a significant shift in a sector long dependent on state-owned logistics.

The Kalahari Manganese Field

The Kalahari Manganese Field stretches for over 50 kilometres along the Mamatwan-Varsteekloof axis in the Northern Cape. It contains more than 200 million tonnes of reserves spread across several major mines: Mamatwan, Wessels, Nchwaning, Gloria, and Kuduman. The ore is notably high-grade, averaging 37-48% manganese content, making South African ore among the best in the world. The field was formed by sedimentary processes in the ancient Kalahari basin, with manganese precipitated out of ancient seawater in the presence of oxygen-producing cyanobacteria ~2.2 billion years ago.

The Future: Steel Staple with a Battery Twist

Manganese's future is more exciting than its past suggests. While steel production will remain the dominant market for decades, the battery opportunity could transform the economics of the sector. If LMFP and other high-manganese cathode chemistries capture significant EV market share, demand for high-purity manganese could grow substantially faster than steel-linked demand. South Africa's challenge is building the processing capacity to produce battery-grade manganese sulphate rather than simply exporting raw ore — capturing more value within the country.
