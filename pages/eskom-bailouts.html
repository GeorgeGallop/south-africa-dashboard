💰 Eskom Bailouts

A history of government bailouts, the cost to consumers, and the fraud that made them necessary
💬 The Bottom Line

Eskom has received over R500 billion in government bailouts, debt assumptions, and equity injections since 2008 — and this number continues to grow. This money comes from South African taxpayers and electricity consumers, not from some abstract government fund. Every time Eskom gets a bailout, it means higher tariffs, higher taxes, or more government debt that future generations must repay. Many of the problems that created the need for bailouts — inflated coal contracts, state capture looting, vandalism by organised crime, political appointments of unqualified executives — amount to what a reasonable observer would call blatant theft from the public purse.

⏱ Timeline of Major Bailouts & Financial Support (2008–2025)
2008–2009 — R60bn Government Loan

The first major post-apartheid bailout. The government provided R60bn in subordinated loans to Eskom to fund the Medupi and Kusile build programme. The conditions were weak, and the loans were structured so they could be converted to equity at the government's discretion — effectively writing them off.

2010–2013 — R350bn Loan Guarantee Programme

The government announced a massive R350bn loan guarantee programme for Eskom to cover its capital expenditure programme. By 2013, R186bn had been drawn. The guarantees allowed Eskom to borrow from banks and development finance institutions at lower interest rates because the government backstopped the debt.

2015 — R23bn Eskom Pension Fund Restructuring

The government changed the rules for the Eskom Pension Fund, allowing Eskom to reduce its contributions. This was effectively a bailout worth R23bn over the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework (MTEF), shifting the pension liability into the future.

2017 — R5bn Allocation (Disaster Management)

Under the guise of disaster management (a constitutional mechanism for emergency spending), the government allocated R5bn to Eskom to prevent a complete financial collapse. This was widely criticised as a misuse of the disaster management framework.

2019 — R59bn Eskom Debt Relief (Year 1)

Finance Minister Tito Mboweni announced a R59bn bailout over two years (R26bn in FY2019/20, R33bn in FY2020/21) to keep Eskom solvent. Conditions included the appointment of a Chief Restructuring Officer and progress on unbundling. The money was largely consumed by interest payments.

2020 — R56bn Special Appropriation (COVID & Debt)

An additional R56bn was allocated to Eskom through a Special Appropriation Bill, on top of the R59bn already committed. Total government support to Eskom by 2020 had reached approximately R200bn.

2023 — R254bn Debt Relief Package (THE BIG ONE)

In the 2023 Budget, Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana announced a R254bn debt relief package over three years (FY2024–2026). This is the largest single bailout in South African history. The package works as follows:

  • R78bn in FY2024/25 (direct takeover of Eskom debt)
  • R84bn in FY2025/26
  • R92bn in FY2026/27
  • Total: R254bn
  • Conditions: strict operational reforms, EAF targets, cost reduction, unbundling milestones, anti-corruption measures
2024 — Further R30–50bn Contingency

In the 2024 Medium-Term Budget Policy Statement (MTBPS), Treasury signalled that Eskom may need additional support beyond the R254bn if the utility fails to meet its operational targets. The Municipal Arrears (R74bn owed to Eskom) also represent an indirect bailout — if municipalities cannot pay, the national government ultimately covers the shortfall through the municipal debt restructuring programme.

Total estimated government support to Eskom (2008–2026): R500bn+ — equivalent to approximately R8,000 per person for every man, woman, and child in South Africa.
🔎 Where Did the Bailout Money Go?

The common question is: if Eskom has received R500bn+ in bailouts, why is the utility still in crisis? The answer lies in where the money actually went:

CategoryEstimated AmountExplanation
Debt interest payments ~R300bn+ Eskom's debt of R400bn+ carries interest rates of 8–12%. Interest payments alone have consumed roughly R40–60bn per year. A large fraction of every bailout went straight to bondholders (banks, pension funds, foreign investors) — not to fixing power stations.
Medupi & Kusile cost overruns ~R300bn The two mega-stations were originally budgeted at R160bn combined. The final cost exceeded R450bn — a cost overrun of R290bn+. This is the single biggest cause of Eskom's debt. Poor project management, design defects, and contractor fraud drove the overruns.
Inflated coal contracts (state capture) ~R20–30bn The Zondo Commission found that coal contracts were awarded to Gupta-linked companies at prices 2–3x the market rate. Tegeta Exploration and Others, owned by the Gupta family, secured multi-billion-rand contracts for low-quality coal that damaged Eskom's boilers — causing further breakdowns.
Diesel for OCGTs ~R30bn+ (2022–2024) Eskom burned diesel at Ankerlig and Gourikwa open-cycle gas turbines at a staggering rate to keep the lights on during load shedding. Diesel costs exceeded R12bn in 2023 alone — roughly 5% of Eskom's total revenue. This diesel was necessary because coal stations were broken.
Maintenance backlog (deferred) ~R100bn+ Maintenance was deferred for years to hide financial problems. By 2023, the backlog had grown to an estimated R100bn+. Each year of further deferral makes it more expensive to fix — boilers, mills, and turbines degrade irreversibly if not maintained.
Franchise & corruption losses ~R5–10bn/yr (ongoing) Coal stockpile theft, diesel theft, cable theft, meter tampering, and illegal connections cost Eskom billions annually. An estimated R5–10bn is lost per year to criminal activity within and around the utility.
📜 The Fraud Question: Who Is Responsible?
⚠️ Caveat: The following section summarises findings from the Zondo Commission (State Capture Inquiry), the Auditor-General's reports, and investigative journalism by AmaBhungane, Daily Maverick, and News24. Allegations are backed by sworn testimony and court documents, but not all have resulted in criminal convictions.
📊 The Zondo Commission Findings (State Capture)

The Judicial Commission of Inquiry into Allegations of State Capture (the Zondo Commission) documented systematic looting of Eskom between 2013 and 2018. Key findings:

  • Coal contract manipulation: Gupta-linked companies (Tegeta, Mxgen, Tirisano) were awarded multi-billion-rand coal supply contracts through a rigged procurement process. Eskom paid above-market prices for low-grade coal that contained high ash content, damaging boiler tubes and causing forced outages.
  • Medupi & Kusile contractor fraud: Contracts worth tens of billions were awarded to politically connected companies with little or no experience in power plant construction. Allegations of bribery, kickbacks, and inflated invoices were documented.
  • Executive capture: The appointment of senior executives (CEOs, CFOs, board members) was controlled by the Gupta family through their relationship with President Jacob Zuma. Competent engineers were replaced by loyalists. Brian Molefe, Matshela Koko, Anoj Singh, and others were named in the commission's reports.
  • Diesel procurement fraud: Diesel contracts for the OCGTs were inflated through intermediaries. Eskom paid R4bn for diesel that should have cost R2bn.
🔎 Beyond State Capture: Ongoing Issues

State capture may have been the most spectacular form of looting, but it is not the only one. Even with a new board and management team (appointed 2023), Eskom continues to bleed money from:

  • Coal theft: Organised criminal syndicates steal coal from Eskom's stockpiles and sell it to local markets and even back to Eskom itself. Trucks with forged waybills leave Eskom premises with "coal" that is never delivered.
  • Diesel theft: Drivers of diesel tankers delivering to OCGT stations have been arrested for selling diesel on the black market while filing false delivery reports.
  • Cable theft: Thousands of kilometres of copper cable are stolen from Eskom's distribution network every year, causing outages and costing R2bn+ annually to replace.
  • Meter tampering & illegal connections: An estimated 10–15% of electricity in South Africa is not paid for — lost to bypassed meters, illegal connections, and non-payment by municipalities.
  • Municipal non-payment: Municipalities collectively owe Eskom ~R74bn (2024). Many are effectively bankrupt and have no intention of paying. The consumer ultimately covers this through higher tariffs.
🏷 The Consumer: The One Who Pays

Here is the mechanism by which the burden falls on households and businesses:

  1. Higher tariffs: Eskom applies to NERSA for tariff increases. NERSA approves increases of 12–18% per year (far above inflation). These increases are necessary because Eskom's cost base is bloated by fraud, inefficiency, and debt service.
  2. Government debt: The R254bn debt relief does not come from thin air. It is added to the national debt, which must be repaid through future taxes — meaning every South African taxpayer is paying for Eskom's bailout.
  3. Load shedding costs: When Eskom fails to supply electricity, the economy contracts. GDP losses from load shedding are estimated at R500bn+ over 2022–2024. This is an indirect cost borne by every business and worker in the country.
  4. Self-generation costs: Households and businesses that can afford it have invested in rooftop solar, batteries, and generators — at their own expense. This creates a two-tier system where the wealthy can escape Eskom's failures while the poor cannot.

⚖️ The Debate: Bailout vs. Blatant Fraud?
❌ The “Bailout = Subsidising Fraud” Argument

Every bailout reduces the incentive for accountability. Eskom knows the government cannot let it fail — so there is no real consequence for mismanagement. The money that should have been spent on maintaining power stations was stolen through inflated contracts, and the bailout repays that theft — not with the perpetrators' money, but with the public's money. This is a textbook case of privatising profits and socialising losses — except Eskom never had profits to privatise. NERSA-approved tariff increases mean consumers pay more for less reliable electricity. By any reasonable definition, transferring hundreds of billions from impoverished households to politically connected contractors and incompetent management is blatant theft from the public.

✅ The “Bailout Is Necessary” Counter-Argument

Eskom is too big to fail — literally. If Eskom defaults on its debt, South Africa's sovereign credit rating would collapse, banks that hold Eskom bonds would face a crisis, and the entire economy would suffer. The bailout comes with conditions (the R254bn package has strict reform requirements) and is structured as debt relief, not a blank cheque. The alternative — letting Eskom default — would be far more expensive for the economy (estimated GDP loss of R1 trillion+). Unbundling Eskom into separate entities (GenCo, Transmission, Distribution) will create transparency and make future bailouts less likely. The fraud perpetrators should be prosecuted individually, but punishing the entire economy by refusing to bail out Eskom is counterproductive.
