Alloying, Steel & Ferroalloy Production

Stage 5 of the process chain — combining refined metals into steel, stainless steel and the ferroalloys that give engineered materials their strength, corrosion resistance and toughness.

What alloying is

Alloying is the final processing stage: mixing a base metal — usually iron — with controlled amounts of other elements to give it properties it does not have on its own. Adding carbon makes steel; adding chromium makes stainless steel; adding manganese, vanadium or niobium produces high-strength and wear-resistant grades. Ferroalloys are the master alloys through which many of these elements are safely and efficiently added to a steel melt.

This is where the minerals value chain meets manufacturing: the refined metals from stages 1–4 become the flat steel, long steel, stainless steel and specialty alloys that supply construction, automotive, mining equipment and export markets.

Core alloying and steelmaking steps
  • Ironmaking: iron ore is reduced in a blast furnace or direct-reduction plant into hot metal or DRI.
  • Primary steelmaking: hot metal is converted to steel in a basic oxygen furnace (BOF), or scrap is melted in an electric arc furnace (EAF).
  • Ladle metallurgy: alloying elements are added and the melt is refined to hit the exact chemistry and cleanliness of the target grade.
  • Ferroalloy addition: ferrochrome, ferromanganese, silicomanganese, ferrovanadium and ferrosilicon deliver the alloying elements into the melt.
  • Casting: the alloy is continuously cast into slabs, blooms or billets.
  • Rolling and finishing: cast product is rolled, heat-treated, coated or drawn into finished long and flat products.
The main alloy families
Family Key alloying elements Properties Typical uses
Carbon steel Carbon, manganese Strong, weldable, inexpensive Construction, rail, pipe, plate
High-strength low-alloy (HSLA) Vanadium, niobium, titanium Higher strength at lower weight Rail, automotive, pressure vessels
Stainless steel Chromium (>10.5%), nickel Corrosion and heat resistant Kitchenware, chemical plant, architecture
Ferromanganese / silicomanganese Manganese, silicon, carbon Deoxidiser and alloying agent for steel Almost all steel grades
Ferrochrome Chromium, iron, carbon Corrosion resistance, hardening Stainless steel production
Ferrovanadium Vanadium, iron Strength and toughness HSLA structural and rail steel
Ferroalloys — South Africa's specialty

South Africa is one of the world's leading producers of ferroalloys because it holds the raw ore and historically had cheap electricity. Submerged-arc furnaces smelt chromite into ferrochrome, manganese ore into ferromanganese and silicomanganese, and vanadium-bearing magnetite into ferrovanadium.

This segment is the most electricity-intensive part of the steel chain, and it is acutely exposed to Eskom tariffs. In recent years many ferrochrome and ferromanganese furnaces have been idled or placed on care and maintenance, with some restarting only under special electricity-pricing agreements.

The South African steel and alloy industry
  • ArcelorMittal South Africa — the dominant integrated steelmaker: blast furnaces and basic oxygen steelmaking at Vanderbijlpark and Newcastle, producing flat and long steel.
  • Columbus Stainless (Middelburg) — Africa's only integrated stainless steel producer.
  • Scaw Metals, Cape Gate, Unica, SA Steel Mills and other mini-mills — electric-arc and rerolling operations that feed construction steel, wire rod, grinding media and rail products.
  • Samancor Chrome, Glencore–Merafe, Assmang and Transalloys — the ferrochrome and ferromanganese producers of the Bushveld.
  • Bushveld Minerals (Vametco) — vanadium alloys for steel and flow batteries.
Why alloying matters

Alloying is where the value chain becomes finished, tradable industrial material. It multiplies the value of the metal several times over and is the foundation of downstream manufacturing — automotive, construction, mining equipment, appliances, packaging and engineering.

It is also the most contested part of the chain: South Africa has long argued that exporting raw ore while importing finished steel is a value loss, which is why local beneficiation, export taxes on raw ore, and electricity pricing for smelters are recurring policy battlegrounds.
