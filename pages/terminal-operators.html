⚓ Terminal Operators

Who Runs South Africa's Ports & LNG Terminals — and the Fight Over the Landlord Port Model & Private Participation

South Africa's ports are in crisis, and terminal operations are at the centre of it. The state-owned Transnet Port Terminals (TPT) operates the country's cargo-handling terminals while Transnet National Ports Authority (TNPA) acts as the "landlord" that owns the infrastructure. But as performance collapses, the Democratic Alliance (DA), business, and even Transnet itself are pushing to open terminal operations to private participation under the landlord port model — the same model that is being applied to the LNG terminals being built to import gas. This page maps who is involved, how they connect to the LNG build-out, how NERSA backs third-party access to gas infrastructure, and why the DA insists the ports must open up.

Ports Authority: TNPA (landlord)
Terminal Operator: TPT (state) → ICTSI, DP World, APM (private)
Key LNG sites: Saldanha Bay, Ngqura, Richards Bay, Durban
Gas Regulator: NERSA (Gas Act 2001)
Key push: Landlord port model + private participation

Who Is Involved — The Cast of Terminal Operations

Terminal operations were historically a closed state monopoly. That is breaking apart.

🏗️ TNPA — The Landlord

Transnet National Ports Authority owns and manages the physical port infrastructure — channels, breakwaters, berths, quays, navigational aids. It does not handle cargo itself. Under the landlord model, TNPA is the "real estate owner" that leases terminal operations to operators. It is also the site planner and marine-safety authority for new infrastructure like LNG berths.

📦 TPT — The State Operator

Transnet Port Terminals is the state-owned cargo-handling operator — containers, bulk commodities, automotive, break-bulk. It holds the terminal monopoly inside the ports. Its bottlenecks (especially at Durban) have caused shipping delays, demurrage costs and ruined export competitiveness. This is the terminal monopoly the DA wants broken open.

🌍 ICTSI — the Private Entrant

International Container Terminal Services Inc (Philippines-based, one of the world's largest terminal operators) partnered with Transnet to develop and manage the Durban Container Terminal. The DA welcomed this as aligned with its policy of "opening up the SOE sector to private sector investment."

🚢 DP World & APM Terminals

Two of the world's largest terminal operators are engaged in port terminal concessions that would give them control of container and bulk handling at Durban, Ngqura and other ports — breaking TPT's terminal monopoly from the ports side, mirroring how private rail operators are entering the rail market.

The LNG Terminal Operators — Where Gas Meets the Ports

The LNG import terminals being built as part of the Gas Master Plan are essentially a new class of port terminal — and they bring a fresh set of operators, developers and offtakers. Because LNG terminals are licensed and tariffed by NERSA (as gas regulator), they sit at the intersection of the port reform and gas reform stories.

Karpowership (FSRU)

Turkish powership and FSRU developer in active discussions for FSRU-based gas-to-power, including at Richards Bay. FSRUs are the fast-track way to get LNG into the grid without a full onshore terminal.

Golar LNG (FSRU)

Leading global FSRU operator with vessels that could be redeployed to South African waters on medium-term charters — the same model used in many emerging gas markets.

TotalEnergies & Shell

The international majors with the most South African gas interest. TotalEnergies is active in Mozambique's Rovuma LNG and could supply LNG while participating in terminal and power plant development; Shell has global LNG trading capability.

Transnet Pipelines & TNPA

Transnet Pipelines is a potential operator/partner for the gas pipeline network; TNPA provides the jetty, berthing, dredging and marine infrastructure for LNG carriers — the port services any LNG terminal depends on.

DMRE / iGas / PetroSA

The Department of Mineral Resources & Energy sets the gas policy (Gas Master Plan); iGas and PetroSA are state entities involved in gas infrastructure planning and potential equity participation in terminals.

NERSA — the Gas Regulator

Licences LNG operations (regasification, storage, transmission, trading), sets gas tariffs, approves import/export applications, and enforces third-party access to gas infrastructure — the regulatory backbone for any LNG terminal.

The Landlord Port Model — Explained

This is the model the DA is pushing, and it is already being piloted. Under it, the state keeps ownership of strategic infrastructure while private operators run the terminals.

1

The State Remains Landlord

TNPA (or the corporatised Ports Authority) retains ownership of the port infrastructure — channels, quays, berths, breakwaters. This is the "strategic infrastructure" that stays in state hands.

2

Private Operators Run Terminals

Private terminal operators (ICTSI, DP World, APM, or consortia) lease terminal operations under long-term concessions — bringing capital, skills and competitive pressure. They pay a concession fee to the landlord.

3

An Independent Regulator Sets Rules

The Transport Economic Regulator (under the Economic Regulation of Transport Act 2024) sets tariffs, enforces non-discriminatory access, and holds both the landlord and operators accountable. The DA wants this Regulator empowered with binding tariff-setting powers, independent of Transnet.

"Private participation under the landlord model keeps strategic infrastructure in state hands while introducing competition, capital and skills that terminal operations badly need. It is a practical model used successfully across Europe and Latin America."

— Democratic Alliance, August 2026, "Government must now open ports to greater private participation"

The DA's concrete demands (August 2026)

In its statement on the launch of Phase 3 of the Government-Business Partnership, the DA argued the government "must now turn this acknowledgement into action" and deliver — not repeat — reforms. Concretely, the DA demanded government must:

  • Fast-track the landlord port model — scale it from the Cape Town Multipurpose Terminal's 25-year concession to Durban's Pier 1, the Ngqura container terminal and Richards Bay.
  • Finalise TNPA's structural and financial separation from Transnet Port Terminals — "promised since 2021 but still incomplete."
  • Empower the Ports Regulator with binding tariff-setting powers, independent of Transnet.
  • Publish quarterly, terminal-by-terminal KPIs — vessel turnaround time, gross crane moves per hour, and truck turnaround time — so importers, exporters, farmers and miners can hold operators accountable.

The DA noted President Ramaphosa had acknowledged that "the decline in rail and port performance has been arrested" and that freight volumes are recovering — but that "recovery off a low base is not competitiveness."

How NERSA Backs Third-Party Access to LNG / Gas Infrastructure

The same open-access principle being applied to rail and ports is already embedded in the Gas Act — and enforced by NERSA.

🔓 Third-Party Access to Gas Infrastructure (Gas Act s.21)

The Gas Act, 2001 requires the Gas Regulator (now NERSA) to impose licence conditions that give:

  • Third-party access on commercially reasonable terms to uncommitted capacity in transmission pipelines and storage facilities.
  • Interconnection with the facilities of suppliers, transmitters, storage companies, distributors, reticulators and eligible customers where technically feasible.
  • No cross-subsidisation — vertically integrated companies must keep separate accounts for transmission, storage, distribution, trading, liquefaction and re-gasification.
⚖️ NERSA as Gas Regulator (Gas Act s.4)

NERSA's functions under the Act include issuing licences for gas transmission, storage, distribution, liquefaction and re-gasification (i.e. LNG terminals), and trading; it:

  • Regulates prices and monitors/approves transmission & storage tariffs, taking action to ensure they are applied non-discriminatorily.
  • Promotes competition in the gas industry.
  • Approves gas import and export applications — the gateway for LNG.
What this means for LNG terminals

Under the Gas Act, any LNG regasification terminal, storage facility or pipeline is licensed by NERSA, and its operator must allow third parties access to uncommitted capacity on commercially reasonable terms. This is the gas-sector equivalent of the rail/port open-access reform — it prevents a single LNG terminal owner from monopolising imports, and it mirrors the third-party access provisions the government is building into rail (TRIM) and ports (the landlord model). NERSA's Gas Licensing Framework is under active update precisely because LNG import infrastructure (Richards Bay, Coega/Ngqura, Saldanha) and gas-to-power licences are being evaluated. See NERSA and Gas Act.

The LNG Assets Being Built — Where Terminal Operators Fit

The Gas Master Plan (2024) identifies LNG import and regasification as the near-term anchor of the gas strategy. The terminal operators engaged with these assets are exactly the same private participants the DA wants in the ports.

These documents together form a consistent reform architecture across rail, ports and gas: keep the strategic infrastructure in public ownership, open its use to competitive private operators, and regulate access through an independent, statutorily empowered body.

Why it all fits together

The DA's landlord port model and NERSA's third-party access to gas infrastructure are the same principle applied to two different network industries. In ports, TNPA stays the landlord and private operators (ICTSI, DP World, APM) run terminals under a Transport Economic Regulator. In gas, the state retains the licensing framework while NERSA forces LNG terminal owners to share uncommitted capacity with third parties. Both are built on the idea that ownership of a strategic asset need not mean a monopoly over its use. The LNG build-out — Saldanha, Ngqura, Richards Bay — is the first place where the port reform and the gas reform genuinely collide, and it is exactly where the landlord model and NERSA's access rules will be tested together.
