A) Background — From CEGB Monopoly to the Pool

Before 1990, electricity in England and Wales was supplied by the state-owned Central Electricity Generating Board (CEGB) — a fully vertically-integrated monopoly that owned all large generation, the national grid, and coordinated despatch. The Electricity Act 1989 privatised the industry and created the Electricity Pool of England and Wales, which began trading on 31 March/1 April 1990.

The Pool was a mandatory, centralised, gross-pool wholesale market. All generators above a de minimis threshold had to sell all output through the Pool, and all suppliers bought from it. A central scheduler (the National Grid Company) ran a day-ahead merit-order optimisation over 48 half-hour settlement periods per day. The design was heavily influential — it is the direct intellectual ancestor of the single-Pricing-Area, SMP-cleared design adopted by SAWEM in South Africa, and a central case study in wholesale electricity market design.

Why it matters for the thesis: the Pool was the first live laboratory for the questions at the heart of the South African market design — whether a single marginal price can discipline a dominant incumbent, how capacity-based revenues should be layered on energy payments, and what happens when market structure and market rules interact. Its successes (entry, falling costs) and failures (duopoly market power, opaque pool prices) directly shaped the NETA/BETTA successor design.

B) How Single Marginal Pricing Worked

The Pool price was built from a pay-as-clear marginal price plus two add-ons. Each day, generators submitted multi-part bids — an availability price (£/MW/h for being available), start-up costs, no-load costs, and a schedule of incremental energy offer prices. The NGC's GOAL scheduler stacked offers by price (merit order), respecting technical constraints (minimum loads, ramp rates, minimum up/down times, plant availability and the operational security standard).

System Marginal Price (SMP)
SMPh = highest offer price of the
          most expensive generating set
          scheduled by the Pool in
          half-hour h, computed on the
          UNCONSTRAINED schedule

Key design point: SMP was derived from the unconstrained merit-order schedule, before transmission constraints were imposed. Constrained-on plant was paid its bid; constrained-off plant was compensated.

The three Pool prices
Capacity Charge:  CC = LOLP × (VOLL − SMP)

Pool Purchase Price (paid to generators):
  PPP = SMP + CC

Pool Selling Price (paid by suppliers):
  PSP = PPP + UPLIFT

UPLIFT: reserve, constraint and
        start-up costs recovered ex post,
        allocated by metered volume
LOLP — Loss of Load Probability

Probability, per half-hour, that demand would exceed available capacity. It rose steeply when spare capacity was tight — and because CC = LOLP × (VOLL − SMP), a small capacity margin could add large sums to the price.

VOLL — Value of Lost Load

Willingness-to-pay to avoid interruption, set by OFFER at £2,000/MWh in 1990/91 and RPI-indexed annually. VOLL acted as the scarcity cap on prices and the anchor for the capacity charge.

UPLIFT

Ex-post add-on recovering reserve costs, constrained-on payments, plant available but unused, and start-up costs. Averaged ~1–2% of PSP, but was the Pool's only day-ahead price uncertainty.

Pay-as-clear property

Every dispatched generator received the same PPP (= SMP + CC) regardless of its individual bid. Infra-marginal plant earned the gap between SMP and its offer — the profit that funded fixed costs and entry.

Why "single" marginal pricing?

The Pool paid a single system-wide price per half-hour. There were no locational prices — constraints were "uplifted" into the PSP rather than priced by node. This contrasts directly with LMP markets (e.g. PJM) and mirrors the single-Pricing-Area SMP design of SAWEM Phase 1, complete with the same constrained-on/off compensation mechanisms.

A structural flaw: because SMP was the offer of the marginal unit on the unconstrained schedule, and because the duopoly's plant frequently set the margin, the two big generators could influence the price with both bid levels and capacity availability games — see Section F.

D) Who Was the Incumbent Generator?

The incumbent was the CEGB. On vesting (31 March 1990) its generation was broken into three companies, deliberately creating a duopoly:

🏭
National Power — the largest thermal generator, ~46–47% of capacity (fossil + some hydro). Inherited most of the CEGB's coal and oil plant.
⚡
PowerGen — the second thermal generator, ~27–28% of capacity.
☢️
Nuclear Electric — took all nuclear stations (~15%), retained in state ownership because the nuclear liabilities (decommissioning, Magnox) were unprivatisable in 1990.
🏴󠁧󠁢󠁳󠁣󠁴󠁿
Scottish + French imports — Scottish Power/Scottish Hydro (which later became ScottishPower and SSE), plus the 2 GW interconnector from Électricité de France, accounting for the remainder.

The result was a private-sector duopoly controlling ~75% of the market: National Power + PowerGen. Neither faced meaningful competition at launch — the CEGB's generation had simply been split in two — and this concentration dominated the Pool's price behaviour and policy history for its first six years.

Parallel with South Africa: SAWEM's Phase-1 SMP market launches with the Central Purchasing Agency acting for Eskom Generation's vesting-contracted plant (~70% market share) — a strikingly similar starting point. The Pool's experience with a dominant incumbent, capacity withholding, and the mitigation that followed is the key comparative case study for whether SMP alone can discipline a ~70% state generator.

E) Where Did the Transmission & Distribution Go?

The Electricity Act 1989 unbundled the CEGB's assets in the following way:

Transmission → National Grid Company (NGC)

The CEGB's 400/275 kV national grid and its pumped-storage stations (Dinorwig, Ffestiniog) were transferred to the National Grid Company, jointly owned by the 12 RECs at vesting. NGC acted as both grid operator and the Pool's central scheduler/clearing house. NGC was flotation-privatised in December 1995, later merging with Lattice to become National Grid Transco (2002) and today's National Grid plc.

Distribution → 12 Regional Electricity Companies (RECs)

Distribution networks passed from the Area Electricity Boards to 12 privatised RECs (e.g. Eastern, Midlands, NORWEB, SEEBOARD, SWEB, Yorkshire, etc.), floated on the London Stock Exchange in December 1990. Each REC held a regional monopoly over wires and (initially) supply, subject to an RPI-X price control set by the regulator OFFER.

Generation

CEGB generation → National Power, PowerGen, Nuclear Electric (1990). Later split further: PowerGen's coal → Edison/TXU, National Power's → AES/Midwest Power at Drax.

Transmission

CEGB grid + pumped storage → NGC (1990), floated Dec 1995.

Distribution & supply

Area Boards → 12 RECs (floated Dec 1990), RPI-X price control by OFFER.

Relevance to SA: this three-way unbundling — generation, transmission (→ NTCSA), distribution (→ municipalities) — is precisely the structure the Electricity Regulation Amendment Act 38 of 2024 is implementing for Eskom. SAWEM's single SMP with a national pricing area and constrained-on/off uplift is modelled directly on the Pool's PSP/UPLIFT mechanics.

F) Major Themes of the Pool's Existence

1990–1995
Market power & the duopoly problem. National Power and PowerGen faced no real competition. Wolak & Patrick (2001) showed they strategically manipulated both offer prices and capacity availability declarations: withdrawing plant raised LOLP (and thus the capacity charge) and forced higher-cost plant to set SMP. Pool prices spiked repeatedly (documented half-hourly SMP above £500/MWh in winter peaks).
1993–1995
Regulatory battles & price caps. OFFER's investigations led to a five-year price cap (1994/95–1998/99) on PPP above a reference level, plus a coal-supply "gas-for-coal" review and the ending of domestic coal contracts. The cap limited the duopoly's ability to profit from scarcity but did not remove the structural problem.
1994
Two-part bidding. The Pool introduced start-up/no-load/energy bid structures to better reflect generator costs and reduce "pricing above marginal cost" gaming.
1996
MMC reference & divestitures. The Monopolies and Mergers Commission found the duopoly's market power contrary to the public interest. National Power agreed to divest 4 GW and PowerGen 2 GW, later increased to 6 GW and 4 GW. Drax (Europe's largest coal plant) passed to AES; plant went to Eastern, Edison Mission, TXU and others — cutting the duopoly's share roughly in half.
1990–2000
The "dash for gas". Open access + falling CCGT costs + cheap North Sea gas produced a construction boom: ~20 GW of CCGTs by 2000, taking gas from ~1% to ~40% of generation while coal fell from ~67% to ~30%. Real wholesale prices fell ~25% and energy efficiency/entry delivered the Pool's core success.
1996–1998
Full retail competition. The 1998/99 opening of the domestic supply market completed the competition chain and allowed suppliers to hedge via electricity Contracts for Differences (CfDs) — the Pool's parallel black market.
1998–2001
Design backlash → NETA. Growing complaints that Pool prices were opaque, volatile, and manipulable (and the 1997–98 "Pool 2000" review) led the new Labour government to commission Ofgem's review, culminating in the New Electricity Trading Arrangements (NETA) — a bilateral, self-dispatch, pay-as-bid balancing market — which replaced the Pool on 27 March 2001.
Theme 1 — Market power v. market rules

The Pool proved that a marginal-price auction cannot discipline a concentrated incumbent by itself. The interaction of the SMP (unconstrained merit order), LOLP-based capacity payments, and availability declarations created a "pricing without output" loophole: generators could push prices up while producing less.

Theme 2 — Entry & stranded assets

The Pool opened the door to merchant entry (CCGTs), but entry stranded coal assets and triggered the "dash for gas" controversy, emissions-related politics, and social consequences in coal-mining regions — an early, real-world "just transition" episode.

Theme 3 — Price transparency & acceptability

The Pool daily published SMP/LOLP/PPP/PSP was transparent but the public found capacity payments and uplift opaque. The political economy of price formation (not just its efficiency) drove the switch to NETA.

Theme 4 — Congestion handling

A single national price with UPLIFT meant the Pool never sent locational signals or locational investment incentives for generation siting — a design lesson for the single-Pricing-Area choices in SAWEM Phase 1.

Real wholesale price fall (late-90s)
  • Prices fell in real terms. Despite early spikes (annual average SMP around £25/MWh and spikes above £500/MWh in 1991–94 winters), real wholesale prices declined ~25% by the late 1990s as entry and divestiture eroded the duopoly.
  • Massive capacity entry. ~20 GW of CCGTs commissioned by 2000 — the fastest capacity build-out in UK history — driven by open access to the Pool and the SMP revenue opportunity.
  • Fuel switching & the coal exit. Coal fell from roughly two-thirds to under a third of generation; the 1993–98 coal contract rundown produced major social disruption in mining regions.
  • Market power was real and quantified. Wolak & Patrick (2001) estimated a substantial share of consumer payments in the mid-1990s represented transfers above the competitive benchmark; the MMC found the market structure "against the public interest" and forced divestiture.
  • Thermal efficiency improved. The competitive squeeze forced incumbents to improve plant performance and availability.
  • Retail competition completed. All customers — including households by 1998/99 — could choose suppliers, with CfD hedging markets developing alongside the Pool.
  • Replacement. Persistent complaints about opacity and manipulability produced NETA (pay-as-bid bilateral trading), which cut wholesale prices sharply after 2001 — though critics note part of that fall was coincident CCGT entry.
Headline lesson: the Pool proved competitive wholesale electricity trading could work and could drive entry and cost reduction — but it also proved that single marginal pricing alone cannot fix a bad market structure. SAWEM's SMP design rests on the same architecture, which is why the Thesis project treats structural mitigation (vesting-hedge decay, CPA, NTCSA unbundling) as essential complements to the SMP.
