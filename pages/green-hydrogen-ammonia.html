Green Hydrogen & Ammonia

South Africa's oldest export economy — mining and minerals — meets its newest: green hydrogen and its shippable derivative, green ammonia. Produced from the country's exceptional solar and wind resource, ammonia can be burned in ship engines as a zero-carbon marine fuel, used as a hydrogen carrier for export, or returned to its traditional role in fertiliser and chemicals. This section covers the production process end to end, the full supply chain, the anchor projects at Saldanha and Coega, ammonia-fuelled bulk carriers and bunkering, the ammonia market and pricing, and the South Africa–EU iron ore green corridor.

🌍 Overview

Green hydrogen is hydrogen produced by splitting water with renewable electricity (electrolysis) rather than from fossil gas. On its own it is awkward to move — it must be compressed to 350–700 bar or cooled to −253 °C, and it has a low volumetric energy density (8.5 MJ/l). The practical solution is to convert it into a carrier molecule. The two candidates are green methanol (needs a sustainable carbon source) and green ammonia (needs only nitrogen from the air).

Ammonia wins in the South African context because nitrogen is unlimited and free, ammonia is already a globally traded commodity with established handling and storage infrastructure, and the same molecule can be used three ways: as marine fuel for ships, as an export carrier for hydrogen, and as industrial feedstock for fertiliser and chemicals. That "triple use" is what makes an ammonia plant bankable — no single market has to carry the whole investment.

Why ammonia and not just hydrogen? Roughly two tonnes of ammonia match the energy content of one tonne of conventional marine fuel oil. Ammonia lubricates the business case for hydrogen: the same plant can sell to shipping, to fertiliser markets, and to hydrogen exporters, spreading risk across several offtake sectors until each one matures.

⚡ Why South Africa

Four factors combine to give South Africa an unusual position in the global hydrogen economy.

1 · World-class renewables

An estimated 920 GW of combined wind and solar potential, with some of the best solar irradiation on the planet concentrated in the Northern Cape. A hybrid solar-plus-wind plant can push electrolyser capacity factors towards 80% by day — the reason the economics of a coastal ammonia plant stack up where a solar-only scheme would not.

2 · Geography on the trade lanes

The Cape sea route between Asia and Europe runs past South Africa's west and east coasts. As shipping decarbonises, more vessels will need to refuel en route. South Africa sits "midway between the eastern and western hemispheres" — an ideal bunkering stop for ships running on fuels with roughly half the energy density of oil.

3 · Existing industrial base

South Africa is already one of the world's largest producers of grey hydrogen (from coal and gas, largely for Sasol's synfuels and petrochemicals). It has deep ammonia and fertiliser handling experience, port infrastructure, and — critically — ~70% of global platinum production, the key catalyst input for electrolysers and fuel cells.

4 · The carbon-border squeeze

South Africa's largest trading partners are introducing carbon border taxes. Analysis presented to the WEF estimates that up to 35% of the country's export value could be at risk if decarbonisation is not pursued. Green hydrogen and ammonia turn a trade threat into an export opportunity.

🧪 Making Zero-Emission Ammonia from Green Hydrogen

"Green ammonia" is ordinary ammonia (NH₃) made without fossil carbon. Chemically it is identical to the ammonia used in fertiliser for a century — the difference is entirely in where the hydrogen comes from. The conventional route ("grey" ammonia) produces hydrogen from natural gas via steam methane reforming (SMR) or from coal gasification, releasing large volumes of CO₂. The green route replaces that fossil hydrogen with hydrogen from renewable-powered water electrolysis.

Renewable electricity + H₂O → H₂ + ½ O₂    (electrolysis)
Ambient air (78% N₂) → N₂                (cryogenic air separation)
3 H₂ + N₂ → 2 NH₃                     (Haber-Bosch synthesis, ~500 °C, high pressure)

Hydrogen and nitrogen combine in a 3:1 ratio. Because losses occur at each step, the practical conversion factor is roughly 5.6 tonnes of ammonia per tonne of hydrogen — the World Bank reference plant turns ~50,000 t/yr of hydrogen into ~280,000 t/yr of ammonia.

The six-step chain

STEP 1
Renewable power

A hybrid solar-and-wind plant generates the electricity. Best sites sit inland where irradiation and wind are strongest; the plant is typically grid-connected so surplus power can be sold back.

STEP 2
Water

Electrolysis needs pure water — about 9 kg per kg of hydrogen plus a margin. Coastal plants use seawater desalination, avoiding competition for scarce freshwater.

STEP 3
Electrolysis

Renewable electricity splits water into hydrogen and oxygen. Alkaline electrolysers are favoured today for cost and stack life; PEM and SOEC are alternatives.

STEP 4
Air separation

An Air Separation Unit (ASU) separates nitrogen from ambient air cryogenically — no feedstock cost, abundant supply, no emissions.

STEP 5
Haber-Bosch

Hydrogen and nitrogen are combined over a catalyst in the Haber-Bosch reactor to form ammonia. Requires steady power — the synthesis plant must not thermally cycle.

STEP 6
Storage & export

Ammonia is chilled to about −33 °C and stored in refrigerated tanks, ready for bunkering barges, export tankers, or pipeline delivery.

Why the synthesis plant needs steady power

The electrolyser is happy to follow the sun and the wind; the ammonia synthesis loop is not. Starting and stopping the Haber-Bosch reactor causes "thermal cycling" that damages the catalyst and shortens equipment life. Because the reactor runs at around 500 °C, the plant needs a constant supply. The World Bank's preferred solution is a grid-connected plant with a utility-scale battery: renewable power is wheeled through the grid to charge batteries by day, then discharged at night to run synthesis from hydrogen buffered in the pipeline. This combination is also what makes the fuel certifiable as renewable under the EU's hourly matching rules.

Electrolyser technologies at a glance. Alkaline — mature, cheapest, longest stack life, favoured today. PEM — more flexible, higher cost. SOEC — highest efficiency but still at demonstration stage. South Africa's platinum endowment matters here: PGM catalysts are a key input to PEM electrolysers and fuel cells.

🔗 The Full Supply Chain

A green ammonia industry is not one plant — it is a value chain that runs from a wind turbine blade on an inland farm to a bunker barge alongside a bulk carrier at anchor. Understanding who sits where, and who takes which risk, is the key to understanding why these projects take so long to reach financial close.

UPSTREAM — molecules
  • Renewable generation — dedicated solar PV and wind farms (often 1.5–2 GW), plus grid connection or "wheeling" arrangements.
  • Water — seawater desalination plants with marine intake and brine outfall.
  • Electrolysis — alkaline/PEM electrolyser banks producing hydrogen.
  • Air separation — cryogenic ASU producing nitrogen.
  • Battery storage — utility-scale Li-ion to steady the synthesis loop.
  • Pipelines — hydrogen and water pipelines linking the inland energy site to the coastal plant (often 70–100 km).
MIDSTREAM — ammonia
  • Haber-Bosch synthesis — the reactor that combines H₂ and N₂ into NH₃.
  • Refrigerated storage — cryogenic tanks at −33 °C (single-containment, ~30,000 m³ for a 280 kt/yr plant).
  • Loader / pipeline to berth — short cryogenic pipeline to a quay with loading arms.
  • Bunker vessels — small LPG-type carriers (~8,000–9,000 DWT) for ship-to-ship delivery.
  • Export carriers — large LPG/LR2-type tankers (up to ~120,000 DWT) for long-haul ammonia exports.
  • Certification — proving the molecule is renewably sourced (EU RED III, IMO rules).
DOWNSTREAM — markets
  • Marine fuel (bunkering) — ammonia-powered bulk carriers, container ships and tankers.
  • Exports — Japan, South Korea and Europe are the anchor buyers for South African green ammonia.
  • Power generation — co-firing ammonia in coal stations (Japan/Korea strategy) to cut CO₂.
  • Fertiliser & chemicals — the traditional, always-present ammonia market.
  • Green steel / DRI — hydrogen for direct-reduced iron, an important local offtake.
  • Hydrogen exports — ammonia as the carrier molecule for the global hydrogen trade.

Port and logistics layer

Every link above meets the sea at a port. South Africa has eight commercial ports and the World Bank estimates that ships calling at them could demand ~56,000 tonnes of hydrogen as early as 2030, rising to about 530,000 tonnes by 2050. The largest demand comes from Durban and Richards Bay, followed by Cape Town and Saldanha Bay. In addition, the passing-by fleet — vessels that never call at a South African port but round the Cape of Good Hope — could add a further 1.3 million tonnes of green hydrogen demand by 2050 if the country positions itself as a bunkering hub.

The three roles a port can play (World Bank): (1) a consumer of hydrogen for local industry, (2) a bunker hub supplying ships, and (3) an export gateway shipping ammonia and hydrogen derivatives to global markets. Saldanha and Coega are pursuing all three; Boegoebaai is built primarily around the export role.

⚓ Saldanha — The Deepwater Hub

Saldanha Bay, on the Western Cape's west coast, is the largest natural deepwater port in the Southern Hemisphere and the loading point for more than 90% of South Africa's iron ore exports. About a fifth of that ore already sails to Europe, with Rotterdam the largest receiving port. Those two facts are why Saldanha sits at the centre of both the country's green-hydrogen plans and the South Africa–EU iron ore green corridor.

The Freeport Saldanha Industrial Development Zone (IDZ) — formerly the Saldanha Bay IDZ and notably the first Special Economic Zone located inside a port — hosts a 365-hectare footprint with Customs Controlled Area status, offering tax and duty benefits to developers. It adjoins the port's iron ore terminal, multi-purpose terminal, and liquid-bulk berths.

Under the West Coast Green Hydrogen Master Plan, the region is being promoted as a hub to produce zero- and low-carbon fuels and goods — for domestic use, to refuel vessels calling at the port, and for export. The World Economic Forum profile of the district estimates it could produce on the order of 50,000 tonnes of green hydrogen a year, generating around 280,000 tonnes of green ammonia.

What the World Bank designed for Saldanha

The World Bank's pre-feasibility case study is the single most detailed public design for a South African green-ammonia plant. Its key features:

  • Inland renewables, coastal synthesis. A hybrid wind/solar farm and the electrolyser sit about 70–75 km inland (near Moorreesburg) where the resource is strongest; the ammonia synthesis plant, storage, desalination and battery sit inside the SEZ alongside the port.
  • 73 km of pipeline carry hydrogen and water from the inland site to the coast, rather than building new high-voltage powerlines — the study cites evidence that moving molecules by pipeline can be up to five times more cost-effective than moving electrons.
  • Grid-connected with battery backup, so the plant sells surplus electricity to the grid (helping the national supply problem) and draws steady power for synthesis at night.
  • Bunkering at the General Maintenance Quay (GMQ), using existing common-user infrastructure, upgraded with loading arms and a short cryogenic ammonia pipeline.
  • Two markets by design. Ship bunkering is only expected to reach the plant's scale after 2030, so the facility is designed to sell into the traditional ammonia market and to hydrogen offtakers (including potential green-steel production) in the interim — a "phased-in" approach the study argues is essential for risk management.
Saldanha's corridor role. The Global Maritime Forum feasibility study concludes that while the corridor's vessels might bunker green ammonia in Rotterdam in the early years, Saldanha is well-positioned to become the long-term bunkering hub for the corridor as South African production and port infrastructure come online towards 2035.

🏭 Coega — The Giga-Scale Ammonia Plant

If Saldanha is the commissioning gateway for marine fuel, Coega is the export engine. The Coega Special Economic Zone near Gqeberha (Port Elizabeth) in the Eastern Cape is South Africa's flagship industrial zone — established in 1999, around 9,000 hectares in extent, and served by the deep-water Port of Ngqura (16–18 m draught), purpose-built and operational since 2009. It is organised into 14 zones along cluster lines (automotive, metals, energy, chemicals, logistics) with Customs Controlled Areas for export-oriented manufacturing.

The anchor hydrogen project is the Hive Hydrogen green ammonia plant, developed by Hive Energy (UK) with South African partner BuiltAfrica holding a 25% stake. Reported at roughly US$5.8 billion for Phase 1, it is described as South Africa's first industrial-scale green ammonia plant.

How the Coega project is structured

  • Scale: reported output of more than 900,000 tonnes of green ammonia a year once complete, with the current target described as ~1 million tonnes a year for export — roughly three-and-a-half times the scale of the World Bank's Saldanha reference plant.
  • Power: about 1,200 MW of electrolysers, fed by the project's own 3.6 GW of solar and wind capacity — and designed to enable up to 9 GW of other independent power producer (IPP) renewables to reach the grid, which the developer argues will help alleviate load-shedding.
  • Markets: export to Japan, Korea and Europe. Japanese investors have begun due diligence to take an equity stake and secure offtake. End-uses include co-firing ammonia in Japanese and Korean coal power stations, and marine fuel to displace heavy fuel oil and diesel.
  • Climate impact: reported to avoid around 2.6 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent a year. Commissioning has been targeted around 2028–2029.
  • Finance: the SA-H2 Fund made its first investment — about US$20 million — in development funding for the Hive Coega green ammonia project.
  • Co-developer: BuiltAfrica, a South African renewable-energy investment and development company, holds 25% of Hive Hydrogen South Africa.
Coega's dual identity. The plant is simultaneously part of the country's export strategy (giga-scale ammonia to the Far East and Europe) and its domestic strategy (jobs, local grid capacity, industrialisation of the Eastern Cape). It does not depend on the iron ore corridor — it is the volume play, while Saldanha is the corridor play.

🧭 The Other Hydrogen Hubs

South Africa's hydrogen programme is deliberately multi-nodal. The Department of Public Works and Infrastructure has selected nine "Strategic Integrated Projects" (SIPs) for expedited approval, and the World Economic Forum workshop focused on three major hub opportunities: Saldanha Bay, Coega / Ngqura, and Boegoebaai, plus a Durban–Richards Bay "hydrogen valley" on the east coast.

Boegoebaai — Northern Cape

A proposed greenfield deep-sea port in the Northern Cape, near the Orange River mouth, designed from scratch around green hydrogen and bulk exports. The WEF lists the Boegoebaai Green H₂ Programme at up to 400 kt H₂/yr, and the Global Maritime Forum lists announced ammonia capacity near the port of around 285 ktpa by 2035.

Because it is greenfield, Boegoebaai can be purpose-built for high-volume export of green molecules (ammonia, hydrogen derivatives) alongside mining and agricultural products — but it must also attract the rail, road and grid infrastructure to get there. The World Bank's second case study examined green shipping fuel production at Boegoebaai alongside Saldanha.

Durban–Richards Bay Hydrogen Valley

A proposed "hydrogen valley" running along South Africa's north-east coastline, from Durban to Richards Bay in KwaZulu-Natal. The idea is to aggregate demand across existing heavy industry — refineries, chemicals, paper and pulp, aluminium, and the ports themselves — to kick-start hydrogen production at scale.

Richards Bay is also the site of the proposed LNG-to-power and FSRU developments, and is one of South Africa's two largest port energy-demand nodes alongside Durban.

Regional context — Namibia

The opportunities do not stop at the border. Namibian projects near Walvis Bay and the Erongo region are part of the same Southern African supply picture, with announced ammonia capacity of about 177 ktpa by 2035. Namibia's flagship is the Hyphen project — a multi-billion-dollar solar, wind and green ammonia development. Together, announced or in-development projects in South Africa's Northern and Western Cape plus Namibia's Erongo region exceed 700 ktpa (hydrogen equivalent), comfortably more than the iron-ore corridor would need under any demand scenario. The WEF describes the potential for a green corridor linking Southern Africa's coastal ports to connect this supply with demand.

🚢 Ammonia-Fuelled Bunker Carriers & Bunkering

Shipping carries over 80% of world trade. It also emitted about 1.1 Gt of greenhouse gases in 2018 — roughly 3% of the global total — and 99% of commercial shipping still burns fossil bunker fuel. In July 2023 the International Maritime Organization (IMO) adopted an ambition of net-zero emissions for international shipping by or around 2050. Meeting the interim target (zero- or near-zero-emission energy making up 5–10% of the fleet's energy mix by 2030) would require at least 5 million tonnes of green hydrogen for shipping alone.

Methanol is the more technologically mature near-term option — engines are already available and more than 200 dual-fuel methanol vessels have been ordered globally. But methanol needs a sustainable source of carbon, and that is precisely where South Africa is short: there is little biogenic CO₂ available near the candidate ports, and industrial CO₂ sources do not close the carbon cycle. Ammonia needs only nitrogen from the air, and it can double as a hydrogen export carrier. That is why ammonia is the preferred South African marine fuel, while methanol remains a complementary option.

How bunkering works

  • The bunker vessel / barge. A small ammonia-capable carrier — in the World Bank design, a mid-size LPG-type vessel of about 9,145 m³ (≈8,000 DWT) — takes ammonia from the shore tank and delivers it ship-to-ship (STS) to a vessel at anchor or alongside a berth.
  • Refrigerated transfer. Ammonia moves at about −33 °C through composite flexible hoses between barge and client vessel.
  • Parcel size. In the World Bank model each receiving vessel takes a parcel of around 4,573 m³, at a ship-to-ship loading rate of 400 m³ per hour. Because ammonia has roughly half the energy density of oil, ships must bunker about twice as often — which is exactly why a mid-voyage bunkering stop like Saldanha becomes valuable.
  • Regional reach. A single bunker vessel can serve both Saldanha and Cape Town (~120 km apart), improving its economics.
Safety is the gating factor. Ammonia is highly toxic. The liquid causes caustic burns on contact, and a release forms a vapour cloud. Refrigerated ammonia bunkering requires quantitative risk assessment: indicative safety distances at a 400 m³/h transfer rate are about 153 m for the 10⁻⁵/year individual-risk contour and 255 m for the 10⁻⁶/year contour, with a toxic cloud "focus area" extending to roughly 1.45 km. Standards for ammonia bunkering (like Rotterdam's PGS-12) are still being written; IMO guidelines are under development. Ammonia is already handled safely at about 150 port terminals worldwide, moving around 20 Mt a year — so the challenge is adapting existing practice to the novel act of bunkering, not inventing handling from scratch. Engine designers must also control nitrous oxide (N₂O) "slip" — a greenhouse gas with a global warming potential about 273 times that of CO₂ over 100 years.

Where the ships are coming from

Ammonia-powered ships are no longer hypothetical. Iron ore bulk carriers make up a large share of ammonia-powered vessels on order globally, with 15 vessels due to hit the water by 2028 and more expected. The Global Maritime Forum feasibility study found that up to 12 of these could be available to serve the South Africa–Europe iron ore corridor in its first deployment years, depending on where a viable business case exists. The preferred deployment is a dedicated fleet on an "S-curve": starting with one vessel in 2029 and adding three a year.

🛤️ The South Africa–EU Iron Ore Green Corridor

A green shipping corridor is a specific trade route where governments, cargo owners, ship operators, ports and fuel suppliers collaborate to make zero-emission shipping viable on that route. More than 70 corridors have been announced since the Clydebank Declaration at COP26 in 2021 — but the South Africa–EU iron ore corridor is one of the very few connecting the Global South to the Global North.

The critical risk: none is financed yet. Ammonia plants typically take five to eight years from concept to commercial operation. To have green ammonia flowing on the corridor in 2029, investment decisions were needed yesterday. Delays come from an uncertain policy landscape, permitting, high capital costs, slow infrastructure development and the absence of secured offtake. Demand aggregation with other buyers — not just the corridor — is likely required to get projects over the line.

📈 The Ammonia Market & Pricing

Two markets in one molecule

Ammonia has been traded globally for a century, and this existing market is what de-risks the green transition. There are effectively two demand pools:

The traditional market
  • Fertiliser — by far the largest use, as a source of nitrogen for crops.
  • Chemicals & explosives — industrial feedstock.
  • Refrigeration — industrial cooling.
  • Around 20 million tonnes of ammonia a year is moved by ship and handled at about 150 port terminals worldwide, including in South Africa.
The emerging energy market
  • Marine fuel — the core opportunity for South Africa.
  • Co-firing in power stations — mainly Japan and Korea.
  • Hydrogen carrier — exporting hydrogen as ammonia.
  • Green steel (DRI) — hydrogen as a reducing agent.

What drives the price

Grey ammonia is priced broadly off natural gas (its main feedstock), so its cost moves with gas prices. Green ammonia replaces that gas cost with the cost of renewable electricity, electrolysers and capital. Because renewables and capital dominate, green ammonia's price is driven by:

  • Renewable energy cost — the single largest input; the reason places with world-class solar and wind can compete.
  • Electrolyser capital cost and efficiency — falling as the industry scales.
  • Cost of capital — the Achilles' heel of emerging markets. In South Africa the cost of capital for energy projects can be as high as 18% (with debt up to ~12%), which is why developers argue the "green premium" needs concessional finance and de-risking to close.
  • Bunkering volume and logistics — economies of scale in port bunkering are typically realised once volumes exceed 200 ktpa, which is why demand aggregation matters so much.

The green premium in numbers — the World Bank's Saldanha analysis

The World Bank ran the Saldanha project through four additive business scenarios, each adding a step of the value chain and a new revenue stream. The results show precisely where the value sits today — and where it does not yet exist:

The punchline. Selling renewable electricity works. Selling green ammonia into the traditional fertiliser/chemicals market works. Selling green hydrogen as an industrial feedstock at US$2/kg does not yet work. And selling green ammonia as a marine fuel at parity with oil — the immature market — does not work today without policy support. This is why the industry talks about a "green premium" and a "cost gap": it is the difference between what green ammonia costs to make and what buyers currently pay for the fossil alternative.

⚖️ Policy & Regulation

The economics of green ammonia depend as much on regulation as on engineering. Three regulatory systems matter most to a South African producer selling into the corridor.

EU — FuelEU Maritime & EU ETS
  • FuelEU Maritime — a greenhouse-gas fuel-intensity requirement that came into force in January 2025.
  • EU Emissions Trading Scheme (EU ETS) — an emissions levy expected to be fully phased in by 2026.
  • Both apply to 50% of voyages entering or leaving the EU, and non-compliance incurs penalties. FuelEU allows trading of over- and under-compliance between vessels.
  • Impact: together these regulations narrow the green ammonia cost gap by 63%, materially strengthening the investment case.
IMO — Net-Zero Framework
  • Implements the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy: net-zero by 2050, with interim targets of at least 20% reduction by 2030 and 70% by 2040.
  • Agreed in April 2025; adoption was delayed in October 2025 and is set to be revisited in 2026.
  • A two-tier global fuel standard plus a credit-trading mechanism. Ships that over-comply can sell surplus units to those that under-comply.
  • Additional rewards for "zero- and near-zero" fuels (≤19.0 gCO₂eq/MJ vs ~93.3 today).
  • Impact: under the most favourable assumptions, green ammonia could be almost 30% cheaper than VLSFO on a total-cost-of-ownership basis; under the least favourable, about 30% more expensive — hence the uncertainty.
EU — RED III & CBAM
  • Renewable Energy Directive III (RED III) requires 42% of all ammonia consumed in the EU to be green by 2030 — for both domestic production and imports. This is a direct demand mandate for South African ammonia.
  • RED III sets strict rules on "additionality", geography and temporal correlation (hourly matching from 2030) that determine whether a molecule can be certified as renewable.
  • CBAM — the carbon border adjustment mechanism — prices the embedded carbon in imports, putting up to 35% of South Africa's export value at risk without decarbonisation.

South Africa's side of the bargain

For the corridor and the plants to proceed, the WEF and Global Maritime Forum both stress that South Africa must deliver an enabling domestic environment. The list keeps recurring:

  • Long-term offtake contracts. Green ammonia plants need agreements of six to ten years or more to reach financial close, but today's shipping market buys fuel on the spot or on contracts of under a year. This mismatch is the single biggest commercial barrier.
  • Concessional and blended finance to lower the cost of capital — the SA-H2 Fund is the template.
  • Port, rail and grid upgrades so that products can physically move and the plants can connect.
  • Regulatory clarity on safety, permitting (EIAs typically take around two years) and certification.
  • Skills — SAREM is coordinating 21 Sector Education and Training Authorities to build the workforce.
Double counting as upside. If EU regulations continue alongside the IMO framework, penalties and rewards could apply in parallel — potentially doubling surplus-unit generation and strengthening the business case for the South Africa–Europe route relative to competing iron ore routes such as Australia–East Asia or Brazil–China.

⚠️ Key Risks & Challenges

Electricity crisis and grid

South Africa's grid operates at little more than 50% capacity and has limited headroom for new generation. Only around 30 GW of renewables can currently be absorbed, far short of the ~190 GW implied by a 2050 net-zero pathway, and at least 14,000 km of new power lines would be needed. Green hydrogen projects must add renewable capacity rather than compete for existing supply.

Port and logistics inefficiency

Operators lack confidence in South African ports' reliability — the World Bank ranked the country's container ports at the bottom of global performance in 2021. For new commodities like ammonia, the port system faces entirely new operational and safety requirements. Protecting infrastructure and restoring efficiency is a precondition for the whole hydrogen economy.

Cost of capital

Energy projects in South Africa face a cost of capital as high as 18% (debt up to about 12%), against far lower rates in Europe and the US. This alone can make an otherwise competitive project unbankable, and it is why concessional finance, guarantees and blended structures are central to the strategy.

Offtake mismatch and no FID

Green ammonia projects need long-term offtake; shipping buys short-term. As of the corridor feasibility report, none of the announced Southern African ammonia projects had reached final investment decision. Without aggregated demand that spans sectors, projects may simply not be built in time for a 2029 corridor launch.

Ammonia safety

Ammonia is highly toxic. Bunker-handling regulations are still being written, safety distances can be substantial, and N₂O slip from engines must be controlled to preserve the climate benefit. South Africa would be introducing a new class of port hazard close to residential areas such as Saldanha town.

Water, land and biodiversity

Large renewable plants need land — 15,700 ha for a wind farm alone in the Saldanha case — much of it potentially classified as Critical Biodiversity Area, triggering offset requirements. Desalination must be managed to protect marine life. Social acceptance depends on these impacts being handled well.

🔭 Outlook — The Decade That Decides It

2025–2026

Corridor feasibility study completed (Sept 2025). FuelEU and EU ETS bite. IMO Net-Zero Framework revisited. SA-H2 Fund deploys first capital into the Coega plant. The next 18 months are about converting pre-feasibility work into bankable deals.

2026–2028

Ammonia-powered vessels begin entering service (15 bulk carriers due by 2028). Saldanha bunkering studies and port infrastructure design progress. Coega targets commissioning around 2028–2029. RED III's 42% green ammonia mandate and hourly matching arrive in 2030, creating hard demand.

2029

Corridor target year for the first green-ammonia-powered iron ore carriers on the Saldanha–Rotterdam route — with fuel likely bunkered in Rotterdam initially.

2030–2032

Shipping's tipping point: at least 5% of global fleet energy from scalable zero-emission fuels. Under the preferred deployment strategy, the corridor could be fully decarbonised by 2031–2032 if dedicated vessels are added as planned. South African bunkering at Saldanha ramps up.

2035

Corridor target for full decarbonisation. Saldanha positioned as the long-term bunkering hub. Regional ammonia capacity (Saldanha, Boegoebaai, Walvis Bay) could exceed 700 ktpa of hydrogen equivalent — more than enough for the corridor under every demand scenario.

2050

The hydrogen economy could add 5.9% to GDP (roughly R500 billion) and up to 1.8–2 million jobs under scenarios with hydrogen exports. International shipping's demand for South African hydrogen could reach ~2 million tonnes a year; the passing-by fleet could add another 1.3 Mt.

The single sentence version. South Africa has the renewables, the ports, the ammonia handling experience and the platinum to build a world-class green ammonia industry — what it does not yet have is finance at a competitive cost, ports that global shipping trusts, and offtake contracts long enough to bank the plants. Those three gaps are the whole game.
