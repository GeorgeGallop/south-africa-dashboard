Eskom Holdings SOC Ltd

South Africa's State-Owned Electricity Utility — Largest Power Generator in Africa

Eskom is the backbone of South Africa's energy system, generating approximately 90% of the country's electricity and operating the largest power generation fleet on the African continent. Founded in 1923, it has been the engine of South African industrialisation, but decades of underinvestment, corruption, and mismanagement have pushed it into a deep financial and operational crisis.

History & Origins
  • 1923: Eskom (then ESCOM — Electricity Supply Commission) established by the South African government to provide a reliable and affordable electricity supply for mining and industrial development. The first major power stations were coal-fired and located near the Witbank coal fields (Mpumalanga), where most of the country's coal reserves are concentrated.
  • 1940s–1970s: Massive expansion during South Africa's industrialisation. Eskom built some of the world's largest coal-fired stations — including Camden, Hendrina, and Grootvlei — supplying cheap power to the gold and platinum mines, as well as to the growing manufacturing sector.
  • 1980s–1990s: Eskom reached overcapacity due to a slowdown in demand growth. Stations were mothballed. The end of apartheid in 1994 brought expectations of electrification and expanded access. Eskom connected millions of new households, but revenue from low-income consumers did not cover the cost of supply.
  • 2000s: By the early 2000s, Eskom's reserve margin had collapsed. In 2008, the utility introduced load shedding — controlled blackouts to protect the grid from total collapse. This marked the beginning of a crisis that has persisted for over 15 years.
  • 2010s: Amid state capture (the Zuma-Gupta era), Eskom was systematically looted — procurement contracts rigged, coal supply overpriced, senior management appointed through patronage, and maintenance budgets slashed. The Medupi and Kusile mega-power stations (6 x 800 MW units each) suffered massive cost overruns and design defects, ballooning from a combined ~R160bn budget to over R450bn.
  • 2020s: The crisis deepened with record levels of load shedding — 329 days in 2023 alone. Eskom's debt exceeded R400bn, forcing the government to announce a R254bn debt relief package (2023–2026). In 2022, the government launched an unbundling process, splitting Eskom into three separate state-owned entities: Generation, Transmission (the National Transmission Company of South Africa — NTCSA), and Distribution.
Generation Fleet & Key Power Stations

Eskom's generation fleet is overwhelmingly coal-fired, with 15 coal-fired power stations providing ~85% of total capacity. The remainder comes from nuclear (Koeberg), pumped storage (Drakensberg, Palmiet, Ingula), open-cycle gas turbines (Ankerlig, Gourikwa), wind (Sere), and solar PV.

Medupi & Kusile (Supercritical)

Medupi (Lephalale, Limpopo) and Kusile (Mpumalanga) are the newest stations, each with 6 x 800 MW units. Construction started in 2007, but both are years late and billions over budget. Medupi was completed in 2021; Kusile is still commissioning units as of 2025. Design flaws in boiler ductwork led to collapses and prolonged outages.

Koeberg Nuclear (1,860 MW)

Africa's only nuclear power station, located near Cape Town. Two pressurised water reactors commissioned in 1984–1985. Unit 1 has been granted a 20-year licence extension to 2044; Unit 2 is under review. Steam generator replacements and life-extension programmes have caused extended shutdowns.

Coal Fleet (Mpumalanga)

The majority of Eskom stations are concentrated in Mpumalanga: Matla (>3,600 MW), Kendal (>3,800 MW), Majuba (>4,100 MW), Lethabo (>3,600 MW), Tutuka (>3,500 MW), Duvha (>3,600 MW), Arnot, Camden, Grootvlei, Hendrina — many operating well past design life with declining energy availability factors (EAF). Average coal fleet EAF has fallen below 60%.

Renewables & OCGTs

Eskom operates the Sere wind farm (100 MW), Lethabo solar PV, and pump storage plants (Drakensberg 1,000 MW, Palmiet 400 MW, Ingula 1,332 MW). Open-cycle gas turbines at Ankerlig and Gourikwa (2,000 MW combined) burn diesel at very high cost to meet peak demand. Eskom also purchases power from IPPs under REIPPP and emergency power programmes.

The Load Shedding Crisis & Its Causes

Load shedding — Eskom's controlled, rotational blackouts — began in 2008 and escalated to unprecedented severity in 2022–2023. In 2023, South Africans experienced 329 days of load shedding (Stage 3–6). Direct economic cost estimated at R500+ billion.

Root causes include:

  • State capture (2013–2018): The Zondo Commission documented systematic looting of Eskom. Coal contracts were awarded to Gupta-linked entities at inflated prices (>R16bn overpayment). Senior executives were appointed through political patronage, stripping the utility of engineering competence.
  • Deferred maintenance: Maintenance budgets were slashed to hide financial problems. Coal mills, boilers, and turbine outages accumulated. By 2022, the average coal unit availability was below 60% — meaning ~40% of nominal capacity was unavailable at any time.
  • Medupi/Kusile cost overruns: The two mega-projects consumed capital that should have funded maintenance of the existing fleet. Combined costs exceeded R450bn — more than 3x the original budget — and both stations delivered far less capacity than planned for years.
  • Sabotage and theft: Coal stockpile theft, diesel theft, cable theft, and vandalism were widespread. Eskom lost billions annually to organised criminal syndicates operating inside the company.
  • Governance failure: Eskom had five CEOs between 2014 and 2020. Board appointments were politicised. A culture of non-accountability developed at all levels.
Unbundling: Eskom's Breakup

In 2022, the government announced that Eskom would be split into three separate state-owned entities, following the model used internationally to introduce competition and transparency:

  • Generation (GenCo): Owns and operates Eskom's power stations. Will compete with Independent Power Producers (IPPs) in the wholesale electricity market.
  • Transmission (NTCSA): A legally independent entity owning the high-voltage grid. Will provide open, non-discriminatory access to all generators and traders. This is the key enabler for private power producers to wheel electricity through the grid.
  • Distribution (DistCo): Manages the low-voltage network that delivers electricity to end users (homes, businesses).

The transmission entity (NTCSA) was legally incorporated in 2022 and began operation in 2024. Full operational separation is expected by 2026–2027. Generation and distribution unbundling are further behind. A key complication is debt allocation — over R400bn of Eskom debt must be allocated across the three entities in a way that does not leave any of them insolvent from Day 1.

Eskom's Debt & Government Bailout

By FY2023, Eskom's total debt exceeded R440bn — equivalent to ~7% of South Africa's GDP. Interest payments alone consumed ~R80bn per year (greater than the budget of several government departments). The utility could not service this debt without government support.

In 2023, National Treasury announced a R254bn debt relief package spread over 3 years (FY2024–FY2026), conditional on strict operational and governance reforms. The package requires Eskom to:

  • Reduce operational costs (including a headcount reduction).
  • Improve plant performance (EAF targets).
  • Implement procurement and anti-corruption reforms.
  • Make meaningful progress on unbundling.
  • Sell non-core assets.

Progress in 2024: load shedding stopped in April 2024 (the longest period without blackouts since 2020). But the fuel cost was high — massive diesel burn from OCGTs kept the lights on, not structural improvement in coal plant performance. The EAF of the coal fleet remains below 60%, and without sustained maintenance, load shedding could return.
