🪨 Karoo Shale Gas

South Africa's Giant Unconventional Gas Resource — Trillions of Cubic Feet Trapped in Ancient Rock, Waiting on Technology, Regulation, and Political Will

Beneath the semi-desert of the Karoo — a vast, sparsely populated region covering roughly 700,000 km² of South Africa's interior — lies one of the world's largest untapped shale gas resources. The Permian-aged Whitehill Formation, a black shale deposited roughly 275 million years ago, is estimated to contain 370 trillion cubic feet (Tcf) of gas in place, with a recoverable estimate of 209 Tcf. To put that in perspective: South Africa's entire annual natural gas consumption is about 0.16 Tcf. The Karoo shale could theoretically supply the country for over a thousand years at current consumption rates. But extracting it requires hydraulic fracturing — fracking — a technology that has ignited one of the most intense environmental and political debates in South Africa's recent history. This page explains what the resource is, where it sits, how it would be extracted, and why, after more than a decade of study and controversy, not a single commercial well has been drilled.

Part 1: What the Karoo Shale Is — 275 Million Years in the Making

The Karoo Basin is one of the great sedimentary basins of the world — a vast depression that accumulated sediments for roughly 100 million years during the Permian and Triassic periods, when South Africa was part of the supercontinent Gondwana. The basin's rocks record environments ranging from glacial (the Dwyka Group tillites, deposited during an ice age) through deep marine anoxic basins (the lower Ecca Group shales) to fluvial and desert deposits (the Beaufort and Stormberg groups).

The target for shale gas is the Whitehill Formation, part of the lower Ecca Group. During the Permian, this area was a deep, oxygen-starved marine basin. Organic matter — algae, plankton, and other marine life — settled to the bottom and was buried under layers of sediment. Over millions of years, heat and pressure converted this organic material into hydrocarbons — oil and gas trapped within the microscopic pore spaces of the shale. This is what makes it an "unconventional" resource: the gas is not pooled in a discrete reservoir trap like a conventional gas field. It is dispersed throughout the rock itself, and the rock is so impermeable that the gas cannot flow naturally to a wellbore. It must be forced out — which is where hydraulic fracturing comes in.

Why the Whitehill Formation Is Prospective
  • Total Organic Carbon (TOC): Average 4.5%, with values up to 14%. Any shale with TOC above 2% is considered a viable target; the Whitehill is exceptionally rich.
  • Thermal maturity: Ro = 1–4%, meaning the organic material has been cooked into the gas window — the right temperature range for natural gas generation.
  • Mineralogy: High quartz content (~50% average) with carbonate-rich intervals. Quartz-rich shales are brittle and fracture well — essential for successful fracking. Clay-rich shales, by contrast, deform plastically and don't fracture effectively.
  • Thickness: Average 35 metres, with thicker sections in the distal (deep-basin) areas. Commercial shale plays typically require at least 30 metres of continuous thickness.
  • Regional extent: Continuous over approximately 200,000 km² — roughly four times the size of the Marcellus Shale play in the United States.
Geological Risks and Uncertainties
  • Dolerite intrusions: The Karoo Basin was invaded by massive dolerite (diabase) sills and dykes during the Jurassic breakup of Gondwana (~180 million years ago). These intrusions cut through the Whitehill Formation in many areas. Where dolerite has intruded, it may have baked the shale (destroying the gas), fractured it (potentially allowing gas to escape), or compartmentalised the reservoir into isolated blocks that cannot be drained by a single well.
  • High maturity in the south: Along the southern basin margin near the Cape Fold Belt, the Whitehill Formation has been metamorphosed to graphite grade (Ro > 4%). The gas has been overcooked — driven off by temperatures that were too high. The sweet spot is likely further north, where maturity is in the 1.5–3.5% Ro range.
  • Sparse data: Most of what we know about the Whitehill Formation comes from a handful of deep boreholes drilled in the 1960s and 1970s, plus surface outcrops along the basin margins. The subsurface between these data points is largely unknown. The PASA/CGS deep drilling programme is designed to fill this gap — but until those wells are drilled and cored, the resource estimates remain speculative.

Part 2: What Hydraulic Fracturing Actually Involves

Hydraulic fracturing — fracking — is a well-stimulation technique designed to extract oil and gas from impermeable rock formations. The process works as follows:

  1. Drill a vertical well to a depth of 1.5–4 km, through the overlying rock layers to the target shale formation.
  2. Turn the well horizontal — once the drill bit reaches the shale, it turns 90 degrees and drills horizontally through the formation for 1–2 km. This exposes thousands of metres of reservoir rock to the wellbore, rather than just the 30-metre vertical thickness.
  3. Steel casing and cement are installed to isolate the wellbore from surrounding rock layers and groundwater aquifers. Multiple layers of steel pipe and cement are used, with the integrity tested by pressure before fracturing begins.
  4. Perforate the casing — shaped charges are fired through the steel casing and cement into the shale, creating small holes that connect the wellbore to the reservoir rock.
  5. Pump fracturing fluid — a mixture of water (~90%), sand/proppant (~9.5%), and chemical additives (~0.5%) is pumped down the well at extremely high pressure (thousands of psi). The pressure creates fractures in the shale that extend tens to hundreds of metres from the wellbore.
  6. The proppant (sand) holds the fractures open after the pressure is released. Without proppant, the fractures would close and the gas would be trapped again.
  7. Flowback and production — the fracturing fluid is partially recovered (flowback water), and gas begins flowing from the shale through the fractures into the wellbore and up to the surface.

A typical shale gas well uses 10–20 million litres of water per fracturing operation, and a well pad may have 6–12 wells. In the Karoo — a semi-arid region where water is scarce and mostly sourced from groundwater — this water demand is one of the central environmental concerns.

If even a fraction of the estimated 209 Tcf is technically recoverable at commercial rates, the Karoo shale gas would transform South Africa's energy landscape. It would provide a domestic gas supply that could fuel gas-to-power plants, displace diesel and coal in industrial applications, provide feedstock for petrochemicals (supplementing or replacing Sasol's Secunda operations), and potentially enable LNG exports. It would also fundamentally change the economics of the Gas Master Plan — instead of racing to build LNG import terminals before the Mozambique "gas cliff" in 2028, South Africa could be looking at energy independence.

But there is a giant gap between "gas in place" and "commercially producible gas." The 209 Tcf is a recoverable resource estimate — the amount of gas that might be technically extractable. It is not a reserve — gas that has been proven by drilling and can be commercially produced. No one knows how much of the 209 Tcf can actually be produced at a cost that competes with imported LNG or pipeline gas. The only way to answer that question is to drill wells, fracture the shale, and measure the flow rates — and that has not yet happened.

Part 4: The Karoo Fracking Debate — Why Nothing Has Happened Yet

South Africa has been debating Karoo shale gas for over a decade. Exploration rights were first applied for in 2008–2010 by Shell, Falcon Oil & Gas, and Bundu Gas & Oil, covering tens of thousands of square kilometres of the Karoo. What followed was one of the most contentious environmental battles in post-apartheid South Africa:

  • 2011: The Department of Mineral Resources imposed a moratorium on shale gas exploration, citing the need to study the environmental impacts.
  • 2012: A government-commissioned study by Econometrix estimated that developing just 5% of the Karoo shale resource (20 Tcf) could add R80–200 billion to GDP and create 300,000–700,000 jobs. The moratorium was lifted.
  • 2013–2015: Proposed fracking regulations were published for public comment. The public response was overwhelmingly negative, driven by environmental groups, farming communities, and the tourism industry — all of whom argued that fracking would contaminate groundwater, consume scarce water resources, and industrialise a uniquely pristine landscape.
  • 2016: The CSIR published its Strategic Environmental Assessment for Shale Gas Development — a 3,000+ page scientific study involving 500+ experts. It concluded that shale gas development could proceed under strict regulation but identified water as the single greatest risk, recommending that fracking be prohibited in areas of high water stress and that baseline groundwater monitoring be established before any drilling.
  • 2017–2019: Legal challenges by environmental groups (including the Treasure the Karoo Action Group) tied up the licensing process. The courts affirmed the right of the government to grant exploration rights, but the regulatory framework remained incomplete. The Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act (MPRDA) regulations for onshore oil and gas, including fracking-specific provisions, were repeatedly drafted, consulted on, and revised — but not finalised.
  • October 2019: The Department of Mineral Resources and Energy mandated the Petroleum Agency SA (PASA) and the Council for Geoscience (CGS) to undertake a regional scientific programme — the Shale Gas Strategic Project — to collect the baseline data that the CSIR said was needed before any commercial drilling.
  • 2022–2024: The PASA/CGS programme continued its work packages: Karoo deep drilling, resource evaluation, baseline groundwater monitoring, risk assessment of abandoned wells, and baseline seismicity monitoring. The first deep stratigraphic drill holes began to be planned — not for production, but for scientific data collection: coring the Whitehill Formation, measuring gas content, testing rock properties, and establishing pre-drilling environmental baselines.
  • March 2026: The government announced its intention to lift the long-standing moratorium on shale gas development in the Karoo Basin. The moratorium had been in place since 2011, originally introduced to allow time for the development of proper environmental and fracking regulations. The announcement marked a decisive shift in government posture — from the precautionary stance that had defined policy for 15 years toward active pursuit of the resource.
  • May 2026: In his budget speech, Minister of Mineral and Petroleum Resources Gwede Mantashe confirmed an allocation of R48.1-million for the implementation of the Karoo Shale Gas Project and declared that South Africa was "overly dependent on imported refined petroleum products." The former Minister of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment Willie Aucamp spoke of ending "decision paralysis" on oil and gas exploration, while stressing the constitutional obligation to promote economic growth without compromising the environment. Mantashe framed petroleum security as "an economic necessity and a national imperative," arguing that a country with significant mineral and petroleum potential should not remain exposed to external supply shocks.

As of mid-2026, not a single commercial shale gas well has been drilled in the Karoo. But the landscape has shifted decisively. The moratorium that had been in place since 2011 is being lifted. R48.1-million has been allocated to the Karoo Shale Gas Project. The government's posture has moved from precaution to pursuit — though the fracking-specific regulations under the MPRDA remain unfinalised, and environmental groups have signalled that legal challenges are inevitable. The PASA/CGS scientific programme continues, but it is now operating in a political environment where the question is no longer whether to explore, but how fast. The gap between where South Africa is (scientific baseline studies) and where it would need to be to produce commercial shale gas (thousands of wells, gathering pipelines, processing plants, and market infrastructure) is measured in decades and hundreds of billions of rand — but the starting gun has been fired.

Part 5: The PASA/CGS Scientific Programme — Five Work Packages

The current Shale Gas Strategic Project is not an exploration programme — it is a scientific data-gathering exercise designed to answer the questions that have prevented exploration from proceeding. The project has five work packages:

1. Karoo Deep Drilling

Drill deep stratigraphic boreholes through the Karoo sequence into the Whitehill Formation. Core the shale, measure its gas content (desorption canisters), analyse its mineralogy, measure its porosity and permeability, and test its mechanical properties. This is the most important work package — without actual core samples from depth, all resource estimates are based on surface outcrops and 50-year-old borehole data.

2. Petroleum Resource Evaluation

Integrate all available geological, geophysical, and geochemical data to produce an updated resource assessment. This includes seismic data (if available), well logs, core analysis, and basin modelling — building a 3D picture of the Whitehill Formation's extent, thickness, depth, maturity, and gas content. The US EIA's 370 Tcf estimate and PASA's 209 Tcf recoverable estimate will be refined once the new drilling data is available.

3. Baseline Groundwater Monitoring

Establish a network of groundwater monitoring wells across the Karoo to measure water quality, water levels, and aquifer characteristics BEFORE any fracking occurs. This is essential because if fracking does proceed, there must be pre-drilling data to determine whether any later water contamination is natural or caused by operations. The Karoo's aquifers are poorly understood; this work will also improve scientific knowledge regardless of whether fracking proceeds.

4. Risk Assessment of Abandoned Wells

The Karoo has hundreds of old boreholes — water wells, coal exploration holes, and a handful of deep petroleum exploration wells from the 1960s–1970s. Many of these were abandoned without modern plugging and abandonment standards. If fracking were to pressurise the subsurface, gas or fracturing fluid could potentially migrate up old wellbores into shallow aquifers. This work package maps and assesses the condition of abandoned wells and identifies those that would need to be re-plugged before any fracking could safely proceed.

5. Baseline Seismicity Monitoring

Fracking can induce small earthquakes — microseismic events — as the rock fractures. In some parts of the world (Oklahoma, Groningen in the Netherlands), wastewater injection associated with oil and gas operations has triggered larger, damaging earthquakes. The Karoo is not a naturally seismically active area, but to determine whether fracking would change that, a baseline seismicity monitoring network must be in place before operations begin. This work package installs seismometers and records natural background seismicity to establish the baseline.

Part 6: The Water Question — Can the Karoo Support Fracking?

The single greatest obstacle to Karoo shale gas development is water. The Karoo is a semi-arid region. Annual rainfall is typically 200–400 mm, mostly in summer thunderstorms. Surface water is scarce — the region's rivers (the Sundays, Great Fish, Gamtoos) are seasonal and unreliable. Groundwater is the primary water source for agriculture (sheep farming, game farming), towns, and communities.

A single shale gas well requires 10–20 million litres of water for hydraulic fracturing. A development programme of even 1,000 wells (a fraction of what a commercial-scale play would require — the Marcellus Shale in Pennsylvania has over 10,000 wells) would require 10–20 billion litres of water — the equivalent of a small dam. Where would this water come from in a region that already struggles with scarcity?

The options are limited:

  • Groundwater: The Karoo's aquifers are low-yield and slow to recharge. Extracting water for fracking at scale would lower the water table and potentially dry up existing boreholes — the same boreholes that farms and towns depend on.
  • Trucked water: Water could be trucked from outside the Karoo — from the Gariep Dam (Orange River) or the coast. But this would be extraordinarily expensive, add heavy truck traffic to the region's roads, and create its own environmental impacts. The CSIR assessment concluded that trucking water at scale is not feasible.
  • Recycled flowback water: After fracking, a portion of the fracturing fluid returns to the surface as flowback water, which is contaminated with salts, heavy metals, and naturally occurring radioactive materials (NORMs) from the shale. This water can be treated and reused for subsequent fracking operations — reducing net water consumption. In the US Marcellus play, recycling rates now exceed 90% in some areas. But recycling requires treatment infrastructure that does not exist in the Karoo, and some fraction of the water will always be lost — permanently trapped in the shale or requiring deep-well disposal.
  • Non-aqueous fracking fluids: Research is ongoing into waterless fracking — using liquid CO₂, nitrogen foam, or gelled propane instead of water. These technologies have been tested in the US and Canada but are not yet commercially proven at scale. They could dramatically reduce water demand — but would introduce different risks (CO₂ leakage, flammability) and are more expensive than water-based fracking.

The CSIR's conclusion: The 2016 Strategic Environmental Assessment recommended that fracking should not proceed in areas of high water stress and that comprehensive baseline monitoring of groundwater must be in place before any drilling. The PASA/CGS groundwater monitoring work package is intended to provide that baseline — but even with good data, the fundamental question of whether the Karoo has enough water to support a shale gas industry remains unanswered.

Part 7: The Future — The Moratorium Is Lifting. Now What?

With the 2026 moratorium-lifting announcement, South Africa has entered a new chapter in the Karoo shale gas saga — one in which the government is actively pushing to unlock the resource rather than holding it at arm's length. The R48.1-million allocation and Mantashe's rhetoric about ending "over-dependence on imported refined petroleum products" signal that the political will has shifted decisively. But the fundamental obstacles that stalled development for 15 years have not disappeared. They have merely moved from being reasons to wait to being battles that must be fought.

The Government's Case: Energy Security & Economic Growth
  • Petroleum security as "an economic necessity and a national imperative": Mantashe argues that a country with significant mineral and petroleum potential should not remain exposed to external supply shocks. The 2028 Mozambique gas cliff makes this argument timely — domestic shale gas, even a decade away, offers a pathway to energy sovereignty that LNG imports cannot match.
  • Ending "decision paralysis": Former Minister Aucamp framed the moratorium-lifting as breaking a 15-year logjam — moving from endless study to action, while still professing commitment to the constitutional obligation to protect the environment. The R48.1-million allocation puts money behind the rhetoric.
  • Economic multiplier logic: The 2012 Econometrix study's projection of 300,000–700,000 jobs and R80–200 billion in GDP from developing just 5% of the resource remains the government's north star — even though those numbers depend on commercial viability that has not yet been proven.
The Opposition's Case: Precaution, Water & Climate
  • Water — the Karoo's lifeline at stake: The Centre for Environmental Rights (CER) and other groups argue that the water risks remain unresolved. Dr Surina Esterhuyse of the University of the Free State has shown that many Karoo catchments were already experiencing extreme groundwater stress as early as 2016. A single fracking operation uses ~42,500 cubic metres of water — roughly 20 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Just 21 wells would consume the estimated annual volume of water used for livestock watering across the entire Karoo shale gas study area. Between 10% and 30% of fracking fluid returns as produced water containing radioactive materials, heavy metals, and salts. The Karoo's dolerite dykes and sills — the very geological features that host its groundwater — can act as pathways for contaminants to migrate from deep to shallow aquifers.
  • Induced seismicity: Dr Ryan Schultz of the Swiss Seismological Service (ETH Zürich) has concluded the Karoo Basin is "particularly susceptible" to induced seismicity, with "no credible reason to expect that hydraulic fracturing induced seismicity would not occur." The basin exhibits more susceptibility indicators than other basins where such earthquakes have already been recorded.
  • Climate contradiction: Methane's climate impact is more than 80 times stronger than CO₂ over a 20-year timeframe. The International Energy Agency has stated that no new oil or gas fields should have been developed from 2021 onward to avoid catastrophic climate change. Opening a new fossil fuel frontier, argue opponents, is in direct contradiction to South Africa's climate commitments and the Just Energy Transition.
  • Constitutional rights: The CER and other civil society groups argue there is "no justifiable basis" on which the constitutional right to an environment not harmful to health or well-being could be limited by plans for further fossil fuel extraction where less harmful alternatives — renewables, battery storage, green hydrogen — are available.
  • It's not just the Karoo: In Lephalale, Limpopo, an extractives company has applied for environmental authorisation for a large-scale coal-bed methane project that may also use fracking. If the government permits fracking in the Waterberg coalfields while also lifting the Karoo moratorium, opponents warn that South Africa risks normalising a controversial extraction method before key questions about environmental safety, regulation, and climate consistency have been answered.
The Unresolved Questions
  • Regulatory uncertainty: The fracking-specific regulations under the MPRDA have been drafted and redrafted for over a decade but are still not finalised. Until they are, no company can obtain the permits needed to drill and fracture a well. The moratorium-lifting announcement does not, by itself, fix the regulatory gap — it changes the political direction but not the legal framework.
  • Litigation risk: The CER, Treasure the Karoo Action Group, AfriForum, and other organisations have used litigation effectively to stall the process for years. The moratorium-lifting is almost certain to face legal challenge — and South Africa's courts have shown willingness to scrutinise government decisions on environmental grounds. The constitutional right to an environment that is not harmful to health or well-being (Section 24) provides a powerful legal tool for opponents.
  • Water — still the irreducible obstacle: The CSIR's 2016 recommendation that fracking should not proceed in areas of high water stress has not been superseded by any new scientific consensus. The PASA/CGS groundwater monitoring programme will eventually provide the baseline data — but the fundamental question of whether the Karoo has enough water to support a commercial shale gas industry has not been answered. No amount of political will changes the hydrology of a semi-arid region.
  • Gas market timing — the LNG competitor: South Africa faces a "gas cliff" in 2028 when Mozambique's Pande/Temane fields begin to decline. The urgency of that deadline is driving investment toward LNG imports (Coega terminal) — a solution that can be operational in years, not decades. If LNG import infrastructure is built and gas is available at $10–14/MMBtu, Karoo shale gas that costs more than that to produce will not be commercial regardless of how much is in the ground.
  • Infrastructure and capital: Developing a commercial shale gas play requires thousands of wells, gathering pipelines, gas processing plants, compressor stations, and transmission pipelines. The upfront capital cost would be hundreds of billions of rand. No single company can finance that alone — and the supermajors (Shell, Falcon) that once showed interest have largely stepped back while the regulatory uncertainty persisted. Whether the moratorium-lifting is enough to lure them back is an open question.
The fork in the road: The moratorium-lifting of 2026 does not guarantee that Karoo shale gas will be developed — it guarantees that the question will now be decided through active political and legal contestation rather than through studied inaction. One path leads toward the PASA/CGS scientific programme accelerating into exploration drilling, the regulations being finalised, and — perhaps by the mid-2030s — the first commercial production. The other path leads through the courts, where environmental groups will argue that the precautionary principle, the constitutional right to a healthy environment, and South Africa's climate commitments make fracking in a water-scarce region indefensible, particularly when renewable alternatives exist. The next five years will likely determine which path prevails.

The Karoo shale gas is a multi-generational decision. If developed, it would reshape South Africa's energy economy for 50–100 years. If left in the ground — whether by court order, commercial reality, or a global energy transition that renders new fossil fuel developments uneconomic — it remains a stranded asset, 209 Tcf of gas that future generations might access with better technology or might never need. The moratorium-lifting of 2026 has fired the starting gun. Whether the race leads to rigs on the Karoo horizon or to a landmark Constitutional Court judgment shutting the door on fracking in South Africa is now the central question.
