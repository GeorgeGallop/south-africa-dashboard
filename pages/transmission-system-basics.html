🗼 Transmission System Basics

The high-voltage "muscle" of the electricity system. Transmission moves bulk power at 132 kV to 765 kV from where it is generated to where it is consumed — across thousands of kilometres, through some of the largest and most expensive electromechanical equipment in the industrial world. In South Africa, the grid is owned and operated by the NTCSA (National Transmission Company of South Africa), separated from Eskom.

🕵️ ~33,000 km of HV lines
⚡ 765 kV backbone
📈 1,900 MW HVDC import

🤝 What Is the Transmission System?

Electricity moves in three stages. Generation produces power at a power station (up to ~24 kV for a generator). Transmission steps that voltage up to 132–765 kV and moves it long distances with minimal loss. Distribution steps it back down to 11 kV / 400 V / 230 V for homes and businesses. The transmission system is the national highway network of the grid — the distribution network is the local road system.

In South Africa, the Transmission System Operator (TSO) is the NTCSA, created by the unbundling of Eskom under the Electricity Regulation Amendment Act (2024). It owns and operates the entire national high-voltage grid, connects generators (Eskom coal, IPP wind/solar, hydro, nuclear) to the network, and — since the launch of SAWEM — also operates the wholesale electricity market. It is the largest single owner of transmission assets in Africa.

⚡ Generation

Produces electrical power. Generators output at 11–24 kV. Large synchronous machines combine a turbine (steam, gas, hydro, wind) with an alternator. Eskom Generation, IPPs, private embedded.

🎭 Transmission (the focus here)

Steps voltage up (132→400→765 kV) and ships bulk power long distances. Transformers, towers, conductors, switchgear, HVDC. Operated by the NTCSA.

🏠 Distribution

Steps voltage down (132→66→33→11→0.4 kV) and delivers to end users. Eskom Distribution + 160+ municipal distributors. This is where most outages (load shedding) physically occur.

💡 Why high voltage? Power loss in a conductor is proportional to the square of the current (P = I²R). By stepping voltage up 30× (e.g. 11 kV → 400 kV), current drops 30×, and losses drop ~900× for the same power. That's why we transmit at 400 kV and 765 kV rather than just running a thick cable from the power station to the city.

🧰 What Goes Into the Transmission System

The transmission grid is built from a small set of repeating building blocks, each a heavy, specialised, high-value asset. These are the components and what they do.

🏗️ The backbone
Overhead Lines & Towers

What: Steel lattice towers holding ACSR conductors (Aluminium Conductor Steel Reinforced) on glass/ceramic/polymer insulators.

What it does: Carries bulk power. A single 400 kV circuit can move ~1,000–1,400 MW; a 765 kV line up to ~2,500 MW. SA is one of the few countries in the world using 765 kV.

Cost: ~R8–15 M/km for a 400 kV line (towers, conductor, insulators), plus land and civil works.

⚡ Most expensive single asset
Power Transformers

What: Static electromagnetic devices that change voltage between circuits. Step-up transformers at generation, auto-transformers coupling 400/275/132 kV levels, step-down at substations.

What it does: The heart of every substation. A single 400 kV auto-transformer can handle ~500–1,000 MVA (enough for a mid-sized city).

Cost: R150–400 M each. Global lead times blew out to 3–5 years during the 2022–24 supply crunch.

🏮 The hub
Substations & Switchgear

What: A substation is the node where circuits meet — busbars, circuit breakers, disconnectors, instrument transformers, surge arresters. GIS (gas-insulated, SF6) or AIS (air-insulated).

What it does: Routes, protects, and switches the network. Circuit breakers interrupt fault currents in milliseconds; disconnectors provide visible isolation for maintenance.

Cost: A full 400 kV GIS substation can cost R500 M–R1.5 B; a single 400 kV breaker ~R10–30 M.

🔧 Voltage control
Reactive Power Compensation

What: Shunt reactors (absorb MVAr), shunt capacitors (inject MVAr), and dynamic devices — STATCOMs and SVCs (Static Var Compensators).

What it does: Keeps voltage within limits. Long lightly-loaded lines "charge up" (Ferranti effect) and need reactors; heavily-loaded lines sag and need capacitors. STATCOMs can swing both ways in milliseconds.

Cost: SH reactors R20–80 M; STATCOM/SVC R150–400 M per installation.

🌟 Throughput
Series Compensation

What: Series capacitors installed in series with a transmission line, plus metal-oxide varistors (MOVs) and by-pass breakers for protection.

What it does: Cancels part of the line's inductive reactance, effectively shortening it. This raises the power transfer limit of a long line by 30–50% without building a new line. Widely used on SA's long 400 kV and 765 kV interconnectors.

Cost: ~R40–100 M per series capacitor bank.

🧠 The brain
Protection, Control & SCADA

What: Numerical relays (distance, differential, overcurrent), SCADA/EMS (Supervisory Control & Data Acquisition / Energy Management System) in the National Control Centre, and communication networks.

What it does: Detects faults in ~50–80 milliseconds and trips the right breakers to isolate a faulted line, protecting equipment and people. SCADA lets operators monitor and control the whole national grid from one room.

Cost: Relays R1–5 M per bay; a National Control Centre upgrade can run to hundreds of millions.

🚀 Cross-border
HVDC Converter Stations

What: High-Voltage Direct Current systems convert AC→DC→AC to move power over very long distances or between asynchronous grids. In SA this is the Apollo converter station (near Johannesburg) receiving from Cahora Bassa in Mozambique.

What it does: The ±533 kV, ~1,400 km HVDC line was the longest in the world when built, delivering up to 1,920 MW into the SA grid from the Zambezi hydro complex.

Cost: Converter stations run to R2–4 B+ — among the most capital-intensive transmission assets ever built.

📡 The nervous system
Telecommunication

What: OPGW (Optical Ground Wire) embedded in the earth wire of transmission towers, plus microwave links, PLC (power-line carrier), and fibre.

What it does: Carries the protection signalling, SCADA data, and voice between substations and the National Control Centre. Reliable telecom is critical — protection schemes cannot operate without it.

Cost: OP GW ~R1–2 M per km installed; part of every new line and substation build.

💰 What's the Most Expensive?

Ranked by single-asset cost (excluding civil works), the transmission "big-ticket" items are clear:

⚠️ The #1 single asset: the big power transformer. A large 400 kV auto-transformer — the component that couples the entire transmission voltage ladder together — is the priciest discrete piece of equipment in the grid, at R150–400 million each. Anything more expensive is a full system (an HVDC converter station, a GIS substation) rather than a single device. Transformer lead times were the single biggest bottleneck in the global energy transition, stretching to 3–5 years at the 2022–24 peak.
⏰ Lead Times — and Why They Matter

Lead time is the elapsed time between placing an order and receiving the equipment — it does not include design, civil works, installation, or commissioning. Lead times exploded globally from 2022 to 2024 as the energy transition, data-centre demand, and post-COVID supply chains collided. They are now easing but remain far above pre-2020 norms.

✅ What this means for South Africa. The NTCSA's grid expansion and connection-queue work — needed to connect the ~19,600 MW of new private capacity — is serially constrained by transformer and switchgear lead times. A project that needs a new 400 kV substation realistically takes 4–6 years from concept to energisation. This is why contemporary planning emphasises: (1) ordering equipment early (long-lead procurement), (2) reusing existing corridors, and (3) cable / underground options where overhead routing is blocked.

🏭 Who Makes It? The Manufacturers

Transmission equipment is a highly consolidated, global oligopoly. A handful of OEMs dominate, with a thin tier of specialised component suppliers beneath them. South Africa has meaningful local content in some segments but imports nearly all of the heaviest, most valuable items.

⚡ Power Transformers

Global leaders: Hitachi Energy (formerly ABB), Siemens Energy, GE Vernova, CG Power, Hyundai Electric, Mitsubishi Electric, Toshiba, Prolec GE, WEG, BHEL.
South African local content: ACTOM (largest local transformer maker, Johannesburg) and smaller specialist shops. Historically a strong local base, now concentrated in lower-voltage and distribution units.

🏮 Switchgear & GIS

Global leaders: Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy, GE Vernova, Schneider Electric, Mitsubishi Electric, Toshiba.
South African local content: ACTOM switchgear, local assembly/panel-building for LV and some MV, but 400 kV GIS is almost entirely imported from Europe/Asia.

🏗️ Conductors & Cables

Global leaders: Prysmian, Nexans, Sumitomo, Southwire, CTC Global (ACCC), General Cable.
South African local content: Aberdare Cables (SA) produces ACSR and XLPE cable locally; SA also has strong aluminium-industry ties (Hulamin) that supply the metal.

🔧 Reactive Power & HVDC

STATCOM/SVC: Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy, GE Vernova, Mitsubishi Electric. HVDC: Hitachi Energy (historic technology leader on Cahora Bassa), Siemens Energy, GE Vernova, Mitsubishi. Synchronous condensers: GE, Siemens, WEG, Andritz.
South African local content: minimal — these are bespoke imported systems.

🧠 Protection, Relays & SCADA

Global leaders: Siemens, Hitachi Energy, GE, SEL (Schweitzer), Schneider Electric.
South African local content: local engineering/integration, panel manufacturing, and SCADA deployment — the National Control Centre uses internationally-sourced EMS platforms integrated by local teams.

🏘️ Towers, Insulators & Steelwork

Towers: local SA steel fabricators plus international suppliers (Valmont, Sabre). Insulators: NGK Insulators (Japan), Lapp, Seves, Sediver (glass), CTC Global.
South African local content: significant — SA has a strong structural steel and galvanising industry that fabricates lattice towers and substation steel locally.

💡 Localisation reality-check. South Africa can fabricate tower steel, ACSR conductor, and lower-voltage switchgear locally. But the heavy, high-value items — large power transformers, 400 kV GIS, HVDC converters, STATCOMs — are all imported. That makes the country exposed to global lead times, currency swings, and a small number of foreign suppliers. Local manufacturing capacity exists but is concentrated at the lower end of the voltage ladder.

🌎 Transmission & the Energy Transition

Transmission is the quiet bottleneck of the energy transition. You can build all the solar and wind you want — but without transmission capacity to move it, it cannot reach the load.

📈 Connection Queue

The NTCSA's Generation Connection Capacity Assessment (GCCA) repeatedly flags limited available capacity on key 400 kV corridors. Thousands of megawatts of private and IPP generation are queued, waiting for either a new line or network reinforcement. The grid, not generation, is often the binding constraint.

⚡ Renewable Hotspots

SA's best solar and wind resources are in the Northern Cape, North West, and Western Cape — far from the coal-belt load centres of Gauteng and Mpumalanga. Moving that renewable energy requires long 400 kV and 765 kV lines, which take the longest to build and face the hardest environmental and land-access hurdles.

🛡️ Cable Theft

Copper and aluminium theft — including from transmission lines and substations — is a chronic operational burden. The NTCSA has spent hundreds of millions on security, aluminium-replacement, and surveillance. It is one of the most under-appreciated threats to transmission reliability in South Africa.

🚀 Long-Lead Procurement

Because transformers and switchgear take 2–4 years, the NTCSA must place orders years ahead of when capacity is actually needed. This is why modern planning is as much about supply-chain management as about power-system engineering — and why early ordering is the single most effective de-risking lever.
