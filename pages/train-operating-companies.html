🚂 Train Operating Companies (TOCs)

Who Gets Access to the Network, What They Carry, and How the Slot-Allocation Machine Decides — South Africa's Rail Monopoly Officially Ends

On 13 May 2026, the Transnet Rail Infrastructure Manager (TRIM) signed formal Rail Access Agreements (RAAs) with 11 private Train Operating Companies — the moment South Africa's single-operator rail monopoly ended and a competitive, multi-operator freight rail market was born. The network, which for 130+ years was the exclusive preserve of the state (SAR&H → SATS → Transnet Freight Rail), is now a shared resource: 12 operators (TFROC + 11 private TOCs) running trains across five strategic corridors. This page details the whole process — who the operators are, who does what, how the "who gets access to move what when" optimization actually works, and where the reform goes next.

Part 1: Who Is Doing What — The Cast of the New Rail Market

The old model had one player: Transnet owned everything and did everything. The new model separates the roles structurally, so that the entity which owns the tracks cannot also favour itself when allocating access.

🛤️ TRIM — The Infrastructure Manager (The Referee)

Role: Owns, manages, operates and maintains the national rail network (~20,000+ km of track, signalling, stations). Allocates train paths (slots) to operators on non-discriminatory, published terms.

  • Publishes the Network Statement (the rulebook + tariff schedule)
  • Runs the annual slot application cycle and the Ad Hoc Slot process
  • Collects access fees (two-part tariff: per train-km + per gross-ton-km)
  • Maximises network utilisation & drives road-to-rail modal shift

Does NOT: operate trains, own terminals, or carry cargo. CE: Moshe Motlohi.

🚂 TFROC — The Incumbent Operator (Now One Among Many)

Role: Transnet Freight Rail Operating Company — the train-operating side of the old TFR. Runs coal, iron ore, manganese, chrome, container, and general freight trains.

  • Must apply to TRIM for slots like everyone else — no privileged access
  • Remains the largest operator (~160+ Mtpa of the ~180 Mtpa baseline)
  • Performance now measured against private competitors

The key change: TFROC no longer controls the track it runs on. The referee (TRIM) is structurally separate.

⚖️ The Regulator — IRERC (now) → Transport Economic Regulator (soon)

Role: The independent referee's referee. Sets access tariff rules, monitors non-discriminatory access, investigates complaints, resolves disputes between TRIM and operators.

  • IRERC (Interim Rail Economic Regulatory Capacity) — interim body that oversaw the draft Network Statement & tariff methodology
  • Transport Economic Regulator — permanent body created by the Economic Regulation of Transport Act (2024); fully operational target: late 2025

Legal basis: Economic Regulation of Transport Act (2024).

🛡️ Railway Safety Regulator (RSR)

Role: Independent safety regulator. Every TOC must hold an RSR permit before it can run a single train.

  • Permit = proof that rolling stock, crews, and operating procedures meet safety standards
  • One of the key "conditions" in the conditional award letters issued to TOCs in Aug 2025
  • Levels the safety playing field — private operators cannot cut corners

Also in the picture: Dept of Transport (policy & gazetting), National Treasury (BFI funding), Minister of Transport Barbara Creecy (political principal).

Part 2: The 11 Train Operating Companies — Who They Are

Selected in August 2025 from 25 applicants, formalised on 13 May 2026. They span coal, manganese, containers, fuel, and general freight — with iron ore added via the corridors.

⛏️ Menar Ports & Rail (MPR)

Allocation: 8.6 Mtpa — the largest single private allocation.

Coal, manganese and ferromanganese "from pit to port" for its own mining products and other operations. Already procuring locomotives and wagons. Part of the Menar family (Menar is a diversified mining group with Eastside Coal & Kangra).

📦 Grindrod

Profile: JSE-listed, one of SA's most diversified logistics groups (ports, terminals, shipping, road).

Brings institutional capital-market credibility to the operator cohort. Well positioned for container and general freight on the Container Corridor.

🚢 TLD Marine (with MSC)

Profile: MSC — the world's largest container shipping line — gains rail access via its partnership with TLD Marine.

Creates a direct link between the world's biggest maritime logistics group and SA's terrestrial freight network — a major vote of confidence in the reform.

🇦🇪 ARC South Africa

Profile: UAE-based African Rail Company's SA arm.

Signed after "two years of intensive and rigorous preparation" with TRIM. Declared "the regulatory phase is complete. It is time to rail!" — signalling international investor appetite beyond domestic players.

⛽ Sharp Logistics

Fuel and general freight carrier. Serves the inland fuel distribution network that Transnet Pipelines alone cannot fully cover — a key energy-security role.

🚃 Barberry

Profile: Holdings group active in rolling stock.

In May 2026 awarded Indian firm Texmaco a contract for 30 diesel locomotives and 2,235+ wagons — the "milestone" order that will power its SA rail entry.

🪨 Minrail

Minerals-focused operator — coal, chrome, magnetite on the North and North-East Corridors. Built to serve mining houses stuck behind Transnet's poor export performance.

🌍 IRACEMA

Multi-commodity operator (coal, containers, general freight). One of the smaller, focused entrants targeting specific corridors where it can win volume.

🚛 Motheo Logistics

South African logistics group extending from road into rail — representative of the "road-to-rail" modal shift the National Rail Policy explicitly targets.

🚂 The Railway Corporation

General freight operator taking advantage of open access to run trains on the mainstream network.

🔗 Interlinks

Multi-sector operator covering containers and general freight — part of the diverse cohort now competing for network slots.

➕ Also in the market: Traxtion

Profile: Africa's largest private rail company; R3.4bn rolling stock programme; first 8 locomotives landed Durban Aug 2026.

Traxtion secured access for general freight on the Durban–Gauteng corridor and is building a Pretoria maintenance hub. The most capital-committed entrant alongside the 11.

Part 3: How the Optimization Works — "Who Gets Access to Move What, When"

This is the heart of the system: a rules-based capacity allocation machine designed to get the maximum economic value out of a scarce, decaying national asset. It replaces the old model where Transnet simply decided everything internally.

Step-by-step: from application to train on the track
Step 1 — The Network Statement sets the rules

TRIM publishes the Network Statement: available capacity, route specifications (line speeds, axle loads, train lengths), access charges, and the application process. Published volumes: V1 (Dec 2024), V2/V3 (Feb 2025 engagement), and V4 (finalising mid-2026). It's updated yearly or every two years. This is the rulebook — comprehensive and published in advance.

Step 2 — The slot application cycle opens

Applications for the first tranche opened 20 December 2024 (the day the Network Statement was gazetted) and closed 27 February 2025. A 60-day adjudication period followed. For the 2026/27 timetable, applications open under V4. Any licensed operator can apply — domestic or international (hence UAE's ARC).

Step 3 — Adjudication: capacity is allocated on published criteria

TRIM scored 25 applications against transparent criteria: operator financial strength, rolling stock readiness, safety compliance (RSR permit pathway), cargo demand (the "what"), and corridor fit (the "where"). 11 met the bar. Minister Creecy: "The evaluation process has been rigorous and was conducted in full compliance with the standards of fairness and transparency."

Step 4 — Conditional allocation over 41 routes on 6 corridors

August 2025: conditional award letters issued with conditions — RSR permit applications, rolling stock readiness, port offloading capacity, operational processes. Allocations ranged 1–10 years. Operators can start as soon as conditions are met.

Step 5 — Rail Access Agreements signed (13 May 2026)

Formal RAAs concluded with all 11 TOCs. Onboarding and operational readiness support begins. 24 Mtpa of new capacity unlocked, scaling to 52 Mtpa over five years.

Step 6 — Ad Hoc Slots for real-time flexibility (Dec 2025+)

TRIM's Ad Hoc Slot process allows rapid allocation of capacity outside the annual cycle — operators can respond to real-time market demand. First application: a short-haul Cato Ridge–Durban service to cut port-precinct truck congestion (targeted start May 2026).

Step 7 — Run trains, pay access fees, get monitored

Operators run trains on allocated slots, paying the two-part tariff (per train-km + per gross-ton-km, differentiated by commodity and corridor). The Regulator monitors non-discriminatory access, and the RSR ensures safety. Unused slots revert to the pool for reallocation — use-it-or-lose-it discipline.

Part 4: The Optimization Logic — How "Optimal" Is Defined

1. Maximise tonnes per scarce path

Slots are scarce because the network can only carry ~180 Mtpa today — below the ~280 Mtpa market demand. The allocation therefore favours heavy-haul bulk (coal, iron ore, manganese, chrome) that moves the most tonnage per slot, and high-value commodities with export revenue attached. Every slot wasted on an under-filled train is export revenue lost.

2. Target the corridors with the biggest revenue at stake

The first allocations concentrate on lines where the economy bleeds most: manganese to Ngqura, chrome to Richards Bay, coal to RBCT, iron ore to Saldanha, containers on Durban–Gauteng. These are the corridors where Transnet's failure cost R200bn in lost mining export revenue in 2023 alone (TRIM CE Motlohi). Private capacity is aimed where the gap hurts most.

3. Differentiated pricing to steer behaviour

The two-part tariff (per train-km + per gross-ton-km) is differentiated by commodity type and corridor. The Regulator sets maximum tariffs, so TRIM cannot gouge. Pricing signals steer operators toward efficient train configurations (longer, heavier trains earn more per fixed slot cost) and toward corridors the policy wants developed.

4. Rules-based, transparent, appealable

Allocation decisions are published with reasons. Denied applicants can appeal to the Regulator — not to Transnet. This is the anti-corruption and anti-discrimination guarantee: the old system's opacity (and the Zondo Commission's findings on how opaque allocation enabled looting) is structurally eliminated.

5. Use-it-or-lose-it discipline

A TOC that doesn't run its allocated slots forfeits them — freeing capacity for others that will. The Ad Hoc process adds a fast cycle for catching real-time demand, so unused capacity is recycled instead of hoarded. This keeps the scarce resource moving toward operators who actually perform.

6. The end goal: modal shift

Every train shifted from road to rail removes ~50 trucks from the N3 / national roads. Target: rail volumes 180 → 250 Mtpa by 2030. The optimization isn't just about rail's internal efficiency — it's about cutting SA's ~11–12% of GDP logistics costs, road damage, emissions and congestion, and restoring export competitiveness.

Part 5: How the Operators Pay — Access Tariffs & the LeaseCo

💵 Access Tariffs — the price of a slot

Two-part structure introduced in Network Statement V1 (Dec 2024):

  • Charge per train-km — covers TRIM's operating costs
  • Charge per gross-ton-km — recovers infrastructure wear-and-tear (heavier trains = more track damage = higher charge)

Differentiated by commodity and corridor. Tariffs are subject to regulatory oversight (IRERC now, Transport Economic Regulator later). The revenue funds TRIM's operations and, in the target model, contributes to maintenance and expansion. Design tension: high enough to fund catch-up maintenance (R65bn needed over 5 years — Transnet can only fund half), low enough not to choke the road-to-rail shift.

🚃 LeaseCo — removing the capital barrier

Rolling stock is the single biggest cost of entry (locomotives: ~R40m+ each). To stop capital-crushing smaller operators before they start, Transnet is establishing LeaseCo, a public-private rolling stock leasing company.

  • 500 locomotives + 17,000 wagons set aside into the entity
  • Leases to both domestic TOCs and Southern African regional operators
  • 5 TOCs have already signed agreements that transfer to LeaseCo once it's formally established (RFP imminent)

This shares the capital cost across the market and enables smaller operators to compete with incumbents who already own fleets.

Part 7: Risks & Obstacles

  • Infrastructure decay: Motlohi is blunt — TOCs enter "at their own risk" a neglected network. R65bn is needed over five years for catch-up investment; Transnet can only fund ~half. Private operators cannot run fast/heavy trains on decaying track.
  • Funding gap: TRIM is seeking fiscal support (SANRAL-style model), customer co-investment, and Budget Facility for Infrastructure (BFI) access. R16.8bn already approved for coal/iron-ore/port networks; R23.6bn in further applications.
  • Cable theft & security: The network's physical security remains a precondition for reliable private operations. Vandalism risk doesn't disappear because the operator is private.
  • Cream-skimming: Private operators will target high-volume, high-margin corridors. The regulator must ensure low-density rural lines aren't abandoned — via pricing design or explicit subsidies.
  • Transitional conflict-of-interest: TRIM is still inside the Transnet group (the "sibling" problem). Until full legal separation, there's a risk of soft capture — the referee favouring the house team (TFROC). The independent Transport Economic Regulator is the counterweight.
  • Operational readiness: TOCs must pass RSR safety permitting, secure rolling stock, and line up port capacity. Some may fail to launch — the market will reallocate their slots.
  • Execution risk at scale: 52 Mtpa from private operators is the ambition. Delivering it requires locomotives, wagons, crews, and above all track that works. The gap between 180 and 250 Mtpa remains the national challenge.
The bigger picture: why this matters

South Africa's freight rail system was moving ~160–165 Mtpa against market demand of ~280 Mtpa — the gap being absorbed by trucks, at enormous cost to roads, fuel use, emissions, and export competitiveness. Moody's explicitly cited logistics reform as credit-positive, noting private participation in rail and ports "is expected to support mining and manufacturing output materially." The 13 May 2026 signing converts policy into operational reality. The model is open access, not privatisation: the network stays publicly owned; what changed is that competitive operators can now use it. Whether the 250 Mtpa target is hit by 2030 depends on operators executing and Transnet (via TRIM) maintaining and funding the network — but the governance architecture to make it happen is now in place.
