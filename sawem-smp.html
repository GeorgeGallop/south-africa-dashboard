System Marginal Price (SMP)

The SMP is the cornerstone of pricing in the South African Wholesale Electricity Market (SAWEM). This page provides an in-depth treatment—from the fundamental unconstrained merit-order intuition, through the full constrained co-optimisation formulation, to the settlement mechanics and the specific legal provisions of the Market Code.

1) What Is the System Marginal Price?

Short definition

The System Marginal Price (SMP) is the shadow price (Lagrange multiplier) of the energy-balance constraint in the market-clearing optimisation. It represents the marginal cost of serving one additional MWh of demand at the least-cost dispatch solution—economically, it is the offer price of the most expensive generator that must run to satisfy demand.

Why it matters

All dispatched generators in the Day-Ahead Market are paid the same SMP for their energy output (pay-as-clear). This creates the incentive for every generator to bid at its true marginal cost—if it bids above marginal cost it risks not being dispatched; if it bids below marginal cost it risks being dispatched at a loss. The SMP is also the reference price for the Intraday Market, the Balancing Mechanism, reserve payments, and the CPA vesting-contract hedge.

SMP

System Marginal Price — the single price per Settlement Period for energy in the DAM.

Pay-as-Clear

All accepted generators receive the SMP, not their individual bid. This is the standard wholesale market model.

Settlement Period

The time granularity for pricing (typically 1 hour in SAWEM Phase 1, moving to 30 minutes).

2) Unconstrained Dispatch — The Merit-Order Foundation

The simplest dispatch problem ignores all network and operational constraints except the energy balance. Generators are stacked in ascending offer-price order (the merit order), and the SMP is the offer price of the marginal unit: the most expensive generator whose output is needed to meet demand. Every generator with a lower bid is dispatched fully (or to its maximum) before the marginal unit is called. Every generator with a higher bid is not dispatched.

Unconstrained optimisation problem
minpi  Σi  Ci(pi)

subject to:
  Σi pi = D                        [energy balance]
  pi,min ≤ pi ≤ pi,max          [generator limits]
Lagrangian & first-order condition (KKT)
ℒ = Σi Ci(pi) − λ·(Σi pi − D)
  + Σi μi,min·(pi,min − pi)
  + Σi μi,max·(pi − pi,max)

∂ℒ/∂pi = C'i(pi) − λ − μi,min + μi,max = 0

At optimality for unconstrained marginal units (μi=0):
  C'i(pi*) = λ   ∀ i marginal
  ∴ λ = SMP
λ (lambda)

The Lagrange multiplier on the energy-balance constraint. At the unconstrained optimum, λ equals the marginal cost of the most expensive dispatched generator. This is the SMP in the unconstrained case.

μi,min, μi,max

Multipliers on generator-capacity limits. When a generator is at its minimum output, μi,min ≥ 0; at its maximum, μi,max ≥ 0. For generators between limits (marginal units), both are zero.

Key insight: In the unconstrained case, all marginal generators "see" the same marginal value λ. This is why unconstrained prices are uniform across the system. The SMP is simply λ, and every dispatched generator receives it.

3) Constrained Dispatch — Adding Network and Operating Limits

In a real power system, the dispatch must also respect transmission network limits (thermal ratings of lines and transformers, voltage stability limits, interface transfer limits) and operational constraints (ramp rates, minimum up/down times, reserve requirements, fuel limits). When these constraints bind, the SMP is no longer a single uniform value driven purely by the merit order—it now contains scarcity premiums from active constraints. However, SAWEM’s current market design clears the DAM with a single national Pricing Area, meaning all generators and loads receive the same SMP even though the underlying constrained dispatch may violate network limits. Congestion is instead managed through lost opportunity payments and constrained-on payments (see Section 5).

Full constrained optimisation (SCUC/SCED)
min Σt [ Σi Ci(pi,t)
          + Σj SUj·uj,t
          + Σj NLj·xj,t ]

subject to (∀ t):
  Σi pi,t = Dt                     [balance]
  Σi ri,tk ≥ Rtk  ∀ k           [reserve req.]
  fℓ,t(p) ≤ Fℓ,max                    [line limits]
  pi,min·xi,t ≤ pi,t ≤ pi,max·xi,t  [unit limits]
  −RDi ≤ pi,t−pi,t-1 ≤ RUi  [ramp rates]
  xi,t ∈ {0,1}                               [commitment]
  ui,t ≥ xi,t−xi,t-1               [start-up indicator]
  Σ xi,τ ≥ ···                              [min up/down]
SMP from constrained solution
At the constrained optimum:

SMP = λ*   (the multiplier on the energy-balance
            constraint at hour t)

Where λ* is the derivative of the total cost
function with respect to an increment of demand,
accounting for all binding constraints.

In a single Pricing Area (uniform SMP), λ* is still
a single scalar — but its value is influenced
by network constraints because the optimisation
must find a feasible dispatch under those limits.

If multiple Pricing Areas or LMP were implemented,
the price at each location n would be:

  pricen = λ + Σℓ SFℓ,n·μℓ

where μℓ is the multiplier on line ℓ
and SFℓ,n is the shift factor.

3.1) Key Constraint Types in the SAWEM Optimisation

Energy balance

Σ pi = D. The defining constraint. Its multiplier λ is the SMP.

Reserve requirements

Co-optimised with energy. Several reserve products (regulating up/down, 10-min spinning, 10-min non-spinning, 30-min supplemental). Each has a multiplier μk indicating the marginal value of that reserve.

Unit-commitment constraints

Minimum generation, minimum up/down times, start-up costs, ramp rates. These create intertemporal coupling—the SMP in hour t depends on decisions made for hour t−1.

Network (line) constraints

fℓ(p) ≤ Fℓ,max. In SAWEM Phase 1 these are not reflected in the SMP—instead, the unconstrained SMP is used and network violations are handled via constrained-on/off payments.

SAWEM-specific: The Market Code mandates a two-step process: first solve the Unconstrained Schedule (no network limits) to determine the SMP, then solve the Constrained Schedule (with network limits) to identify which generators must be curtailed or started to respect the network. This is a deliberate design choice— it avoids locational price differences while still achieving a feasible physical dispatch. The cost of this approach is that generators constrained off are compensated via lost opportunity payments (Section 5), and generators constrained on receive their bid price instead of the SMP.

4) KKT Conditions — The Mathematical Anatomy of SMP

The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions provide the complete set of necessary conditions for optimality in the constrained optimisation. Understanding them reveals exactly why the SMP takes the value it does and which constraints are driving price formation.

General constrained problem (single period, for simplicity)
min  f(p)  =  Σi Ci(pi)

s.t.  h(p)  =  Σi pi − D = 0          (equality: energy balance)
      gk(p) ≤ 0   ∀ k                  (inequality: network + operating limits)
KKT conditions
1. Stationarity:
   ∇f(p*) + λ·∇h(p*) + Σ μk·∇gk(p*) = 0

2. Primal feasibility:
   h(p*) = 0,  gk(p*) ≤ 0

3. Dual feasibility:
   μk ≥ 0

4. Complementary slackness:
   μk·gk(p*) = 0

4.1) What Each KKT Condition Means for SMP

1

Stationarity and the SMP formula

Expanding the stationarity condition for each generator i: 

C'i(pi*) − λ + Σk μk·∂gk/∂pi = 0

Rearranging gives the SMP for any generator that is marginal (between limits): 

SMP = λ = C'i(pi*) + Σk μk·∂gk/∂pi

If no network/operating constraints are active (μk = 0 ∀ k), this reduces to λ = C'i(pi*)—the unconstrained result. When constraints bind (μk > 0), the SMP includes a congestion premium that reflects the marginal cost of respecting those constraints.

2

Primal feasibility

The dispatch p* must actually balance supply and demand (h=0) and respect every line limit, ramp rate, reserve requirement, and generator bound (gk ≤ 0). The optimisation finds the cheapest feasible dispatch, not just the cheapest merit-order dispatch.

3

Dual feasibility

Shadow prices on “≤” constraints are non-negative. A positive μk means that relaxing constraint k by a small amount would reduce total cost. This is the marginal value of increasing transfer capability, reserve requirement, or any binding limit.

4

Complementary slackness

μk·gk(p*) = 0. If a constraint is not binding (gk < 0), its multiplier μk must be zero. If a multiplier is positive, the constraint must be tight (gk = 0). This is what we mean when we say “active constraints have non-zero shadow prices.”

Bottom line from KKT: The SMP is not “the highest accepted bid”— that is only true in the trivial unconstrained case. In the full constrained problem, the SMP is the marginal cost of serving demand while respecting every physical and operational limit. Its value is determined by the interaction of all binding constraints, not by any single generator’s bid.

5) Lost Opportunity Payments & Constrained-On Payments

Because SAWEM uses a single national SMP but respects network constraints in the dispatch, there will be generators that are constrained off (reduced below their unconstrained dispatch to respect a line limit) and generators that are constrained on (started or increased above their unconstrained dispatch to support the network). These generators must be compensated to remain financially whole.

Constrained-off (constrained-down) payment

A generator that was dispatched in the Unconstrained Schedule but is instructed to reduce output in the Constrained Schedule loses the profit it would have earned at the SMP.

Lost opportunity payment
Payment = (SMP − BidPrice) × Volumeconstrained-off

The generator is left indifferent between the constrained dispatch and the unconstrained outcome—it receives the SMP on its actual (reduced) output plus the lost opportunity payment making up the difference.

Constrained-on payment

A generator that was not dispatched in the Unconstrained Schedule but is required to run in the Constrained Schedule would lose money if paid only the SMP (since its bid price is above the SMP).

Constrained-on payment
Payment = (BidPrice − SMP) × Volumeconstrained-on

Alternatively, the generator may be paid its bid price for the constrained-on volume. The generator is made whole for the loss incurred by running when the SMP is below its costs.

Who pays? The constrained-on/off payments are recovered through the settlement system as uplift charges allocated to all BRPs pro rata based on their net energy volume. These uplift charges are the “cost of congestion” in a single-Pricing-Area market—in an LMP market, these costs would instead appear as locational price differences.

6) Co-optimisation of Energy and Reserves

A distinguishing feature of SAWEM is that the Day-Ahead Market clearing solves for both energy and reserve schedules simultaneously. This is fundamentally different from sequential markets (e.g., European-style where the energy market clears first and reserves are procured separately). Co-optimisation produces the globally least-cost solution.

Co-optimised objective
min  Σi [ Ci(pi) + Cires(ri) ]

subject to:
  Σi pi = D              → λ (SMP)
  Σi rik ≥ Rk     → μk (reserve shadow price)
  pi + ri ≤ pi,max   → γi (capacity allocation)
  pi − ri ≥ pi,min   → (minimum generation)
First-order conditions
For energy:
  C'i(pi*) − λ − γi = 0

For reserves:
  C'ires(ri*) − μk − γi = 0

At the optimum, for a unit providing both:
  λ − μk = C'i(pi*) − C'ires(ri*)

This is the “opportunity cost” of
reserving capacity instead of selling energy.
γi (capacity shadow price)

The multiplier on the constraint pi + ri ≤ pi,max. When a generator is at its capacity limit, γi > 0, indicating that the capacity is scarce—increasing the unit’s maximum output would reduce total cost.

Reserve shadow price μk

The marginal cost of meeting an additional MW of reserve requirement k. When μk is high, reserves are expensive and the system would benefit from more fast-ramping capacity. These values inform the System Operator’s reserve adequacy assessment.

Why co-optimisation matters for SMP: The SMP (λ) is determined jointly with reserve prices (μk) because the same generator capacity is shared between energy and reserves. If reserve requirements are high, some cheap generators must hold capacity back from the energy market, which pushes the SMP up (more expensive generators must run to meet energy demand). This interaction is invisible in sequential markets.

7) SMP Across Market Layers

The SMP is not confined to the Day-Ahead Market. It is the reference price that flows through every layer of the SAWEM multi-market model, creating consistent price signals across time horizons.

Day-Ahead Market

  • SMP determined from market clearing (SCUC/SCED)
  • All energy settled at SMP (pay-as-clear)
  • Constrained-on/off payments at bid price or SMP ± spread

Intraday Market

  • Same SMP methodology as DAM
  • Gate closure 1 hour before delivery
  • Corrections/revisions to DAM schedules settled at intraday SMP

Balancing Mechanism

  • Imbalance priced relative to DAM SMP
  • System Buy Price (SBP) = SMP × penalty factor (≥1.1)
  • System Sell Price (SSP) = SMP × penalty factor (≤0.9)
  • Asymmetric to incentivise schedule accuracy

Reserve & CPA

  • Reserve providers paid SMP for reserve MWh + lost opportunity cost
  • CPA’s energy revenue = SMP × vesting-contract volume
  • CPA’s hedge settlement (SMP − reference price) × volume

8) SMP and the Central Purchasing Agency

The Central Purchasing Agency (CPA) is the single largest participant in the DAM. It is the BRP for all Eskom Generation stations (via vesting contracts) and all legacy IPP PPAs. The SMP therefore directly determines the CPA’s financial position, which in turn determines the legacy charge paid by consumers.

CPA energy revenue

RevenueCPA = Σh SMPh × VolumeCPA,h

The CPA sells all contracted energy into the DAM at the SMP. Higher SMP means higher CPA revenue and a lower legacy charge.

Eskom vesting-contract hedge

CPA pays Eskom Gen:
  AvailabilityPayment + CapacityPayment
  + HedgePayment

HedgePayment = δ·(ReferencePrice − SMP) × Volume

where δ = hedge ratio (100% → 0% over 5 years)

When SMP rises above the reference price, the hedge payment to Eskom becomes negative (Eskom pays the CPA). This prevents Eskom Generation from profiting from high SMP caused by its own market power.

Market power mitigation: The declining hedge is the core mechanism preventing Eskom Generation’s ~70% market share from translating into sustained high SMP. In Year 1, Eskom is hedged at 100% of the reference price—it is indifferent to the SMP. By Year 5, Eskom is fully exposed to the SMP, by which time the market should have enough competing generation to discipline prices.

9) Market Code Provisions — Sections Governing SMP

The SMP is defined and governed by the SAWEM Market Code (Rev 2.3, Feb 2026). Below are the key sections, paraphrased for clarity. The full authority flows from the Electricity Regulation Amendment Act 38 of 2024 through the Market Code to the Market Operator’s daily operations.

Section Provision
§3.5 Market clearing algorithm. The Market Operator must run a co-optimised SCUC/SCED that determines the Unconstrained Schedule and the Constrained Schedule. The algorithm must minimise total bid costs including start-up costs, no-load costs, and incremental energy costs, subject to the energy balance, reserve requirements, unit-commitment constraints, and network security constraints.
§3.6.1 SMP determination. The Market Operator must determine the System Marginal Price for each Settlement Period as the Lagrange multiplier (“shadow price”) of the energy balance constraint in the market optimisation.
§3.6.2 Pay-as-clear settlement. All Energy accepted in the Day-Ahead Market shall be settled at the SMP for the relevant Settlement Period.
§3.6.3 Scarcity pricing. If no marginal unit can be identified (e.g., system-wide scarcity where all available generation is dispatched and demand exceeds supply), the SMP is set to the Value of Lost Load (VOLL) as determined by NERSA.
§3.7 Lost opportunity payments. A generator constrained off shall receive (SMP − its bid price) × the volume constrained off. This ensures the generator is indifferent to the constraint.
§3.8 Constrained-on payments. A generator constrained on shall receive its bid price for the constrained-on volume, or the SMP plus a make-whole payment, as specified in Schedule C.
§5.5 Balancing Mechanism prices. The System Buy Price (SBP) and System Sell Price (SSP) are derived from the SMP with penalty factors to incentivise schedule accuracy. SBP ≥ 1.1 × SMP; SSP ≤ 0.9 × SMP.
Sch. C Full mathematical formulation. Schedule C to the Market Code provides the complete mathematical formulation of the optimisation problem, including the objective function, all constraints, and the definition of the SMP as the dual variable of the energy balance constraint.
Legal force: The Market Code has the force of law under Section 14H of the ERA Amendment Act. Any contravention of its SMP provisions is an offence punishable by a fine or imprisonment not exceeding five years.

10) The Complete SMP Picture — Summary

SMP in one formula (conceptual)
SMPt = λt

where λt solves:

min Σi [ Ci(pi,t) + SUi·ui,t + NLi·xi,t ]

s.t.  Σi pi,t = Dt                              → λt
      Σi ri,tk ≥ Rtk                          → μtk
      pi,min ≤ pi,t ≤ pi,max                       → γi,min, γi,max
      |pi,t − pi,t-1| ≤ RDi, RUi              → δi,t
      [unit commitment, min up/down, network limits]         → various μ
Unconstrained

SMP = C'marginal. Only the merit order matters. All marginal units have the same cost at the optimal dispatch.

Constrained (single Pricing Area)

SMP = λ*. The shadow price of the energy balance under all binding constraints. Congestion handled via uplift (constrained-on/off payments).

Co-optimised

SMP determined jointly with reserve shadow prices μk. The capacity allocation between energy and reserves affects SMP.

Locational (LMP, future phase)

SMPn = λ + Σ SFℓ,n·μℓ. Prices differ by location based on network congestion.

🔑 The two things to remember:

  1. SMP is always the shadow price of the energy balance. This is true whether the optimisation is unconstrained, constrained, co-optimised, or locational. Everything else (merit order, network constraints, reserve requirements) affects the SMP through their effect on the feasible region and thus on λ.
  2. Constraints add scarcity premiums. The gap between the SMP in an unconstrained world and the SMP in the real constrained world is the marginal cost of respecting the binding constraints. In a single-Pricing-Area market like SAWEM Phase 1, these premiums appear in the SMP itself, and the cost of congestion is recovered through uplift charges on all market participants.
