🛤️ Transnet Rail Infrastructure Manager (TRIM)

The New Rail Manager — Transnet's 7th Operating Division That Controls the Tracks, Sells Train Paths, and Makes Open Access a Reality

The Transnet Rail Infrastructure Manager — known as TRIM — is the single most important new institution in South Africa's rail reform. Created on 1 November 2023 as an interim entity, TRIM is the dedicated division that owns, manages, operates and maintains the national rail network infrastructure — the tracks, signalling, stations, and related assets — while remaining inside the Transnet group during the transition. Its creation is the physical embodiment of the government's plan to break Transnet's vertically-integrated monopoly: from now on, one entity (TRIM) controls the infrastructure, while Transnet Freight Rail Operating Co (TFROC) and any licensed private operators compete to run trains over it. If the National Rail Policy (2022) and the Economic Regulation of Transport Act (2024) are the legislation, TRIM is the machine that makes open access actually happen.

Part 1: Why TRIM Exists — The Problem It Was Built to Solve

For most of its history, Transnet operated as a single, vertically-integrated monopoly: it owned the tracks, ran the trains, operated the ports, and managed the pipelines — all under one roof. There was no separation between the infrastructure (tracks, signalling) and the services that ran over it. This created a fundamental structural problem:

  • No competition: A mining company wanting to move coal, iron ore or manganese had no alternative but to contract Transnet Freight Rail (TFR). There was no other train operator it could turn to, no competitor to apply pressure, and no consequence for poor service.
  • No transparency: Because TFR controlled both the network and its own trains, it could give itself preferential train paths, hide inefficiencies, and allocate capacity arbitrarily. There was no independent body to say "this operator should get this slot."
  • Declining performance: By the 2010s-2020s, the consequences of the closed monopoly became catastrophic: coal exports via Richards Bay collapsed from ~76 million tonnes to under 50 million tonnes per year; cable theft and vandalism crippled lines; maintenance was deferred for years; and billions of rand in mineral export revenue was stranded on the network.
  • State capture legacy: The Zondo Commission documented how the monopoly structure enabled the looting of Transnet — most infamously the ~R50+ billion locomotive procurement scandal (1,064 locomotives, ~R16 billion overpriced). A closed, unaccountable monopoly is also a corruption machine.
The core insight of the reform: You cannot fix a monopoly by asking the monopolist to behave better. You have to change the structure. The international evidence (the EU's railway packages, the UK's Network Rail model, Great Britain's open-access freight) showed that separating the infrastructure manager from the train operators — with a regulator setting the rules — creates the conditions for competition, efficiency and investment. TRIM is South Africa's version of that separation, adapted to a developing-country bulk-export context.

Part 2: What TRIM Actually Does — The Job Description

TRIM's mandate, as set out by Transnet and the National Rail Policy, is the management, operation and maintenance of the rail network infrastructure. Concretely, this means:

🚉 Infrastructure Custodianship

TRIM owns and maintains the physical network: ~20,000+ km of track, bridges, tunnels, signalling systems, level crossings, stations, and related land. Its job is to keep the network safe, available, and in good condition — the maintenance backlog accumulated over the past decade is one of its greatest challenges.

🗂️ Train Path Allocation

TRIM allocates train paths (slots on the network) to qualified operators — TFROC and any licensed third-party train operating companies (TOCs). Allocation must be non-discriminatory, based on published Network Statement rules. If a path is not being used by TFROC, it becomes available to other operators.

💵 Access Tariffs & Revenue

TRIM sets and collects access fees (tariffs) from operators for using the network. These tariffs fund TRIM's operating costs and, under the target model, contribute to infrastructure maintenance and expansion. Tariffs are published in the Network Statement and are subject to regulatory oversight.

📊 Maximising Network Utilisation

TRIM's explicit commercial objectives: maximise network utilisation, increase network density (more trains, heavier loads per kilometre), and drive a modal shift of freight from road back to rail. This is a national strategic objective — every tonne shifted from truck to train reduces road damage, congestion, and carbon emissions while improving export competitiveness.

What TRIM does NOT do: It does not operate trains. It does not own or operate terminals (that remains Transnet Port Terminals / TPT). It does not transport cargo itself. Its job is to be the neutral landlord of the network — the referee, not a player. In the interim period it sits inside Transnet, but the reform design is that the permanent Infrastructure Manager will operate with functional and regulatory independence.

Part 3: Who Oversees TRIM — The Regulatory Architecture

An infrastructure manager without an independent referee is just another monopoly. The reform therefore pairs TRIM with a parallel regulatory track:

IRERC — The Interim Regulator

The Interim Rail Economic Regulatory Capacity (IRERC) is the temporary market-sounding and regulatory body (established by the Department of Transport) that works with TRIM to develop the draft Network Statement, draft access agreements, and the tariff methodology. It provides regulatory oversight during the transition before the permanent regulator is legislated.

The Transport Economic Regulator (TER)

The Economic Regulation of Transport Act (2024) establishes a permanent Transport Economic Regulator that will ensure private operators get fair access, regulate pricing, monitor compliance and penalties, and resolve disputes between the infrastructure manager and operators. This is the independent expert body that removes tariff-setting and access disputes from political control.

Dept of Transport & Minister of Transport

The Minister of Transport is the political principal: he gazettes the final Network Statement and approves access tariffs, and the Department consults on the draft documents. The Minister's gazetting of the Final Network Statement in 2024, and again in July 2026, is the formal act that makes open access legally operative.

The three pillars working together:
  1. TRIM = the infrastructure manager (allocates paths, maintains network, sets access fees)
  2. TFROC + private TOCs = the train operators (compete for train paths and customers)
  3. IRERC → TER = the independent regulator (guarantees access, sets pricing rules, resolves disputes)

Part 4: The Network Statement — TRIM's Rulebook

The Network Statement is TRIM's foundational document — the rulebook that governs how access to the network works. Modelled on the European rail network statement regime, it sets out the rights and duties of the Infrastructure Manager and access seekers, and defines how Network Services are provided. Its publication timeline tells the story of the reform:

April 2024

The draft Network Statement, draft access agreement and proposed tariff methodology were published for consultation — with the formal opening of train-path applications for third-party operators.

December 2024

TRIM published the final version of the Network Statement and the approved rail access tariffs for the 2024/2025 financial year, gazetted via Government Notice 51819 in GG 2914. This was the moment open access legally became available in South Africa.

February 2025

TRIM and IRERC ran stakeholder engagement sessions on the significant changes between Network Statement Volumes 2 and 3, refining the access regime with input from the industry.

July 2026

The Minister of Transport gazetted the Final Network Statement and relevant annexures, officially making open access a reality in South Africa from the date of publication. TRIM continues to issue formal announcements on changes, updates and clarifications to the Network Statement and its annexures.

The political milestone: As President Ramaphosa put it in October 2023 before the finalisation: "We are working to reverse the legacy of the past era of corruption and mismanagement of our state-owned enterprises, which has left us with a persistent energy crisis and an inefficient ports and rail network." He confirmed that port, rail and electricity infrastructure would remain in public ownership, but that introducing competition in operations would "create greater efficiency and reduce prices in the long term" — the exact philosophy that created TRIM.

Part 5: TRIM and the Operators — Who Runs Trains Now?

With TRIM in place as the neutral network landlord, the train-operating side of the market is opening up. The structure is now:

Transnet Freight Rail Operating Co (TFROC)

TFROC is Transnet's train operating entity — the part of the old TFR that actually runs trains. Under the new model, TFROC is just one operator among many. It must apply to TRIM for train paths like everyone else, and cannot rely on privileged access to the network. Its performance will now be tested against private competitors.

Third-Party Train Operating Companies (TOCs)

Over 100 applications for train paths/access have been received since the process opened. The most advanced entrants include Traxtion (which has imported 40 locomotives and secured access for general freight on the Durban–Gauteng corridor, planning container trains between City Deep and Durban), Mkango Rail (manganese exports from the Northern Cape to Ngqura), and Barloworld/Value Logistics (shifting automotive and consumer goods off the N3). Mining majors including Anglo American, Kumba and South32 have signalled interest in running their own trains.

The key point: TRIM is the enabling institution for all of this. Without a neutral infrastructure manager, none of these operators could enter the market — because the incumbent would control both the track and the trains. TRIM's existence is what makes "open access" more than a slogan.

Part 6: The Challenges TRIM Faces

TRIM is the right idea, but it operates in extraordinarily difficult conditions. Its key challenges:

  • A severe maintenance backlog: Years of deferred maintenance mean large parts of the network are in poor condition. Private operators cannot run faster or heavier trains on decaying track. Catch-up maintenance is measured in tens of billions of rand, and a clear funding model (government investment grants + access fees) is still being settled.
  • Security — cable theft & vandalism: The physical security of the network is a precondition for private investment. Until TRIM (working with partners) can secure the network against the organised theft that crippled TFR, no operator — public or private — can run reliably.
  • Transitional ambiguity: TRIM is currently an "interim" entity inside Transnet, sharing the group banner. True independence (legal separation from the train-operating side) is the target, but it will take years to complete. Until then, there is a risk of soft capture — the infrastructure manager and the dominant operator are still siblings.
  • Resistance and labour: Internal Transnet constituencies and unions have strong incentives to slow-walk the separation. Private operators bring their own crews and potentially different labour models, raising fears of job losses and strike risk.
  • Funding model still evolving: The current design is that government funds infrastructure investment while access fees fund TRIM's operating costs. Getting the access-pricing methodology right — high enough to fund maintenance, low enough not to choke the modal shift back to rail — is a delicate balancing act.
  • Cream-skimming risk: Private operators will naturally target high-volume, high-margin corridors (Durban–Gauteng containers, manganese to Ngqura). The regulator must design pricing and obligations so that lower-density rural lines are still maintained or explicitly subsidised.
The honest picture: TRIM was stood up in less than a year, published the most important regulatory documents in the history of South African rail reform, and made open access legally real — all while the network around it was collapsing from theft, decay and underfunding. Its success will not be measured by documents published but by trains running on time, on track that is safe, at a price that pays for maintenance. That is a multi-year, multi-billion-rand project whose outcome is genuinely uncertain.

Part 7: Where This Goes — The Reform Trajectory

2023-2024

TRIM established; draft & final Network Statement; open access legally live (Dec 2024)

2025-2026

Third-party trains on Durban–Gauteng corridor; Network Statement volumes refined; TRIM announcements regime live

Late 2020s

Permanent Infrastructure Manager separation; Transport Economic Regulator fully operational; concessions for export corridors

2030s

Full open access across core network; rail modal share growing; network investment programme mature

Why it matters for South Africa: Transnet's rail collapse cost the economy hundreds of billions of rand in lost exports and imposed enormous external costs (trucks on roads, congestion, emissions). TRIM is the structural answer: a neutral manager of a national asset, opening it to whoever can run trains efficiently. If it works, South Africa reclaims its position as the African continent's freight logistics hub. If it does not — if TRIM remains a captive arm of Transnet, or the network keeps collapsing — the reform will be remembered as another promising paper exercise. The next five years will decide which story is told.
