LNG & Regasification

South Africa’s LNG subsector is the practical answer to the country’s current gas supply gap: it brings global gas into the market while the local resource base is developed.

What the LNG subsector covers

This sector includes LNG import terminals, floating storage regasification units (FSRUs), pipeline connections, and the downstream gas-to-power and industrial offtake structures that convert imported LNG into usable gas.

In South Africa, LNG is the main route for matching the country’s growing gas demand with the limited domestic supply available today.

Current projects and announcements
  • Saldanha Bay has been highlighted as a priority site for an LNG import terminal and associated gas hub. The 2024 Gas Master Plan and government announcements consistently name Saldanha as a near-term priority location.
  • Richards Bay and Durban / KwaZulu-Natal have also been proposed for additional LNG import or FSRU capacity to serve coastal industrial demand and power plants.
  • Fuel supply to gas-fired power remains a major driver: the government’s gas-to-power push is explicitly linked to the new regas terminals and the planned 2–3 GW of gas-fired generation that can provide stable capacity.
  • Emergency power and FSRUs were announced as part of power security measures, with companies such as Karpowership, Golar LNG, and global FSRU operators identified in media and project reports as likely partners for ships and floating terminals.
Who is building the LNG infrastructure?

The LNG subsector is a mix of public-sector planners, port authorities, private developers and international LNG specialists:

  • DMRE and the national government set the policy, approval and licensing framework.
  • Transnet National Ports Authority handles port site planning and logistics for coastal terminals.
  • PetroSA and state energy bodies are involved in planning strategic gas hubs and the upstream offtake design.
  • International FSRU and LNG specialists such as Karpowership, Golar LNG, Shell and TotalEnergies are the likely builders/operators of floating and import terminal assets.
  • Engineering, procurement and construction work is expected to involve firms with LNG and port experience: Worley, TechnipFMC, McDermott, Saipem, Fluor and others.
Timeline and status

The LNG sector is currently in the planning, permitting and early project stage, with the following broad timing expectations:

  • 2024–2026: government approvals, environmental studies and site selection for Saldanha, Richards Bay, Durban and other proposed terminals.
  • 2025–2027: contracting of FSRU ships, export terminal arrangements and initial pipeline link design.
  • 2026–2028: construction and commissioning windows for first LNG import capacity, with commercial operation expected once the associated gas-to-power plants and industrial customers are ready.

This timeline is subject to regulatory approval, financing, port readiness and the actual pace of gas-fired power plant development.

Why LNG matters for South Africa now

With domestic gas scarce, LNG is the fastest route to unlock meaningful volumes for power and heavy industry. It allows South Africa to:

  • firm variable renewables with gas-fired plants,
  • keep industrial feedstock supply competitive,
  • reduce the reliance on diesel and coal for flexibility, and
  • buy time while offshore exploration matures.
