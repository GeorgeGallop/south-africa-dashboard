AEMFC

African Exploration Mining and Finance Corporation — the state's mining company, a CEF subsidiary

AEMFC is how the government owns a coal mine. It is a wholly owned CEF subsidiary, created by ministerial directive in April 2007, with a mandate to acquire and hold energy-mineral rights on behalf of the state and to mine them. It is not part of the SANPC oil-company merger. Its customer is primarily Eskom.

Owner: CEF SOC Ltd (100% Republic of South Africa)
Created: Ministerial directive, 3 April 2007
Mandate: Hold exploration and mineral rights for energy-related (and other) minerals; mine them; support security of energy supply
Core operation: Vlakfontein coal mine, Mpumalanga — thermal coal to Eskom
Stated role: Feedstock security for Eskom power stations and, in the original directive, for indigenous fuels / CTL
SANPC: Outside the merger. Remains a CEF mining subsidiary.

AEMFC

Vlakfontein · Eskom coal · state miner

What it is

South Africa's coal industry is private (Exxaro, Seriti, Thungela, Glencore and others). AEMFC is the exception: a state-owned miner sitting inside the energy holding company rather than inside a mining SOE. The 2007 directive told it to safeguard energy supply by holding rights and mining them — a reaction to load shedding and to the fear that private coal contractors could leave Eskom short.

In the CEF group chart it sits next to PetroSA, not under it. Same shareholder (the Minister, via CEF), different commodity. Treasury's 2026 Vote 34 still lists AEMFC as a CEF subsidiary and flags "critical minerals exploration" as a forward workstream, so the mandate is no longer coal-only on paper even if coal is what it actually digs.

What it mines / who buys

  • Vlakfontein (Mpumalanga) — the producing asset. Thermal coal trucked or railed to Eskom stations. The West Block extension has been the recent project, with the usual Mpumalanga complications: blasting consents, wayleaves, community access.
  • Buyer: Eskom is the offtaker that matters. The original MOI language also mentioned feedstock for PetroSA-style indigenous fuels / CTL; that second customer has not materialised as a going concern.

There is no listed AEMFC share. Private juniors and majors compete with it for rights; they do not own it.

Why it sits under CEF

Because the Act that created CEF already authorised the state to "finance and promote the acquisition of coal" and to manufacture liquid fuel from coal. AEMFC is that clause given a company. It is an awkward fit in a group that is otherwise oil, gas and tanks — which is why it was left out of SANPC. A national petroleum company does not need a coal pit; a national energy holding company, under a 1977 statute written for coal-to-liquids South Africa, still does.

The future

AEMFC's fate is tied to Eskom's remaining coal fleet, not to Orange Basin wells. If stations close on the IRP schedule, the state's own mine shrinks with them. If Eskom keeps burning Mpumalanga coal into the 2040s, AEMFC is a small but politically useful supplier that can be directed in a shortage. The "critical minerals" line in the 2026 budget is the option to become something other than a one-mine coal company. Until a right is granted and a pit is opened, that is an aspiration, not an operation.
