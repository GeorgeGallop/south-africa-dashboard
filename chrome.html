History of South African Chrome

Discovery and Early Use (1797-1893)

Chromium was discovered as an element in 1797 by French chemist Louis Nicolas Vauquelin, who isolated it by heating crocoite with charcoal. Initially used in dyes and pigments, chromium's true importance emerged in 1893 when English metallurgist Harry Brearley invented stainless steel. Brearley found that adding ~12% chromium to steel created a corrosion-resistant alloy — a discovery that revolutionised modern industry. Chrome was no longer a curiosity; it was essential to everything from cutlery to chemical plants to architecture.

Bushveld Discovery (1900s-1940s)

Chromite deposits were identified in the Bushveld Igneous Complex early in the 20th century. The Bushveld's layered structure — formed 2 billion years ago — contained massive chromite seams, including the UG2 reef (which also contains PGMs). Commercial mining began in the 1920s on a small scale near Lydenburg and Steelpoort. World War II drove a step-change in demand, as chrome was a strategic military mineral for armoured steel production.

Ferrochrome and Export Growth (1950s-1990s)

Post-war, South Africa emerged as a major exporter of raw chromite. But the real value lay in ferrochrome — the processed alloy used to add chromium to molten steel. The 1960s and 1970s saw the construction of ferrochrome smelters in Mpumalanga (Witbank, Middelburg), leveraging South Africa's cheap coal-fired electricity. Samancor Chrome was formed through a series of mergers and became the world's largest ferrochrome producer. By 1975, South Africa supplied ~30% of world chrome; by the 2000s, that share exceeded 40%.

Global Dominance (2000-2020s)

Through the 2000s and 2010s, South Africa's chrome production grew from ~7.5 million tonnes to over 17 million tonnes annually. The country consistently produced 35-46% of the world's chrome, making it the clear #1 producer. The Bushveld Complex contains ~70% of global chrome reserves — an estimated 20 billion tonnes. China emerged as the primary market, importing the bulk of SA chrome for its massive stainless steel industry.

Energy Crisis Impact (2019-2024)

Ferrochrome smelting is extraordinarily energy-intensive — requiring ~3,500-4,000 kWh per tonne. Eskom's worsening load shedding hit the sector hard. Smelters faced the choice of curtailing production during peak blackout periods or investing in private power generation. By 2023, chrome smelters had invested in hundreds of megawatts of solar and wind capacity — a significant shift that insulated them from Eskom but raised capital costs. Transnet's rail failures also constrained chrome exports, though chrome's lower dependence on rail compared to coal and iron ore made it somewhat less vulnerable.

The Bushveld Chromite Reserves

The Bushveld Complex's chromite layers are extraordinary in scale. The LG6, MG1, and UG2 chromitite layers are among the most consistent and extensive mineral seams on Earth, extending over hundreds of kilometres. The UG2 is particularly significant as it also contains PGMs, meaning chrome and platinum can be co-mined — a unique economic advantage that makes Bushveld mines among the most cost-competitive in the world.

The Future: Stainless Steel and Green Infrastructure

Chrome demand is tied to stainless steel consumption, which in turn is driven by global infrastructure investment, construction, and industrial production. The shift toward green infrastructure — desalination plants, renewable energy facilities, electric vehicle charging networks — all require stainless steel. South Africa's dominance of chrome reserves ensures it will remain the world's critical supplier. The primary risks are not geological but operational: energy costs, rail capacity, and the health of the global stainless steel market.
