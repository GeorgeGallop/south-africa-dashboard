Sasol Ltd

Integrated Chemicals & Energy Company — Global Leader in Coal-to-Liquids (CTL) Technology

Sasol is South Africa's largest petrochemicals company and an iconic national industrial champion. It operates the world's biggest coal liquefaction plant at Secunda (Mpumalanga), a major chemicals facility at Sasolburg (Free State), and has a global presence across Africa, Europe, the Americas, Asia, and the Middle East. Sasol is also the largest single emitter of greenhouse gas in South Africa, making its decarbonisation pathway critical to the country's climate targets.

Sector: Petrochemicals, Liquid Fuels, Chemicals, Energy

History & Origins

Sasol was founded in 1950 as the South African Coal, Oil and Gas Corporation — a state-owned entity established to produce synthetic liquid fuels from coal, motivated by South Africa's political isolation and lack of domestic crude oil reserves. The company was built around Fischer-Tropsch coal liquefaction technology originally developed by German chemists and engineers in the early 20th century.

  • 1954: Sasolburg facility commissioned — the first commercial coal-to-liquids plant in South Africa, producing synthetic petrol, diesel, and chemicals.
  • 1970s–1980s: Following the oil crises and growing international sanctions against the apartheid government, Sasol built the much larger Secunda CTL complex (Secunda I & II, later Secunda III). Secunda became the largest coal liquefaction plant in the world.
  • 1979: Sasol listed on the JSE. The South African government began reducing its shareholding.
  • 1990s–2000s: Post-apartheid, Sasol diversified internationally — it acquired chemical assets in Europe, the US, and Asia, and developed gas-to-liquids (GTL) technology. The company established a major GTL plant in Qatar (Oryx GTL) and explored gas monetisation in Mozambique, Nigeria, and Canada.
  • 2010s: Sasol launched the massive Lake Charles Chemicals Project (LCCP) in Louisiana, USA — a $12+ billion ethane cracker and derivatives complex. This was the largest single investment in Sasol's history but suffered major cost overruns and delays, straining the company's balance sheet.
  • 2020s: Responding to financial pressure from LCCP debt, the global energy transition, and shareholder demands, Sasol announced a strategic pivot — selling international assets, refocusing on its South African core, and committing to significant decarbonisation targets.
Secunda & Sasolburg — The Industrial Core

These two facilities are the centrepiece of Sasol's South African operations and the focus of the country's petrochemicals transition. Together they account for approximately 11% of South Africa's total greenhouse gas emissions.

Secunda (Mpumalanga)

Built: 1980s

Primary role: Coal-to-liquids (CTL) — the world's largest. Produces synthetic liquid fuels (petrol, diesel, jet fuel) and supplies chemical feedstocks to Sasolburg and Natref.

Feedstock: ~35–40 million tonnes of coal per year from tied mines (Bosjesspruit, Twistdraai, Syferfontein, etc.)

Output: ~150,000 barrels per day of liquid fuels equivalent; significant volumes of chemicals (ethylene, propylene, ammonia, polymers, solvents).

Sasolburg (Free State)

Built: 1954

Primary role: Chemicals production — polymers, solvents, waxes, ammonia, explosives, and industrial chemicals. Also operates Natref, a conventional crude oil refinery (joint venture).

Feedstock: Receives chemical feedstocks (ethylene, propylene) and gas from Secunda via pipeline. Its own coal mines are now largely depleted.

Key products: Polyethylene, solvents, acrylic acid, butanol, waxes, mining explosives, phenol.

The two facilities are closely interlinked: Secunda supplies chemical feedstocks and gas to Sasolburg, and supplies liquid fuel components to Natref. They form an integrated value chain that cannot be analysed in isolation.

Value Chain — Upstream & Downstream

Sasol's value chain extends across multiple industries and sectors of the South African economy:

Upstream inputs:

  • Coal mines — provide the primary process feedstock for Secunda's CTL plant, as well as energy for both facilities. Sasol operates its own tied coal mines, giving it a secure but carbon-intensive supply.
  • Natural gas — from Mozambique's Pande and Temane gas fields via the Rompco pipeline, providing process and energy inputs to both facilities. Gas also supplies Sasol's gas-to-liquids (GTL) operations.
  • Electricity — drawn from Eskom's coal-heavy national grid, supplemented by Sasol's own cogeneration at Secunda.

Future upstream: In a decarbonised future, inputs are expected to shift to green hydrogen (electrolysis using renewable energy), sustainable carbon sources (biomass, captured CO₂), and renewable electricity procured through wheeling arrangements.

Downstream markets:

  • Liquid fuels — Sasol supplies a material proportion of South Africa's inland petrol, diesel, and jet fuel. The transport sector is the single largest market for Sasol's fuel products.
  • Chemicals — polymers, solvents, waxes, explosives, ammonia, phenol, and specialty chemicals sold to South African industry and exported globally.
  • Mining sector — Sasol is a major supplier of explosives (blasting agents) to South Africa's gold, platinum, and coal mines.
  • Agriculture — ammonia-based fertilisers and industrial chemicals for the agricultural value chain.
Why Sasol Matters for South Africa

Sasol is not just a company — it is a strategic national asset. The Meridian Economics Transition Petrochemicals project identified Secunda and Sasolburg as warranting focus based on six criteria, ranking "high" in all:

  • Centrality — Sasol's facilities sit at the core of South Africa's liquid fuels and chemicals value chains. Without them, large parts of the economy would face immediate supply disruption.
  • Interlinkages — Secunda and Sasolburg are connected by material and energy flows. Sasol also supplies feedstocks to downstream chemical manufacturers and fuel to the inland transport corridor.
  • GHG emissions — Secunda is the largest single emitter of greenhouse gas in the world (~11% of South Africa's total). What Sasol does about emissions determines whether South Africa meets its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) under the Paris Agreement.
  • Lifetime — the facilities have decades of remaining economic life. Premature closure would strand billions of rand of capital and destroy tens of thousands of jobs.
  • Governance — one central point of control (Sasol Ltd) makes coordinated decarbonisation planning feasible.
  • Fossil fuel dependence — Secunda is the world's most coal-dependent mega-facility. Its transformation is the petrochemicals sector's single biggest decarbonisation challenge.
Financial Crisis & Strategic Pivot

The Lake Charles Chemicals Project (LCCP) in Louisiana was intended to transform Sasol from a South African CTL company into a global petrochemicals powerhouse. Instead, it became a near-fatal financial event:

  • Budgeted at ~$8 billion, the project escalated to over $12 billion.
  • Start-up delays, technical problems, and lower-than-expected chemical margins compounded the cost overruns.
  • Net debt ballooned to over $8 billion (FY2020), triggering a debt crisis, asset sale programme, and share price collapse.
  • Sasol was forced to sell its US base chemicals business (for $1.8 billion), its stake in the Gemini HDPE joint venture, and its European wax business.

The pivot back to South Africa involves:

  • Divesting international assets to reduce debt.
  • Focusing capital on the South African core — Secunda, Sasolburg, and the Mozambique gas value chain.
  • A decarbonisation roadmap targeting 30% reduction in Scope 1 and 2 emissions by 2030, and net-zero by 2050. The roadmap includes replacing coal with gas at Secunda, adding renewable energy (~1.2 GW by 2030), deploying carbon capture and storage (CCS), and developing green hydrogen capacity.
  • Restructuring the group to separate South African operations from international holdings, improving capital allocation efficiency.
Decarbonisation Challenge & Pathways

Sasol's decarbonisation challenge is immense: as the company's own climate report states, Secunda's emissions are driven by the fundamental chemistry of coal liquefaction. Traditional efficiency measures can only achieve modest reductions. Transformational change is required:

  • Coal-to-gas fuel switching — replacing coal with natural gas at Secunda could reduce emissions by ~30–40%. This is the most immediate lever but requires secure gas supply (Mozambique expansion, LNG imports).
  • Renewable energy — Sasol has secured rights to develop ~1.2 GW of wind and solar, to be wheeled through the grid and used to displace coal-fired electricity purchases from Eskom.
  • Green hydrogen — Sasol has positioned itself as a potential green hydrogen champion in South Africa. The company's Fischer-Tropsch expertise is directly transferable to producing e-fuels and green chemicals from green hydrogen and sustainable carbon.
  • Carbon capture and storage (CCS) — potential storage in depleted offshore gas fields or South Africa's onshore geology. CCS is a high-cost option but may be needed for residual emissions.
  • Value chain transition — the downstream economy that depends on Sasol's products (transport fuels, plastics, fertilisers) will also need to decarbonise. This means demand-side shifts (electric vehicles, circular plastics, green fertilisers) interact with Sasol's supply-side choices.

The transition is not just Sasol's problem — it is a national industrial policy challenge. Premature closure of Secunda would create a catastrophic fuel and chemicals supply gap. The Meridian Economics study frames the question as: how does South Africa transition its petrochemicals value chain in a way that is economically sustainable, socially just, and aligned with climate targets?
