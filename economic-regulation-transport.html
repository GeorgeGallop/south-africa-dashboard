⚖️ Economic Regulation of Transport Act (2024)

Act No. 6 of 2024 — The Statutory Engine of Rail & Port Reform

The Economic Regulation of Transport Act is the legislative instrument that gives legal force to the reforms envisioned in the National Rail Policy (2022). Signed into law in 2024, it creates an independent Transport Economic Regulator with real powers — to set tariffs, enforce open access, investigate anti-competitive conduct, and impose remedies. Without this Act, the Rail Policy would remain a statement of intent. With it, the government has statutory authority to break open Transnet's monopoly and regulate the resulting competitive market.

Act No: 6 of 2024
Signed: 2024
Status: In Force
Policy Basis: National Rail Policy (2022)
Regulator: Transport Economic Regulator (new body)

Why an Act Was Needed

For decades, South Africa's rail and port sectors operated without independent economic regulation. Transnet set its own tariffs, controlled its own access, and was accountable to no external referee. The 1988 Transport Deregulation Act liberalised road freight but left rail untouched as a protected monopoly. The result was a system where the dominant player was also the rule-maker — a structural conflict of interest that produced predictable outcomes: high prices, poor service, no accountability.

The Economic Regulation of Transport Act solves this by creating an independent regulator with statutory powers — modelled on successful economic regulators in network industries worldwide (the UK's Office of Rail and Road, Australia's ACCC, and South Africa's own energy regulator NERSA for electricity). The key principle: the entity that owns the infrastructure cannot also set the rules of access to it.

What the Transport Economic Regulator Does

The Act gives the Regulator five core functions, each backed by enforceable legal powers:

1. Sets Access Tariffs

The Regulator determines the maximum prices that Transnet (or any infrastructure owner) can charge for third-party access to rail tracks, ports, and terminals. Tariffs must be transparent, non-discriminatory, and cost-reflective. This ends the practice of Transnet setting its own prices without external scrutiny.

Critical for private operators: they can build business cases knowing access charges are objectively set, not negotiated in a rigged game.

2. Enforces Open Access

The Regulator ensures that any licensed operator who applies for train paths or terminal slots receives fair treatment. If Transnet's Infrastructure Manager denies access, the operator can appeal to the Regulator. The Regulator can compel access if the denial is found to be anti-competitive or unjustified.

This is the sharp end of the reform — it means Transnet cannot quietly refuse slots to competitors and claim "operational constraints."

3. Investigates Anti-Competitive Conduct

The Regulator has investigatory powers comparable to the Competition Commission. It can probe complaints of discriminatory pricing, refusal to supply, margin squeeze, cross-subsidisation, and other abuses of dominant position by Transnet or any market participant.

Covers both the infrastructure owner (Transnet) and future dominant private operators — the framework is forward-looking, not just Transnet-specific.

4. Imposes Remedies

The Regulator can impose structural and behavioural remedies: fines, mandatory access orders, price caps, accounting separation requirements, and divestiture recommendations. In extreme cases of non-compliance, it can refer matters for criminal prosecution.

Without enforcement teeth, regulation is theater. This Act gives the Regulator a full set of remedies — from pricing intervention to structural separation orders.

5. Monitors & Reports on Market Performance

The Regulator publishes annual reports on the state of competition in rail and ports: market shares, investment levels, service quality metrics, tariff trends, and barriers to entry. This creates transparency and public accountability — and gives investors the data they need to assess market opportunities.

How the Act Delivers Vertical Separation

Vertical separation — splitting infrastructure ownership from train operations — is the defining structural reform of the National Rail Policy. The Economic Regulation of Transport Act is the legal mechanism that makes it enforceable. Here's the chain of logic:

1

Infrastructure Manager Created

The Act legally creates the Rail Infrastructure Manager as a separate entity, distinct from Transnet Freight Rail's train operations. Infrastructure assets are transferred to it.

2

Access Rules Codified

The Infrastructure Manager must publish a Network Statement with transparent access criteria. All operators — including TFR — apply on equal terms. No more inside track for the incumbent.

3

Regulator Enforces Fairness

If the Infrastructure Manager treats TFR preferentially — or denies access to a private operator — the Regulator can investigate, compel access, and impose penalties. The referee has a whistle and isn't afraid to use it.

Why this is different from what came before: In the old model, a mining company wanting to run its own trains on Transnet's tracks had to negotiate with Transnet — the very company it was competing against. Transnet had every incentive to delay, overprice, or outright refuse. Under the new model, the access decision is made by a structurally separate Infrastructure Manager, subject to appeal to an independent Regulator. The conflict of interest is broken by design, not by hoping Transnet management will be more cooperative.

Scope: What the Regulator Covers

✅ Rail Infrastructure

Tracks, signalling, stations, marshalling yards — all the fixed infrastructure on which trains run. Access to this is the primary regulatory target.

✅ Port Infrastructure & Services

Harbour channels, berths, cargo terminals, pilotage, towage, and stevedoring. The Regulator can set port tariffs and enforce non-discriminatory terminal access — critical for private terminal operators.

✅ Pipelines

Transnet's ~3,000 km petroleum and gas pipeline network falls under the Regulator's jurisdiction for access and tariff-setting, ensuring energy logistics are also opened to competition.

✅ Future Infrastructure

The Act is forward-looking. Any future privately built rail line, port terminal, or pipeline that constitutes "essential infrastructure" can be brought under the Regulator's jurisdiction to prevent new private monopolies from replacing the state one.

Institutional Design: Independence Matters

The Act places the Regulator outside the Department of Transport's direct control — it is an independent statutory body accountable to Parliament, not to the Minister. Its members are appointed through a transparent process with parliamentary oversight, have security of tenure, and can only be removed for cause. This independence is essential: a regulator that can be leaned on by politicians or the regulated entity cannot credibly enforce open access.

The funding model is also designed for independence: the Regulator is funded through a combination of parliamentary appropriation and industry levies, reducing dependence on any single funding source that could be used as leverage.

From Bill to Regulator: The Timeline

2022

National Rail Policy approved by Cabinet. Policy commitment to independent regulation made.

2023

Economic Regulation of Transport Bill tabled in Parliament. Public hearings and committee review.

2024

Act signed into law. Process begins for appointing the Regulator's board and building the institution.

Late 2025

Transport Economic Regulator fully operational. First access tariff determinations. First enforcement actions.

The bigger picture: why this Act is a turning point

South Africa has spent three decades trying to fix Transnet through management changes, turnaround plans, and financial bailouts — all within the same monopoly structure. None worked. The Economic Regulation of Transport Act represents a recognition that the structure was the problem, not the people. You cannot regulate a monopoly by asking it nicely to behave better. You have to break open the monopoly and create an independent referee with real power. This Act does both. It is, alongside the unbundling of Eskom, one of the two most important structural economic reforms in post-apartheid South Africa.
