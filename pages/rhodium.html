Rhodium (Rh)

South Africa mines more than 80% of the world's rhodium
Overview Platinum Palladium Rhodium Ruthenium Iridium Osmium
What is it?

A rare, hard, silvery-white PGM (atomic number 45). The outstanding industrial NOx-reduction catalyst, and historically the most price-volatile of the six metals.

Where is it found?

Almost entirely as a by-product of Bushveld PGM mining. The UG2 chromitite is particularly rhodium-rich relative to the Merensky Reef. Small quantities come from Russia, Zimbabwe and North America.

What is it used for?

Gasoline three-way catalytic converters (NOx reduction), chemical process catalysts, and glass manufacturing (high-temperature bushings and thermocouples, often alloyed with platinum).

Supply Chain

Recovered in the PGM refinery after platinum and palladium. Sold mainly as sponge to autocatalyst fabricators under tightly held industrial contracts. Very little investment or jewellery demand.

What rhodium is

Rhodium is the third "autocatalyst PGM" after platinum and palladium, but it is far rarer. Primary mine supply is only about 700,000 ounces a year — roughly an eighth of platinum and a tenth of palladium. It is corrosion-proof, reflects well, and catalyses the reduction of nitrogen oxides more effectively than any other practical metal. That last property is why modern gasoline cars, facing ever-tighter NOx limits, cannot easily design rhodium out of the three-way catalyst.

In the Bushveld, rhodium grades are higher in UG2 than in Merensky. When South African producers shift mill feed toward UG2 (often because Merensky shafts are ageing or more expensive), the rhodium yield in the basket rises. There is no rhodium mine; output is a function of how much PGM ore is mined and which reef it comes from. The International Platinum Group Metals Association notes that platinum and palladium are 86% of the mined PGM basket by mass and rhodium only about 5%.

Price history is extreme. Rhodium ran to around $30,000/oz in 2021, averaged about $15,500 in 2022, then collapsed toward $4,000–6,000 as auto production normalised and consumers destocked. In 2025 it recovered: the Minerals Council average was US$6,269/oz, and Johnson Matthey records spikes above $9,000 in late December. USGS ranked rhodium the second-most-critical mineral of 60 evaluated in its 2025 assessment, largely because of this supply concentration.

What it is used for

Johnson Matthey estimates 2025 demand at 1.06 million ounces:

  • Automotive (~899 koz, ~85%): The NOx-reduction component of gasoline three-way catalysts, and some diesel applications. Loadings have been "thrifted" (reduced per vehicle) after the 2020–21 price spike, but chemistry still requires rhodium. Hybrids still use it; battery-electric vehicles do not.
  • Chemical (~91 koz): Hydroformylation (oxo-alcohols), acetic acid related chemistry, and other process catalysts. A smaller but high-value industrial book.
  • Glass (~38 koz): Platinum-rhodium alloys in bushings that extrude fibreglass and in equipment for display glass. Metal in this use is largely closed-loop — plants hold large inventories that are periodically recycled.
  • Electrical and other (~35 koz): Contacts, thermocouples, and minor industrial uses.

There is almost no jewellery market and, until recently, almost no investment market. Rhodium ETFs saw active buying in 2025 for the first time in nearly a decade, but this remains tiny next to auto demand.

Who buys it

  • The same four catalyst majors — BASF, Johnson Matthey, Umicore, Heraeus — dominate physical offtake. They buy sponge from South African refiners and coat converters for global OEMs.
  • The United States is the world's largest rhodium importer. USGS reports South Africa as the leading supplier of US rhodium imports (~34% in 2024, about $643 million), with the rest moving through European refining and trading hubs.
  • Glass manufacturers in China, Europe and the US (fibreglass and LCD/display glass) are the main non-auto industrial buyers, often via fabricators who also handle platinum.
  • Chemical companies in Europe, China and the US take the oxo-alcohol and related catalyst demand.

Because the market is small and opaque, a handful of industrial consumers and a few trading houses set the price. Producer stock sales from South Africa can move the market for months. That is why first-quarter South African maintenance and stocktakes are watched so closely: a few weeks of weak shipments can reprice the metal.

Production and consumption

South Africa supplied 566,000 oz in 2025 (~17.6 tonnes), more than 80% of primary mine output. Russia (68 koz) and Zimbabwe (45 koz) are the only other meaningful sources. Recycling from autocatalysts added 312 koz. Demand of 1.06 Moz left a modest 50,000 oz deficit — small in ounces, large relative to a 700 koz mine market.

IPA figures for 2025 put global primary rhodium around 22 tonnes, with South Africa, Russia, Zimbabwe, Canada and the United States accounting for essentially all of it. CSIS cites South Africa at 81% of world production. Mine supply cannot be raised independently of platinum: if Bushveld PGM output keeps sliding, rhodium slides with it, unless the mix shifts further into UG2.

The future

Rhodium's fate is tied more tightly to the gasoline car than any other PGM. Hydrogen technologies do not need it. Jewellery will not save it. The bear case is straightforward: BEVs take share, loadings are thrifted further, and a 700 koz market becomes a 400 koz market supplied increasingly from scrap.

The bull case is that NOx chemistry still requires rhodium, Euro 7 / equivalent standards do not relax, hybrids prolong catalyst demand, and South African mine supply keeps shrinking. In a market this small, a 10% drop in Bushveld output is a crisis. Glass and chemical uses provide a high-value floor, and above-ground stocks that cushioned the post-2021 crash are no longer as ample.

For South Africa, rhodium is often the swing metal in the PGM basket price. A UG2-heavy producer (Northam, parts of Implats and Sibanye) is structurally long rhodium. That is a strategic advantage as long as gasoline engines are built, and a concentrated political-economy risk: the world cannot replace South African rhodium at any meaningful scale this decade.
