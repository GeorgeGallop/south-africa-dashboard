Osmium (Os)

The rarest PGM in use — a few hundred kilograms a year, almost all from South African refining
Overview Platinum Palladium Rhodium Ruthenium Iridium Osmium
What is it?

The densest naturally occurring element (22.59 g/cm³), atomic number 76. A hard, brittle, bluish-white metal. Its tetroxide is useful in chemistry and microscopy — and highly toxic — which is why osmium never became a bulk industrial PGM.

Where is it found?

Only as a trace by-product of PGM refining. South Africa is the dominant source of osmium-bearing refinery streams; Russia (Norilsk) is the other. There is no osmium mine.

What is it used for?

Osmium tetroxide for biological staining and organic synthesis, specialty alloys (fountain-pen nibs, electrical contacts), a few medical-implant alloys, and a very small jewellery niche.

Supply Chain

Separated in tiny batches at PGM refiners (Implats Springs, Valterra's Precious Metals Refinery, Heraeus in South Africa/Germany). Sold as metal, powder or OsO4 compounds to laboratories and specialty-chemical houses. Most years the entire world market is hundreds of kilograms, not tonnes.

What osmium is

Osmium is the sixth PGM and the odd one out. It is denser than iridium, oxidises slowly in air to osmium tetroxide (OsO4) — a volatile, pungent, extremely toxic compound that can damage eyes and lungs at low concentrations. That chemistry is both the reason osmium is useful (OsO4 is a powerful oxidant and a superb lipid stain for electron microscopy) and the reason it was never fitted into catalytic converters, jewellery at scale, or hydrogen hardware.

Johnson Matthey's annual PGM market report covers five metals and does not publish osmium supply-demand tables. The African Development Bank notes that osmium is omitted from most PGE statistics because use is niche. The International Platinum Group Metals Association's 2026 osmium factsheet is blunt: reliable quantitative information is extremely scarce; companies do not report production and sales; global volumes are expressed in kilograms per year.

IPA estimates annual global production around 500–550 kg in 2024 and 2025. USGS US imports illustrate the scale: 1 kg in 2021, 1 kg in 2022, none in 2023, 1 kg in 2024, and an estimated 57 kg in 2025. A single industrial order can move the entire US import line.

What it is used for

End-use shares are approximate — there is no Johnson Matthey-style census — but the industrial picture is consistent across IPA, USGS and trade descriptions:

  • Chemical and pharmaceutical (~largest industrial use): OsO4 and osmate salts as oxidants in organic synthesis, including syn-dihydroxylation (Sharpless-type chemistry) for fine chemicals and some pharmaceutical intermediates. Demand is laboratory- and batch-scale, not bulk.
  • Microscopy and biology: OsO4 stains lipids and adds electron density for transmission electron microscopy. Hospitals, universities and pharma labs are the buyers. Volumes are grams to kilograms.
  • Specialty alloys: Osmium-iridium and platinum-osmium alloys were historically used in fountain-pen nibs, compass bearings and electrical contacts because they are extremely hard and wear-resistant. Medical implants (some heart-valve and pacemaker alloys) have used platinum-osmium in small quantities. These uses have shrunk as ceramics, other PGMs and newer alloys took share.
  • Jewellery: A marketing niche exists for crystalline osmium, but IPA describes jewellery demand as very limited. It is not a meaningful offtake compared with platinum jewellery.

Osmium is not used in autocatalysts, PEM electrolysers, hard disks or glass bushings. It does not ride the auto or hydrogen cycles that move the other five metals.

Who buys it

  • Precious-metals houses — notably Heraeus (Hanau, with a South African refinery) and Johnson Matthey — are the practical vendors of osmium metal and OsO4. End users rarely buy from a mine; they buy a reagent or a mill product from a refiner.
  • Laboratory and life-science suppliers (electron-microscopy houses, chemical catalogue companies) distribute OsO4 in sealed ampoules to universities, hospitals and pharma labs in the US, Europe, Japan and China.
  • Specialty alloy and medical-device firms take small lots of metal for contacts and implants.
  • Trade: Osmium is usually bundled in customs data with iridium and ruthenium (HS 711041). South Africa's combined exports of that group run to the order of a billion dollars, but almost all of that value is iridium and ruthenium. Japan, Hong Kong, the United States, China and the UK are the listed destinations. Osmium's share of that flow is a rounding error in rand terms and a majority of world osmium in kilogram terms.

Producers are the same Bushveld refiners as for the other PGMs: Valterra (Rustenburg Precious Metals Refinery), Implats (Springs) and Sibanye-Stillwater, plus Norilsk in Russia. They recover osmium because it is in the ore, not because a customer queued up for 500 kilograms.

Production and consumption

Treat every osmium number as an order-of-magnitude estimate. IPA's 500–550 kg/year for 2024–25 is the best current public range. Sales in any one year can diverge from production because refiners stockpile through periods of weak demand.

By comparison, South Africa's 2025 platinum supply was about 123 tonnes — more than two hundred times the entire world osmium market. Osmium does not move the Bushveld's economics. It is recovered because leaving it in the residue would waste a saleable (if tiny) product and because the refinery already has to handle it as a toxic process stream.

The future

Osmium will remain a niche by-product. There is no hydrogen, auto or AI demand story on the scale of the other PGMs. Microscopy and fine-chemical oxidation will continue at laboratory scale. Alloy uses are more likely to shrink than grow. Crystalline-osmium jewellery is a marketing experiment, not an industrial offtake.

Supply will track South African PGM mining and refining. If Bushveld output falls, osmium falls with it — but the market is so small that a 20% drop is tens of kilograms, easily buffered by refinery stocks. The binding issues are handling and regulation (OsO4 toxicity), not scarcity in the platinum-or-rhodium sense.

The honest conclusion for this dashboard: osmium completes the set of six, it is geologically South African, and it does not belong in the same strategic conversation as platinum, palladium, rhodium, ruthenium or iridium. Those five metals have buyers, deficits and industrial futures. Osmium has a safety data sheet and a few hundred kilograms a year.
