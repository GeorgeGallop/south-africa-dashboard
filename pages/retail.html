🛒 Retail

How South Africans actually buy — listed supermarket groups at one end, spazas, hawkers and township wholesale at the other, with a large grey zone in between.

Overview

Retail is one of the few South African sectors that still generates large-scale employment and is visible in every settlement in the country. Stats SA’s Quarterly Labour Force Survey puts wholesale and retail trade among the biggest employers in the economy — on the order of 3 million people when you count both formal stores and the informal traders who sit outside them. Household final consumption is the backbone of GDP; food, clothing and household goods are the first place a wage, a grant or a stokvel payout is spent.

The formal end is concentrated and listed. Shoprite (Checkers, Usave, Shoprite, LiquorShop) is Africa’s largest retailer by store count and turnover. Pick n Pay and SPAR (a voluntary trading group of independent owners) compete in groceries. Woolworths holds the premium food and clothing slot. Massmart (Makro, Game, Builders) is the warehouse and general-merchandise play. Clothing and general merchandise sit with Pepkor (PEP, Ackermans, Shoe City), Mr Price, The Foschini Group and Truworths. These groups run the malls, the distribution centres, the private-label brands and the JSE retail index.

That is not how most of the country shops every day. Township and rural retail still runs through spaza shops, street hawkers, taxi-rank stalls, shebeens, China malls and cash-and-carries. Formal chains have spent two decades trying to take that trade — Boxer, Usave, township malls (Maponya, Jabulani, Mall of Thembisa, Soweto’s older centres), Flash and Kazang till points, PayShap QR codes. They have taken share. They have not replaced the informal layer. For the size of that layer, who is in it, and who is trying to get in, see Informal Market.

Three structural facts sit under both sides of the sector. First, households are stretched: high unemployment, grant dependence and food inflation mean volume lives at the value end (Usave, Boxer, PEP, spazas), not at Woolworths. Second, wholesale is the choke point — Jumbo, Trade Centre, Makro, Fordsburg and the China malls feed thousands of micro-retailers who never see a listed chain’s DC. Third, crime and municipal by-laws (trading permits, pavement clearances, liquor, health certificates) decide who can operate on a given street more than any national industrial policy does.
