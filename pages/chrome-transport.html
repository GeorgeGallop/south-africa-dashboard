Chrome Transport

Where the lines are — and why so much chrome now moves by road
Overview Mining Transport Smelting Ferrochrome Industry End Markets

Chrome does not have a dedicated heavy-haul line the way iron ore has Sishen–Saldanha or coal has Ermelo–Richards Bay. It shares Transnet's North and Northeast corridors with coal and magnetite, leaks across the border to Maputo, and — more than any other bulk mineral except maybe manganese in a bad year — goes to port on trucks.

Where the lines are

Bushveld chrome sits in two clusters. Each has a different way out.

Cluster Rail geography Ports
Eastern limb — Steelpoort, Burgersfort, Lydenburg, Dwarsrivier Steelpoort branch into the Northeast Corridor (Mpumalanga / Limpopo general freight). Connects toward Komatipoort (Maputo line) and south toward Ermelo / Richards Bay. Maputo (closest, often preferred); Richards Bay bulk terminal; some Durban
Western limb — Rustenburg, Brits, Marikana, Boshoek North Corridor and the Rustenburg–Gauteng web. Chrome trains here are typically 75- or 150-wagon consists, not the 200-wagon coal jumbos. TFR's North Corridor is ~40% of Transnet Freight Rail volume and rails export coal, Eskom coal, chrome and chemicals. Durban (road-heavy); Richards Bay if it can get a slot; Maputo via N4 / Lebombo

Transnet's own annual report is blunt: chrome mining is concentrated in North West and Limpopo, and exports are channelled through Richards Bay and Maputo. The 2025 rail-open-access round allocated North Corridor routes for coal and chrome (six new entrants, 15 routes) and Northeast Corridor routes for coal, chrome, magnetite, fuel and containers (six entrants, 16 routes). Private train operating companies are supposed to put extra chrome tonnes on those paths from 2026/27.

The two ports that actually matter

  • Richards Bay — Transnet wants a chrome/magnetite mega-terminal and a private partner (up to 49%) on the bulk terminal, explicitly to claw back 4–6 million tonnes of chrome a year that left for Mozambique. Corridor rehabilitation and PSP studies are 2025/26 priorities.
  • Maputo / Matola — the escape valve. When TFR and Richards Bay seize up, eastern-limb ore goes down the N4 and the Ressano Garcia line through Lebombo. Maputo handled a record ~32 million tonnes of all cargo in 2025; Matola bulk hit 9.9 million tonnes. CFM is upgrading the line toward ~19 mtpa. Grindrod is the commercial operator most associated with this chrome leak.

Durban still takes bagged and containerised chrome and ferrochrome, but it is a congested general port, not a purpose-built ore machine. Inland corridor and border delays (Lebombo queue times) often wipe out any berth-side improvement.

Train vs road

Coal and iron ore are rail commodities. Chrome has become a road commodity that sometimes gets a train.

  • Why rail should win: a tonne of chromite is low-value bulk. Rail is cheaper per kilometre if the wagons, locomotives and slots exist. TFR still runs dedicated chrome trains on the North Corridor.
  • Why road won: locomotive shortages, cable theft, branch-line decay on the Steelpoort system, and port congestion at Richards Bay. Exporters bought truck fleets rather than wait. At Richards Bay, dry-bulk modal split has been quoted around 70% truck, 25% rail, 5% conveyor — the opposite of a heavy-haul port. Hundreds of thousands of trucks a year now enter Richards Bay as minerals leave the coal line.
  • Mine-to-smelter: even before export, a lot of ore never sees a main line. Western-limb chrome to Wonderkop/Boshoek and eastern-limb ore to Lion or Tubatse often moves a short distance by road or private siding. When the furnaces are off, that ore is re-routed to port instead of to the plant next door.

The Eswatini Rail Link (planned through 2030) is Transnet's attempt to peel ~12 mtpa of general freight — including chrome and ferrochrome — off the south-of-Ermelo coal line so export coal and chrome stop fighting for the same path to Richards Bay.

What this does to the industry

Road chrome is how South Africa kept exporting 20-plus million tonnes of ore while the railways were broken. It is also how illegal ore disappears into the same queues. It wrecks the N4 and the roads into Richards Bay, and it is more expensive than a working railway — which is one reason non-integrated miners still ship ore rather than wait for a furnace: the truck to Maputo gets paid now.

Private TOCs on the North and Northeast corridors are the first serious attempt to put chrome back on rail without waiting for a full Transnet turnaround. Until those trains actually run, assume: eastern ore → Maputo by road/rail mix; western ore → Durban/Richards Bay mostly by road; ferrochrome, when any is made, follows the same ports in containers or bulk bags.
