✈️ Aeroderivative Gas Turbines

Gas turbines born from aircraft jet engines: fast-starting, lightweight, modular and flexible. Specs, advantages, costs, and why they are the perfect match for what South Africa's grid is looking for.

🧭 What Is an Aeroderivative Gas Turbine?

An aeroderivative gas turbine is a power-generation turbine adapted from an aircraft jet engine. The core — the gas generator — is essentially a jet engine mounted on its side and coupled to a power turbine that drives a generator. Because the design inherits decades of aviation engineering, it is lighter, faster-starting, faster-ramping and far more portable than a heavy industrial frame machine.

Aeroderivatives typically sit in the ~20–120 MW class, between small industrial turbines and the huge H-class frames (300–800 MW). They shine at the fast-response, mid-merit and peaking duties that a modern grid with high shares of wind and solar desperately needs.

💡 Aero vs Frame in one line: Frame turbines (GE 9F, Siemens SGT5-8000H) are big, heavy and most efficient at steady baseload; aeroderivatives (GE LM series, Rolls-Royce Trent) are smaller, quicker and built to cycle hard every day.

✅ The Advantages

⚡ Fast start & extreme ramp rates

Full load in 5–10 minutes and ramp rates of tens of MW per minute. When the sun sets or the wind drops, aeroderivatives catch the load in minutes — something a large frame turbine, let alone coal or nuclear, cannot do.

🔁 Built to cycle daily without penalty

Aeroderivative engines are descended from aircraft engines that start and stop, throttle and re-throttle on every flight. They handle thousands of starts per year with minimal maintenance impact — ideal for a grid that needs dispatchable back-up behind renewables, not a baseload machine.

📦 Lightweight, packaged & quick to install

Aero engines are compact and mostly factory-assembled on skids. A complete simple-cycle plant can ship as modules, need far less civil works, and reach commercial operation in 6–12 months from order — versus 3–5 years for a large frame or CCGT project. They can even be containerised and relocated as demand shifts.

🛠️ Exchangeable cores — shorter outages

Because the gas generator is a self-contained jet-engine core, maintenance can mean swapping the entire core in days rather than a lengthy in-situ overhaul. A spare core restores the plant to service almost immediately — a major availability advantage over heavy frames.

💧 Minimal or zero water use

Simple-cycle aeroderivatives need no cooling water — no steam cycle, no cooling towers. In a water-stressed country like South Africa, this removes a whole class of siting constraints and environmental permissions.

⛽ Fuel & hydrogen flexibility

Multi-fuel capable (natural gas, diesel, kerosene) with dry low-emissions (DLE) combustors on modern models, and growing hydrogen-blend readiness — aligning with South Africa's Green Hydrogen Programme and the future conversion of new gas plants.

🌡️ Part-load performance that holds up

Unlike large frames whose efficiency collapses at part load, aeroderivatives stay comparatively efficient when running at 30–70% load — exactly where a mid-merit plant behind renewables spends most of its life.

💰 The Costs

Aeroderivatives are cheaper to buy per plant, faster to build, but more expensive per MW of capacity and per MWh of energy than the largest frame machines. That trade is exactly right for peaking and mid-merit duty — and the numbers below are typical ranges:

🟢 Capital cost (EPC, simple cycle)

Roughly $700–$1,000/kW installed (including balance of plant) for a skid-mounted aero plant. That is higher per kW than a giant frame simple cycle (~$400–$600/kW) — but the total project cost is far lower because the plant is small, modular and quick, and you only pay for the capacity you need now.

🟢 Example: an LM6000 plant

A 4 × 50 MW LM6000 simple-cycle plant (~200 MW) is typically a $150–$200 million project and can be up and running in under a year — a fraction of the capital and timeline of a 800 MW H-class CCGT (~$600–$900 million, 4+ years).

🟠 Operating cost

Non-fuel O&M for aeroderivatives is typically $5–$10/MWh — higher than a large frame baseload machine (~$3–$5/MWh) because aero engines run hot and need more frequent hot-gas-path inspections. The offset is fuel: at the low load factors (5–30%) where SA will run peakers, total annual O&M spend stays modest.

🟠 Fuel economics & heat rate

Simple-cycle heat rates of ~6,500 kcal/kWh (LM6000 class) mean aero turbines burn 15–25% more gas per MWh than an H-class CCGT. But they only run when the grid needs them. For peaking on imported LNG, the relevant figure is the cost of the marginal MWh at the exact hour of scarcity — where starting in minutes, not hours, is what captures value.

💡 The bottom line on cost: Aeroderivatives are not the cheapest electricity per MWh — they are the cheapest capacity to deliver fast, the cheapest way to firm up renewables, and the cheapest insurance against load shedding when every hour of unserved energy costs the economy billions.

🇿🇦 Why They Are Perfect for What South Africa Is Looking For

⚡ The grid crisis demands speed — and aero is the fastest path

South Africa's electricity emergency is now. IRP2025 and the 2025–2026 gas-to-power procurement aim to add dispatchable gas capacity on a tight schedule. An aeroderivative simple-cycle plant can deliver new megawatts in 6–12 months, modular in 50 MW blocks, while frame CCGT projects take 4–6 years. For the immediate capacity gap, nothing matches aeroderivatives for speed.

🌬️ They are the perfect partner for wind and solar

South Africa is adding gigawatts of wind and solar, which are intermittent. The grid needs fast-starting, fast-ramping, daily-cycling capacity to fill the gaps at dawn, dusk and on windless weeks — precisely the duty cycle aeroderivatives were built for. Their 5–10 minute starts and high part-load efficiency make them the natural complement to the renewable build-out.

🏭 South Africa already runs this technology

Eskom's Ankerlig power station (Western Cape, ~1,338 MW) operates a fleet of GE LM6000 aeroderivative turbines, making SA one of the most experienced operators of the platform in Africa. Local crews, local maintenance organisations (including Ansaldo's Atlas Facilities MRO in Cape Town) and an existing spares ecosystem mean new aero plants plug into established local capability rather than importing a brand-new skillset.

💧 No water, no problem

Simple-cycle aeroderivatives need no cooling water. In a country where water constraints already throttle coal and will shape new CCGT siting, aero plants can be placed near demand centres and gas supply without water availability becoming a dealbreaker — and they can be dispersed across the grid to relieve transmission bottlenecks.

⛽ Matched to LNG import timelines

LNG imports are set to arrive at Richards Bay and Coega within the next few years. Aero turbines can run on natural gas, diesel or both — meaning plants ordered today can burn diesel for emergency peaking immediately, then switch to cheaper pipeline/LNG gas as supply comes online. That sequencing flexibility is a perfect fit for a transition with uncertain timing.

🌱 Hydrogen-ready for the long game

The OEMs offer aeroderivatives capable of burning blends of hydrogen with natural gas, and the class is on a clear roadmap to higher H₂ percentages. South Africa's Green Hydrogen ambition means turbines purchased now are not stranded assets — they can progressively decarbonise as domestic green hydrogen scales up.

🟩 The right mix, not one technology: Aeroderivatives are the right tool for fast-reacting peaking and mid-merit duty. For genuine baseload on cheap gas, a high-efficiency H-class CCGT remains the right tool. An optimal South African gas fleet — exactly what the gas-to-power programme is now procuring — will likely combine aeroderivative peakers for flexibility with a smaller number of H-class frames for baseload. The aeroderivative is the part of that mix that answers the urgent question: "how do we close today's capacity gap fastest, without locking in tomorrow's problem?"
