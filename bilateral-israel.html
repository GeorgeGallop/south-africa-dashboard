🇮🇱 Israel

South Africa–Israel Bilateral Trade Relationship — History, Present, and Key Institutions

South Africa and Israel have one of the most complex and politically charged bilateral relationships on the continent. The two countries share deep historical ties — Israel was one of the few nations to maintain trade and military cooperation with apartheid-era South Africa when much of the world had imposed sanctions. Post-1994, the ANC government, with its longstanding alliance with the Palestinian liberation movement, shifted South Africa's stance dramatically. Today, the relationship is defined by tension between economic cooperation in technology, agriculture, and security — and growing political hostility driven by South Africa's pro-Palestinian foreign policy, most visibly through the ICJ genocide case against Israel in 2024. This page surveys the history, the trade flows, the key institutions (including the Israel Trade Administration's South Africa desk), and the political dynamics that make this one of the most volatile bilateral relationships in South Africa's portfolio.

Part 1: The Historical Relationship — From Apartheid Ally to Adversary

The South Africa–Israel relationship has passed through three distinct eras, each defined by the political context of the time:

Era 1: The Sanctions Partnership (1960s–1993)

During the apartheid era, as international sanctions tightened against South Africa, Israel was one of the few Western-aligned states that maintained and deepened trade and military ties. The relationship was pragmatic on both sides: Israel, itself diplomatically isolated in much of the developing world, sought reliable partners for trade and technology cooperation; South Africa, cut off from Western arms suppliers, sought Israeli military technology, electronics, and agricultural expertise. The partnership extended to joint military development (including reported cooperation on nuclear technology), intelligence sharing, and substantial trade in diamonds, minerals, and agricultural products. At its peak in the 1980s, bilateral trade was estimated at $500–800 million annually — significant for both countries given the sanctions context. This relationship made Israel deeply controversial within the anti-apartheid movement, where the ANC — which had strong ties to the PLO — viewed Israel as a complicit partner of the apartheid regime.

Era 2: Post-Apartheid Rebalancing (1994–2015)

After 1994, the ANC government inherited a relationship it was ideologically hostile to but pragmatically maintained. President Mandela established full diplomatic relations with both Israel and the Palestinian Authority, maintaining a "two-state solution" position. Trade continued and grew — Israeli technology companies invested in South Africa's telecoms, agritech, and security sectors. South Africa exported coal, minerals, fruit, and chemicals to Israel. Bilateral trade grew to approximately $800 million–$1 billion by the early 2010s. The relationship functioned at the level of economic pragmatism: South Africa did not agree with Israeli policy toward Palestinians, but did not allow that disagreement to fully disrupt trade. The ANC's pro-Palestinian rhetoric was consistent but did not translate into economic sanctions or trade disruption during this period.

Era 3: Confrontation (2018–Present)

The relationship began deteriorating sharply around 2018 when South Africa downgraded its embassy in Tel Aviv to a liaison office, recalled its ambassador, and escalated rhetorical condemnation of Israeli policy. The Gaza war of 2023–2024 triggered a complete break from pragmatic engagement: South Africa brought a case at the International Court of Justice (ICJ) in December 2023 accusing Israel of genocide under the Genocide Convention. The ICJ issued provisional measures in January 2024, finding it "plausible" that Israel's actions could constitute genocide. South Africa's government has since called for boycotts of Israeli goods, supported BDS (Boycott, Divestment, Sanctions) movement activities, and signalled that trade with Israel is inconsistent with South Africa's foreign policy principles. Trade has declined — estimates put the 2024 figure significantly below the 2022 peak. The future trajectory depends on the Gaza conflict's resolution and whether South Africa's political leadership maintains or escalates its confrontational stance.

Part 2: What South Africa and Israel Trade

Despite the political hostility, the economic complementarity between the two economies is real — and has been the force sustaining trade through multiple political crises:

South African Exports to Israel

Coal: South Africa is a significant coal exporter, and Israel imports thermal coal for power generation. This has been the largest single export category by value.

Minerals & Metals: PGMs (platinum, palladium), manganese, iron ore, and ferroalloys — Israel is a smaller but consistent buyer of South African mineral exports, largely for industrial processing and re-export.

Agricultural Products: Citrus, deciduous fruit (apples, pears, grapes), wine, and nuts. South African fruit competes with Israeli domestic production but serves the Israeli market during counter-seasonal periods (Southern Hemisphere summer = Northern Hemisphere winter).

Chemicals: Industrial chemicals, fertilisers, and pharmaceutical intermediates.

South African Imports from Israel

Technology & Electronics: Israeli technology exports to South Africa include telecommunications equipment, cybersecurity software, medical devices, and electronics components. Israel's tech sector — one of the world's most advanced per capita — has a natural export market in South Africa's financial services, mining technology, and telecom sectors.

Agricultural Technology: Drip irrigation systems (Israel invented modern drip irrigation), greenhouse technology, water management systems, and precision agriculture equipment. Given South Africa's water scarcity, Israeli agritech has been a consistent import category, particularly for the Western Cape's fruit and wine industries.

Machinery & Industrial Equipment: Manufacturing machinery, mining equipment components, and specialised industrial tools.

Pharmaceuticals: Generic medicines and pharmaceutical ingredients from Israel's large generic drug manufacturing sector (Teva, the world's largest generic drug manufacturer, is Israeli).

The complementarity paradox: The Israeli and South African economies are unusually complementary. Israel is a technology-intensive, water-scarce, resource-poor economy that exports high-value manufactured goods and imports raw materials and agricultural products. South Africa is a resource-rich, water-scarce, technology-importing economy that exports raw materials and agricultural products and needs exactly the kind of water technology and industrial machinery Israel produces. In a purely economic world, bilateral trade between the two countries would be far larger than ~$1 billion. The fact that it is not reflects the political constraints that override economic logic.

Part 3: Key Institution — The Israel Trade Administration (South Africa Desk)

The Israel Trade Administration — operating under Israel's Ministry of Economy and Industry — maintains a dedicated South Africa desk, accessible at itrade.gov.il/south-africa/. This is the primary institutional vehicle for Israel–South Africa trade and investment promotion. Understanding what it does, and how it operates within the politically constrained environment, is key to understanding the bilateral relationship.

What the Israel Trade Administration Does in South Africa
  • Trade Promotion: Organises Israeli national pavilions at South African trade shows (e.g., mining expos, agritech conferences, technology events), coordinates business delegations between the two countries, and facilitates B2B matchmaking between Israeli exporters and South African buyers.
  • Investment Promotion: Works to attract South African investment into Israel (particularly in technology and real estate) and Israeli investment into South Africa (particularly in agriculture, water technology, renewable energy, and ICT).
  • Business Opportunities: Publishes a regularly updated list of business opportunities — Israeli companies seeking South African partners, distributors, integrators, or end-users. Recent listings include companies in telecommunications (TDCOMM), medical technology (Medispec), virtual/augmented reality (Inception XR), and logistics technology (LOGINNO).
  • Market Intelligence: Provides Israeli companies with market entry analysis, regulatory guidance, and sectoral reports on the South African market. Publishes sector-focused content on opportunities in data centres, high-tech, financial services, and infrastructure.
  • Event Coordination: Maintains a calendar of trade events, conferences, and exhibitions relevant to bilateral trade, including the Mobile World Congress (MWC) and Israel's HLS & Cyber conference.
What's Publicly Visible

The South Africa desk's website is actively maintained with posts as recent as January 2026. Content includes sectoral analysis (data centres, high-tech resilience), event announcements (MWC 2026), and business opportunity listings. This indicates that — despite the political tensions — the Israeli government continues to invest in trade promotion infrastructure targeting the South African market. The desk operates as part of Israel's global network of trade offices, which spans ~40 countries and is organised under the Foreign Trade Administration.

What's Less Visible — The Political Constraints

The South Africa desk operates in an increasingly hostile political environment. Public events that explicitly promote Israeli business in South Africa risk boycott campaigns, protest disruptions, and political condemnation from the ANC government and its allies. Many trade promotion activities likely occur below the public radar — private B2B meetings, virtual matchmaking, and trade facilitation that does not involve public branding. The contrast between the professional, outward-facing trade promotion apparatus (the website, the event listings, the business opportunity posts) and the hostile political environment in which it operates is one of the defining features of the contemporary South Africa–Israel trade relationship.

Relevance to South African stakeholders: For South African businesses importing Israeli technology (irrigation systems, medical devices, cybersecurity software) or exporting to Israel (fruit, minerals, coal), the Israel Trade Administration's South Africa desk is the primary institutional point of contact. It provides market intelligence, partner identification, and trade facilitation services that can reduce the transaction costs of operating in a politically complex bilateral environment. South African companies seeking Israeli technology partners — particularly in agritech, water management, fintech, and cybersecurity — can use the desk's business opportunity listings and matchmaking services to identify potential Israeli counterparts. The desk also provides guidance on navigating Israeli import regulations, standards, and certification requirements for South African exporters.

Part 4: The Political Dynamics — Why This Relationship Is Under Constant Stress

The South Africa–Israel relationship sits at the intersection of multiple political fault lines:

The ANC–PLO Legacy

The ANC's alliance with the Palestine Liberation Organisation (PLO) dates to the anti-apartheid struggle. Nelson Mandela explicitly framed the Palestinian cause as parallel to the South African liberation struggle, famously stating that "South Africa's freedom is incomplete without the freedom of the Palestinians." This is not a recent political posture — it is embedded in the ANC's ideological DNA from the movement's founding generation. Every ANC president since Mandela has maintained strong rhetorical support for Palestine, though the intensity and practical consequences of that support have varied. The ICJ case represents the most dramatic operationalisation of this ideological commitment — moving from rhetoric to legal action with concrete diplomatic and economic consequences.

The Apartheid Framing

South Africa's government and civil society have increasingly adopted the framing that Israeli policy toward Palestinians constitutes apartheid — a term that carries unique weight in the South African context. Human rights organisations, trade unions (COSATU), and ANC-aligned civil society groups have been at the forefront of BDS campaigns, comparing Israeli policies to apartheid-era South Africa. This framing — whether one accepts its accuracy or not — has powerful mobilising force in South African domestic politics. It makes maintaining normal trade relations with Israel politically costly for any South African political party, not just the ANC. Even the DA, which is generally more sympathetic to Israel, has to navigate this framing carefully.

The BRICS Factor

South Africa's membership of BRICS — alongside China and Russia, both of which have their own complex relationships with Israel — adds another layer. China maintains robust trade with Israel (particularly in technology) while rhetorically supporting Palestine. Russia has historically had a large Israeli-Russian population and maintained functional relations until the Ukraine war complicated its positioning. South Africa's BRICS alignment creates pressure to coordinate foreign policy positions — though in practice, BRICS members have divergent Israel policies. The expansion of BRICS to include Iran, the UAE, and Egypt in 2024 further complicates the Israel dimension: the UAE has normalised relations with Israel (Abraham Accords), while Iran is Israel's most determined adversary.

The Economic Rationality Underneath

Beneath the political hostility, economic rationality persists — quietly. Israeli technology (irrigation, cybersecurity, medical devices, agritech) is genuinely valuable to South African businesses and consumers. South African raw materials (coal, minerals, fruit) are genuinely valuable to Israeli industry and consumers. Businesspeople on both sides continue to trade, invest, and seek partnerships — often below the political radar. The Israel Trade Administration's continued operation of the South Africa desk, with active posting of business opportunities and event listings into 2026, is evidence that the economic relationship has a logic of its own that survives political shocks. The question is not whether this economic logic exists — it clearly does — but whether it can withstand the escalating political confrontation, or whether political pressure will eventually overwhelm the economic complementarity.

Part 5: Future Trajectory — Where Does This Go?

The South Africa–Israel trade relationship faces three possible futures:

Scenario 1: Quiet Normalisation

The Gaza conflict reaches a durable ceasefire. South Africa maintains its rhetorical support for Palestine but de-escalates the ICJ case and allows economic relations to return to a pre-2023 trajectory. Trade recovers to ~$1 billion+. The Israel Trade Administration continues operating normally. Probability: Low-Medium — requires diplomatic resolution to the Gaza conflict and a shift in South Africa's foreign policy posture, neither of which appears imminent.

Scenario 2: Protracted Semi-Hostility

The current situation persists: political rhetoric remains hostile, the ICJ case proceeds slowly, boycott campaigns continue, but economic relations — while diminished — do not collapse entirely. Trade stabilises at $500–800 million. The Israel Trade Administration continues operating but with limited public visibility. Probability: Medium-High — reflects the most likely near-term trajectory given the entrenched positions on both sides and the absence of a clear resolution mechanism.

Scenario 3: Full Breakdown

South Africa escalates — formally severs diplomatic relations, imposes trade sanctions, enforces BDS recommendations through government procurement and trade policy. Trade collapses to minimal levels. The Israel Trade Administration closes its South Africa desk. Israeli technology and investment exit the South African market. Probability: Low-Medium — the economic cost to South Africa (loss of Israeli agritech, technology, investment) would be material, creating domestic political resistance to full severance.

The lesson of Taiwan: South Africa has precedent for severing a trade relationship entirely for political reasons — the switch from Taiwan to China in 1998 eliminated a ~$3 billion trade relationship almost overnight. The difference: Taiwan was replaced by China, which offered a vastly larger market. There is no equivalent replacement for Israel — no alternative source of Israeli agritech, cybersecurity, and medical technology that offers the same value proposition. This makes full severance more costly for South Africa than the Taiwan switch was, and thus less likely — but not impossible if the political dynamics continue to escalate.

The South Africa–Israel trade relationship is a case study in how foreign policy principles and economic rationality collide. The two economies are complementary, the institutional infrastructure for trade exists (the Israel Trade Administration's South Africa desk is active and professional), and businesses on both sides want to trade. But the political context — the ANC's historic alliance with Palestine, the ICJ case, the boycott campaigns — makes normal trade increasingly difficult. The future of the relationship depends on whether the economic logic can survive the political confrontation, or whether — as with Taiwan in 1998 — politics will override economics entirely.
