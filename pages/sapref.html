SAPREF

South African Petroleum Refineries, Durban — bought by CEF from Shell and BP for R1

SAPREF used to be a 50/50 Shell and BP joint venture, the largest crude refinery in southern Africa. The April 2022 KwaZulu-Natal floods wrecked it. Shell Downstream SA and bpSA sold the land, tanks, process units, Island View pipelines and the single buoy mooring to the Central Energy Fund for a nominal R1. The site is now state-owned. Private majors walked away; the environmental and restart bill stayed.

Owner now: CEF Group (100% Republic of South Africa)
Owner before: Shell Downstream SA and bp Southern Africa (50/50)
Purchase price: R1 (announced 2024; Competition Commission approved September 2024)
Location: South Durban, KwaZulu-Natal — linked to Island View terminal and a single buoy mooring for crude imports
Nameplate: ~180,000 barrels per day when running
Status: Non-operational since the 2022 floods
Stated plan: Restart, then a larger rebuild (CEF has publicly talked 400,000 bpd; some SANPC remarks have gone as high as 600,000)
Also called: SNPC refinery in some CEF financial commentary after the deal

SAPREF

Durban · former Shell/BP · CEF for R1

What it is

For decades SAPREF was the coastal workhorse of South Africa's liquid-fuels system: crude in over the buoy, petrol, diesel and jet out into KwaZulu-Natal and up the pipeline inland. Together with Engen's Wentworth plant (also in Durban, also shut), Astron in Cape Town, and Natref (Sasol/TotalEnergies, inland), it was why the country refined most of what it burned.

Those private refiners have been shutting or selling. SAPREF's flood damage in April 2022 was the event that ended Shell and BP's appetite. They had earmarked on the order of R3 billion of maintenance that then did not happen. Selling to CEF for R1 was a clean break: the state got a strategic site, the majors got rid of a liability.

What CEF actually bought

  • The SAPREF land in south Durban.
  • Crude and finished-product tanks.
  • Process units (idle).
  • Pipelines between SAPREF and the Island View terminal.
  • The single buoy mooring used for crude imports.

DTIC investment material (2025) already describes SAPREF as a 100% CEF Group subsidiary, owned through the Department of Mineral and Petroleum Resources. That is the same ownership chain as PetroSA: Minister → CEF → operating company. No listed stock. No private controlling owner.

Who used to buy / who would buy

When it ran, offtakers were the South African fuel market — retailers, commercial diesel, jet into King Shaka and the inland airports, and the Durban corridor that still handles on the order of 70% of national fuel volumes. A restarted SAPREF would sell into that same system. Traders and inland depots remain private; the refinery itself would be a state plant sitting next to them, the same pattern as SFF tanks next to commercial storage.

Why R1 is not a bargain

Open Secrets / Daily Maverick (July 2025) published due-diligence material showing why Shell and BP were happy with a rand: flood damage, deferred maintenance, and environmental exposure in a densely populated south Durban basin that has fought this plant for years (SDCEA opposed the purchase). CEF and SANPC argue the opposite: South Africa is now a fuel importer, refining capacity is a security-of-supply asset, and walking away from Durban would lock in that import dependence. Both can be true. A R1 asset with a multi-billion restart and a pollution legacy is a fiscal risk transferred from two oil majors onto the taxpayer.

The future

Minister Mantashe's SANPC launch remarks treated Mossel Bay GTL and SAPREF as a pair: reinstate the one, rebuild the other. CEF has talked about lifting Durban from 180,000 bpd toward 400,000 using spare land; SANPC funding notes have used a 600,000 figure. None of that is running metal. The immediate sequence is: close remaining conditions in the sale, decide restart versus greenfield rebuild, fund it, and get a social licence in south Durban that Shell and BP no longer wanted to hold.

Until then SAPREF is a CEF-owned industrial site with tanks, a buoy and a political brief. It belongs in this menu because the state owns it — not because it is producing.
