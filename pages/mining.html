⛏️ South Africa Mining Sector

🇿🇦 Overview

South Africa sits on one of the most extraordinary geological endowments on the planet. From the Witwatersrand Basin — which produced half the gold ever mined — to the Bushveld Igneous Complex — containing over 70% of the world's platinum group metals and the bulk of its chrome and vanadium — the country's mining sector has been the engine of its modern economy for over 140 years. It built Johannesburg, funded the industrialisation of an entire nation, and made South Africa synonymous with mineral wealth.

The Boom: Late 1800s – 1980s

Mining's story begins with two explosive discoveries: diamonds at Kimberley in 1869 and gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886. These weren't just mineral finds — they were nation-building events. Kimberley gave rise to Cecil Rhodes and De Beers, the most powerful monopoly in commodity history. The Witwatersrand gold rush built Johannesburg from nothing into Africa's economic capital within a decade, triggered the Second Boer War (1899-1902), and by the 1970s was producing two-thirds of the world's gold — over 1,000 tonnes per year — making South Africa the undisputed gold capital of Earth. In parallel, coal mining grew to power the entire industrial base, eventually supplying ~95% of the country's electricity through Eskom's expanding fleet of coal-fired stations. The post-war period saw the development of the Bushveld Complex's platinum group metals, along with chrome, manganese, and vanadium, establishing South Africa as the world's dominant supplier of these strategic minerals. By 1980, mining accounted for around 20% of South Africa's GDP — its peak share of the economy.

The Long Unwinding: 1990s – 2010s

The decline was gradual at first, then relentless. Gold — the sector's historic anchor — saw its share of world output fall from two-thirds to under 5% by the 2020s, as the easy ore ran out and mines chased seams deeper and deeper. By 2018, 75% of South African gold mines were unprofitable. Employment in gold alone collapsed from a peak of over 500,000 to a fraction of that. Diamonds followed a similar arc: De Beers' global market share eroded from 90% in the 1980s to under 30% by 2019, squeezed by discoveries in Canada and Australia and — more recently — by the existential threat of lab-grown diamonds. Even coal, the workhorse of the economy, entered a crisis period as Eskom's ageing fleet of "six-pack" power stations broke down with increasing frequency, Medupi and Kusile were hobbled by corruption and cost overruns, and the world began demanding a transition away from fossil fuels. The Marikana massacre of 2012 — 34 striking miners killed by police — became a symbol of the deep social and labour tensions running through the industry.

A Tale of Two Sectors: 2020s Present

Today the mining sector presents a starkly divided picture. On one side, gold and diamonds are shadows of their former selves — deep, dangerous, expensive, and in structural decline. On the other, PGMs, chrome, manganese, and vanadium remain globally dominant and increasingly strategic. The energy transition is reshaping the landscape: PGMs are critical for hydrogen fuel cells and catalytic converters; manganese and vanadium are battery metals with growing demand from the EV and grid-storage sectors; chrome is essential for stainless steel in green infrastructure. South Africa is the world's #1 producer of PGMs, chrome, manganese, and vanadium, and holds the largest reserves of several of these — a position that should be enormously valuable in a decarbonising world.

The Infrastructure Trap

But geological riches mean little without functioning infrastructure. Eskom's load shedding — which reached Stage 6 in 2023, cutting power for up to 12 hours a day — has been devastating for deep mines that require continuous ventilation, refrigeration, and pumping. Transnet's rail and port failures have choked export routes, leaving billions of rands' worth of mined ore stranded at pits while global prices surged. The Sishen–Saldanha iron ore line and the Richards Bay coal line — two of the country's most critical economic arteries — have both suffered from locomotive shortages, infrastructure theft, and maintenance backlogs. For coal, iron ore, and manganese — commodities that depend on bulk rail and port capacity — this has been a silent crisis. Mining houses have responded by investing in their own private power generation (solar and wind farms at mine sites) and even exploring private rail concessions, marking a historic shift away from reliance on state-owned utilities.

By the Numbers

The sector's economic weight has shifted but not disappeared. Mining's direct contribution to GDP has stabilised in the 7–8% range in recent years, down from the 20% peak of the 1980s, but still a significant driver of exports, foreign exchange, and industrial activity. The industry directly employs around 450,000 people — down from over 800,000 at its peak — but indirectly supports several times that number. Mining remains South Africa's single largest export earner, though the composition has changed: where gold once dominated, today it is PGMs, coal, iron ore, and manganese that bring in the revenue.

The Bottom Line

South Africa's mining sector has come full circle. It was born in a frenzy of gold and diamond rushes, powered through a century of industrialisation on coal, dominated global markets in PGMs, chrome, manganese, and vanadium, and is now navigating the most complex set of challenges in its history: deepening ore bodies, decaying state infrastructure, rising social expectations, the climate transition, and competition from new mineral provinces around the world. The geological endowment is still there — some of the richest deposits on Earth lie beneath South African soil. The question is whether the country can overcome its infrastructure, energy, and governance crises to unlock that value for another generation. The outcome will determine not just the fate of mining companies, but the trajectory of the South African economy itself.
