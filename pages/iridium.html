Iridium (Ir)

South Africa produces ~80–89% of the world's iridium — the bottleneck metal of green hydrogen
Overview Platinum Palladium Rhodium Ruthenium Iridium Osmium
What is it?

A very dense, extremely corrosion-resistant PGM (atomic number 77). The most heat- and acid-resistant of the six, and the anode catalyst that PEM electrolysers need to split water into green hydrogen.

Where is it found?

A minor metal in Bushveld PGM ore, especially UG2. Africa accounts for ~97% of mined iridium. Tiny amounts come from Russian nickel-PGM and North American operations.

What is it used for?

PEM water electrolysers, spark-plug electrodes, crucibles for growing crystals (LEDs, sapphire, high-tech substrates), chemical catalysts (including acetic acid), and medical radioisotopes.

Supply Chain

Recovered last in the PGM refinery in small batches. Sold as sponge, powder or fabricated crucibles/anodes to electrolyser OEMs, electronics crystal-growers and chemical companies. Annual primary supply is only ~7 tonnes.

What iridium is

Iridium is among the rarest stable elements in the Earth's crust and, with osmium, the densest. It melts at 2,446°C and resists aqua regia. Those extremes are why it is used where other metals fail: the tips of spark plugs, the walls of crucibles that hold molten oxides above 2,000°C, and the anode of a PEM electrolyser sitting in hot, acidic, oxygen-evolving conditions.

World primary supply is only about 230,000 ounces a year (~7 tonnes). Heraeus / SFA estimate five South African producers control ~71% of iridium and the country ~89% of primary supply; the African Development Bank puts Africa at 97% of mine output. Sibanye-Stillwater reported 64,000 oz (~2 tonnes) of iridium in 2024 — a single company, a material share of the entire global market.

Unlike ruthenium, iridium did not rally in 2025. The Minerals Council average was US$4,399/oz, down 7.6% on 2024, after several years in the $4,500–5,200 range (USGS). The hydrogen story is priced as a 2030s volume event, not a 2025 one: electrolyser makers have spent years thrifting iridium loadings so that scarce ounces can cover more gigawatts.

What it is used for

Johnson Matthey puts 2025 demand at 236,000 ounces (~7.3 tonnes):

  • Electrochemical (~100 koz, ~42%): The growth engine. Iridium oxide (often with ruthenium) coats the anode of proton-exchange-membrane water electrolysers. Chlor-alkali and other electrolytic processes also use iridium-bearing anodes. This is the green-hydrogen bottleneck: a GW-scale PEM build-out at old loadings would consume years of mine supply.
  • Other (~69 koz): Spark plugs (NGK, Bosch and others — iridium tips last far longer than copper), medical (iridium-192 for brachytherapy), and miscellaneous high-temperature uses.
  • Chemical (~34 koz): The Cativa acetic-acid process (iridium-promoted) and other process catalysts.
  • Electrical and electronics (~33 koz): Iridium crucibles used to grow single crystals — sapphire, LED substrates, and specialty oxides used in high-tech and some AI-related hardware. Northam has pointed to this crystal-growth use as a new industrial bid alongside hydrogen.

Automotive exhaust catalysts are not a meaningful iridium market. The metal's demand is industrial and, increasingly, energy-transition.

Who buys it

  • Electrolyser manufacturers in Europe, China, the US, Japan and Korea — Nel, ITM/Cummins, Siemens Energy, Plug Power, Thyssenkrupp Nucera, and a long list of Chinese PEM OEMs — plus the anode-coating specialists who sit in front of them (Heraeus, Johnson Matthey, Umicore and others).
  • Electronics crystal-growers who fabricate iridium crucibles; demand follows LED, specialty-substrate and some semiconductor capex.
  • Spark-plug makers serving global auto and small-engine OEMs. This is a stable, replacement-driven book.
  • Chemical companies running acetic-acid and related plants, especially in Asia.
  • Trade: Combined South African exports of unwrought iridium, osmium and ruthenium go to Japan, Hong Kong, the United States, China and the UK. USGS US iridium imports were 1,360 kg in 2024 and an estimated 2,200 kg in 2025.

Buyers cannot dual-source from a second geology. They can only thrift, recycle closed-loop plant metal, or wait for South African (and to a lesser extent Zimbabwean) PGM mining to deliver more UG2 ore.

Production and consumption

Primary supply was 229,000 oz in 2025 (~7.1 tonnes), essentially unchanged from 2024's 237 koz. Demand of 236 koz left a small 7,000 oz deficit. Johnson Matthey sees 2026 supply still at 229 koz against demand of 240 koz (electrochemical rising to 109 koz).

Those deficits look small next to platinum's 900 koz hole, but they are 3–5% of a 7-tonne market with almost no swing producer. African Development Bank 2023 figures already showed South Africa as the overwhelming miner. Every Bushveld shaft closure therefore has an outsized effect on the hydrogen industry's bill of materials.

The future

Iridium is the PGM most tightly bound to green hydrogen. Alkaline electrolysers do not need it; PEM electrolysers do. If governments and industry scale PEM for hydrogen used in trucks, ammonia, steel and power, iridium demand from electrochemical uses could grow faster than mine supply even after aggressive thrifting (research aims to cut loadings by 5–10×). Closed-loop recycling of spent anodes will become mandatory; it is not yet large.

The bear case is that PEM loses the electrolyser race to alkaline and solid-oxide technologies, or that thrifting plus recycling caps net new-metal demand near today's 100 koz electrochemical book. Spark plugs decline slowly with ICE. Crystal-growth demand follows electronics capex and is cyclical.

For South Africa the strategic implication is out of proportion to the ounces. Iridium is a few percent of a 6E basket by mass, but it is the metal that makes Bushveld UG2 relevant to the hydrogen economy. A country that already dominates platinum and rhodium also holds the anode metal of PEM. Whether that translates into downstream industry (anode coating, electrolyser manufacturing) or remains an export of sponge is a policy choice — the geology is already in place.
