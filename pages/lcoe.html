What each generating technology costs per megawatt-hour in South Africa — and how that has moved since 2010
Power Overview REDZs LCOE

Lazard publishes a yearly LCOE comparison for the United States. South Africa has no exact equivalent, so the closest public benchmark is the EPRI supply-side cost report prepared for Eskom's Integrated Resource Plan, supplemented by the actual prices won in the REIPPPP bid windows. This page assembles that data into the Lazard-style view: a cost range per technology, tracked over time, in rands.

Everything on this page is driven by one file — js/lcoe-data.js. Change a number there and the chart, table and CSV all follow. The values below are indicative compilations anchored on published bid outcomes and EPRI/IRP modelling, not verbatim EPRI output.

Why the South African numbers differ from Lazard's US figures

DriverEffect on South African LCOE
Cost of capital The single largest difference. A higher WACC raises the cost of every capital-heavy technology — solar, wind, nuclear, CSP — much more than it raises gas. South African project finance typically prices several hundred basis points above US utility finance, which can add 30–50% to a solar LCOE even with identical hardware costs.
Solar resource Northern Cape irradiation is among the best on earth, around 2,100–2,300 kWh/m²/year versus 1,600–1,900 in much of the US. This pushes capacity factors up and LCOE down, partly offsetting the financing penalty.
Wind resource Eastern Cape and Karoo wind is strong and steady, supporting capacity factors in the mid-30s to low 40s — competitive with good US sites.
Coal and gas fuel Domestic coal is cheap at the mine but expensive to transport; gas has no domestic supply at scale and must be imported as LNG. Both make South African thermal LCOE more volatile than the US benchmark.
Dry cooling Water constraints force dry cooling on new thermal plant, which cuts capital efficiency and raises the cost per unit of output by several percent.
Local content and logistics REIPPPP local-content thresholds, port capacity and inland transport add cost relative to a US build, though the effect is modest next to financing.
Currency Plant is largely imported and priced in dollars or euros; the rand cost therefore moves with the exchange rate independently of technology cost. At roughly R18–19 to the dollar, a US$40/MWh solar plant lands near R730–760/MWh before local costs.

How to read this without being misled

  • Technology LCOE is not system cost. The cheapest energy is not necessarily the cheapest system. Solar and wind are cheapest per MWh delivered, but a system with a high share of them also needs storage, transmission and firm capacity — costs that sit outside a technology LCOE and get recovered elsewhere.
  • Bid prices are not modelled LCOE. REIPPPP outcomes are actual contracted tariffs, which include the developer's return and any risk premium, and are struck at a moment in time. They are the best market signal for solar and wind, but they are not a technology cost model.
  • Nominal years are not comparable in real terms. A 2024 rand is worth less than a 2010 rand. Coal's apparent rise is partly inflation; solar's fall is real even after it.
  • The range matters more than the midpoint. Lazard publishes ranges because the honest answer is a band, driven by resource, financing and utilisation. A single number invites false precision.
  • Firm capacity has a price. Compare a solar-plus-4-hour-battery line, not bare solar, against nuclear or coal when the question is what keeps the lights on at 20:00.
  • Capacity factor is doing a lot of work. Nuclear and coal assume high utilisation; peaking plant assumes very little. Change the assumption and the ranking moves.

Method and sources

Method. Unsubsidised, utility-scale, full-lifecycle LCOE per technology, expressed in ZAR/MWh in nominal money of the stated year, presented as a low–high range rather than a point estimate. Midpoint lines are the arithmetic mean of the range, not a weighted central case. Solar PV and onshore wind low ends are anchored on REIPPPP bid-window tariffs; thermal, nuclear, storage, CSP and offshore wind ranges follow EPRI/IRP modelling and IRENA benchmarks, escalated to the stated year.

Primary sources

  • EPRI, Supply-Side Cost and Performance Data for Eskom Integrated Resource Planning: 2023–2024 Update — the closest South African equivalent to Lazard; LCOE and full cost/performance data for coal with and without CCS, CCGT and OCGT, nuclear (EPR, AP1000, SMR), solar PV with battery, CSP, offshore wind, storage and conversion options. Published by the Department of Mineral Resources and Energy for the IRP process.
  • Department of Mineral Resources and Energy, Integrated Resource Plan 2019 and subsequent IRP inputs — the policy demand case and cost assumptions.
  • REIPPPP bid-window results, Independent Power Producer Office — actual winning tariffs, Bid Window 1 (2011) through Bid Window 7 (2023/24). The most reliable market signal for solar PV and onshore wind in South Africa.
  • IRENA, Renewable Power Generation Costs (annual) — global and African regional LCOE benchmarks for solar, wind and CSP.
  • CSIR and Meridian Economics analyses of IRP cost assumptions — useful correctives where official modelling diverges from observed market prices.
  • Lazard, Levelized Cost of Energy+ — the methodology and presentation this page follows, for US figures.

Caveat: the ranges here are an indicative compilation for comparison and teaching. They are not official EPRI, IRP or Lazard output, and they are not a substitute for the EPRI tables when a decision depends on the number. Where a figure matters, take it from the EPRI report and the bid-window record directly — and edit js/lcoe-data.js so this page reflects your source.
