🔍 What NERSA Does

NERSA is South Africa's independent energy regulator with three core mandates.

⚡
Electricity Regulation

Under the Electricity Regulation Act 4 of 2006, NERSA:

  • Issues licences for generation, transmission, distribution, trading, and import/export of electricity
  • Approves Eskom's tariff applications through Multi-Year Price Determinations (MYPD)
  • Sets conditions for electricity supply and grid access
  • Enforces compliance with the Electricity Regulation Act and licence conditions
  • Approves municipal tariffs and bulk electricity prices
  • Regulates the transition to a competitive wholesale electricity market (SAWEM)
🔥
Piped-Gas Regulation

Under the Gas Act 48 of 2001, NERSA:

  • Issues licences for gas trading, storage, transmission, distribution, and LNG operations
  • Sets and approves gas tariffs and pipeline access conditions
  • Regulates the construction and operation of gas facilities
  • Enforces gas industry codes of conduct
  • Approves gas import and export applications
  • Oversees the development of South Africa's gas market
🛢️
Petroleum Pipelines Regulation

Under the Petroleum Pipelines Act 60 of 2003, NERSA:

  • Issues licences for petroleum pipeline construction and operation
  • Regulates pipeline tariffs and access conditions
  • Enforces the Petroleum Pipelines Code
  • Monitors compliance with environmental and safety standards
  • Adjudicates disputes between pipeline owners and users
  • Promotes equitable access to petroleum pipeline infrastructure

📋 How NERSA Handles Eskom Rate Cases: The MYPD Process

The Multi-Year Price Determination (MYPD) is NERSA's primary mechanism for setting Eskom's allowable revenue and tariffs. It's a complex, multi-stage process designed to balance Eskom's financial sustainability with consumer protection.

1 Eskom Submits Revenue Application

Eskom files a detailed application with NERSA projecting its revenue requirements for the next 3–5 years. This includes: forecast operating costs, fuel costs, capital expenditure, depreciation, return on assets, debt service costs, and expected sales volumes. The application runs thousands of pages and is supported by financial models, plant performance data, and demand forecasts.

2 Regulatory Review & Due Diligence

NERSA's technical team reviews Eskom's application for reasonableness. They scrutinise cost assumptions, benchmark against international utilities, audit plant performance data, verify capital expenditure plans, and identify disallowed costs (e.g. inefficiencies, excessive executive pay, or non-prudent spending). NERSA may appoint independent consultants.

3 Public Hearings & Stakeholder Consultation

NERSA publishes Eskom's application and its own analysis for public comment. Public hearings are held in all nine provinces where consumers, business groups, unions, and civil society can make submissions. Key stakeholders include: National Treasury, Department of Mineral Resources & Energy, organised business (BUSSA), unions (NUM, NUMSA), and consumer groups.

4 NERSA Deliberation & Decision

The Energy Regulator (NERSA's Board of nine members) deliberates based on the technical report and public input. They issue a decision specifying: the total allowable revenue for each year, the maximum tariff increase (%), the Regulatory Asset Base (RAB) value, the allowed return on assets, and any conditions Eskom must meet (e.g. maintenance targets, EAF improvements, cost-reduction milestones).

5 Implementation & Compliance Monitoring

Eskom implements the approved tariffs. NERSA monitors compliance through quarterly reports, annual performance reviews, and audits. If Eskom fails to meet conditions (e.g. EAF targets), NERSA can impose penalties or adjust future allowances. Eskom can also apply for "regulatory clearing accounts" to recover under-recoveries from prior years.

⚖️ Judicial Review (If Disputed)

If Eskom disagrees with NERSA's decision, it can apply to the High Court for judicial review under Section 31 of the Electricity Regulation Act. This happened with MYPD6 — Eskom challenged NERSA's Generation RAB calculation, leading to the R54 billion settlement in July 2025.

💡 Key principle: NERSA does not set Eskom's profits. It determines the allowable revenue — the maximum amount Eskom is permitted to collect from customers. The tariff (c/kWh) is derived by dividing allowable revenue by expected sales. If sales fall short, Eskom bears the risk (or applies for a RCA adjustment).
MYPD Framework Evolution
  • MYPD1 (2007–2010): Initial 3-year price path
  • MYPD2 (2010–2013): 3-year path after 2008 crisis
  • MYPD3 (2013–2018): 5-year path, then abandoned
  • MYPD4 (2019–2022): COVID-disrupted period
  • MYPD5 (2022–2025): Single-year extensions
  • MYPD6 (2025–2028): Current — R54bn settled
Key Regulatory Concepts
  • RAB — Regulatory Asset Base: The value of Eskom's assets on which it earns a return
  • WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital: The allowed return on RAB
  • EAF — Energy Availability Factor: Plant performance metric
  • RCA — Regulatory Clearing Account: True-up mechanism
  • Section 34 Determination: Ministerial approval for new generation capacity

📌 Current Matters Before NERSA

The most significant regulatory decisions currently in progress or recently decided.

⚡ MYPD6 Settlement Implementation
Active

Following the High Court settlement in July 2025 admitting R54 billion in errors, NERSA is implementing revised tariff determinations for 2025/26–2027/28. The corrected tariffs mean higher increases for consumers than originally announced.

Status: Settlement approved Aug 2025. Tariffs published Feb 2026. Implementation ongoing.

🔌 Grid Access Rules & Capacity Allocation
Decided Nov 2025

NERSA approved new grid access rules to level the playing field between Eskom and IPPs. Key changes: remove Eskom's preferential access to transmission capacity, implement firm access rights, and create a secondary capacity trading market. Effective January 2026.

Impact: Enables IPPs to wheel power through the grid on equal terms — a critical enabler for private generation investment.

📝 Section 34 Determinations (New Capacity)
Active

Under Section 34 of the Electricity Regulation Act, the Minister of Electricity & Energy must make determinations for new generation capacity. NERSA reviews these and grants licences. Current matters include: 2,500 MW nuclear procurement, additional REIPPP bid windows, and private transmission projects.

Status: Draft regulations for private transmission published Apr 2025. Public comment period closed.

🏗️ SAWEM Market Rules Approval
Draft Stage

The South African Wholesale Electricity Market (SAWEM) is South Africa's new competitive electricity market. NERSA must approve the Market Code, Conduct Rules, and Pricing Methodology before go-live. Drafts were published for comment in 2024–2025.

Status: Market Code finalisation in progress. Target operational date: 2026–2027.

🔥 Gas Licensing Framework Update
Active

With the Gas Master Plan driving LNG import infrastructure, NERSA is updating its gas licensing framework. Applications for LNG regasification terminals (Richards Bay, Coega, Saldanha), gas-to-power plants, and pipeline expansions are under review.

Status: Multiple LNG licence applications under evaluation. Pipeline tariff methodology being reviewed.

🏘️ Municipal Electricity Tariff Approvals
Recurring

NERSA must approve all municipal electricity tariffs annually. With Eskom's bulk tariff increases flowing through, municipalities are under pressure. Several municipalities have defaulted on Eskom payments (municipal debt ~R74bn). NERSA is under court pressure to process tariffs faster so municipalities can set lawful budgets.

Status: Ongoing annual cycle. High Court case (Afriforum v NERSA, Oct 2025) ordered faster processing.

📅 Key NERSA Decisions: Timeline

Major regulatory decisions that have shaped South Africa's energy landscape.

2006
Electricity Regulation Act enacted

NERSA established as the custodian and enforcer of the national electricity regulatory framework, replacing the National Electricity Regulator (NER).

2007–2010
MYPD1: First Multi-Year Price Determination

Set Eskom's revenue path following the 2008 load-shedding crisis. Approved average increases of ~27% per year to fund the build programme (Medupi, Kusile).

2011
REIPPP Bid Window 1 Approval

NERSA licenced the first renewable energy IPPs under the Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme. This unlocked SA's first large-scale wind and solar projects.

2013
MYPD3: 5-Year Determination

Approved 5-year price path (2013–2018). Eskom later abandoned MYPD3, claiming under-recovery. Led to regulatory clearing account claims and disputes.

2019
MYPD4: 3-Year Determination

Approved increases of 9.41%, 8.76%, and 15.06% for 2019/20–2021/22. Included conditions on plant performance, cost reduction, and unbundling milestones.

2021
Nuclear Procurement Approval

NERSA approved the procurement of 2,500 MW of nuclear power under Section 34. This decision was controversial — critics argued it was premature without a clear cost-benefit analysis.

2022
Eskom Unbundling & NTCSA Establishment

NERSA supported the legal separation of Eskom's transmission entity (NTCSA). Licenced NTCSA as an independent transmission utility with open-access obligations.

Jan 2025
ERA Amendments Commence

Amendments to the Electricity Regulation Act took effect, enabling private transmission projects, removing the 1 MW generation licence threshold, and strengthening NERSA's enforcement powers.

Jun 2025
MYPD6 Decision (Original)

NERSA published its MYPD6 revenue decision for 2025/26–2027/28. Eskom immediately challenged it, citing a R107 billion shortfall due to errors in the Generation RAB calculation.

Jul 2025
R54 Billion Settlement

NERSA admitted errors in its MYPD6 calculation. Settled with Eskom for R54 billion additional revenue over three years — significantly higher tariffs than originally announced. Admitting the error was unprecedented for NERSA and sparked political backlash.

Nov 2025
Grid Access Reform Package

NERSA announced sweeping grid access changes: removed Eskom's priority access rights, implemented firm access rights, approved secondary capacity trading, and introduced non-discriminatory connection rules. Called the "most significant electricity market reform since 1994."

Jan 2026
Grid Access Rules Take Effect

New grid connection and capacity allocation rules became effective. IPPs can now access transmission capacity on equal terms with Eskom. NERSA began processing connection applications under the new framework.

Feb 2026
Revised MYPD6 Tariffs Published

Following the court settlement, NERSA confirmed the revised tariff increases for 2025/26–2027/28. Eskom's peak rate rose to 684.59 c/kWh for 2025/26 — a ~12.74% increase reflecting the corrected RAB.

🏛️ Governance & Structure

How NERSA is constituted and how it operates.

Board Composition

NERSA is governed by a Board of nine members (the "Energy Regulator") appointed by the Minister of Mineral Resources and Energy, in consultation with Parliament. The Board includes a Chairperson, CEO, and seven other members with expertise in:

  • Energy regulation — electricity, gas, petroleum
  • Economics & finance — tariff setting, cost modelling
  • Engineering — power systems, pipelines, safety
  • Law — regulatory and administrative law
  • Consumer protection — public interest advocacy
  • Environmental science — climate, emissions, sustainability

Current Chairperson: Thembani Bukula
Current CEO: Nomalanga Sithole

Regulatory Divisions

NERSA's operational structure is divided into four regulatory divisions, each headed by a Senior Manager:

  • Electricity Regulation Division — Licensing, tariff approvals, compliance monitoring, MYPD management, market rules, grid access regulation
  • Gas Regulation Division — Gas licensing, pipeline tariffs, LNG project approvals, gas market development
  • Petroleum Pipelines Regulation Division — Pipeline licensing, tariff regulation, code enforcement, dispute resolution
  • Corporate Services — Finance, HR, IT, legal, communications, stakeholder management
Funding

NERSA is funded through regulatory levies charged to licensees (Eskom, IPPs, gas companies, pipeline operators). The levy is typically 0.15 c/kWh on electricity sales — a tiny fraction of the tariff but sufficient to fund the regulator's R200m+ annual budget.

⚠️ Criticisms & Challenges

NERSA faces significant scrutiny and institutional challenges that affect its credibility and effectiveness.

R54 Billion MYPD6 Error

The most damaging episode in NERSA's history. Admitting to a R54 billion calculation error in Eskom's revenue determination severely damaged public trust. Parliament summoned NERSA for an explanation in September 2025. The error was attributed to "incorrect application of the Generation RAB methodology" — a fundamental flaw in the regulator's core function.

Regulatory Independence Questions

Critics argue NERSA lacks true independence from the executive. Board members are appointed by the Minister. Major decisions (e.g. nuclear procurement, Section 34 determinations) originate from the Minister, with NERSA's role reduced to "rubber stamping." The energy regulator should be insulated from political pressure to function effectively.

Capacity & Skills Constraints

NERSA struggles to attract and retain skilled technical staff (engineers, economists, financial modellers) due to public-sector salary constraints. Eskom's applications are enormously complex — requiring deep expertise in power systems, financial modelling, and regulatory accounting. The MYPD6 error partly reflects these capacity limitations.

Slowness of Decision-Making

NERSA has been criticised for lengthy processing times — licence applications can take 12–18 months, tariff approvals often miss budget deadlines, and market rule approvals have delayed the SAWEM launch. The High Court (Afriforum case, Oct 2025) ordered NERSA to process municipal tariff applications within prescribed timeframes.
