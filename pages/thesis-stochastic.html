🏀 Stochastic Dynamical Systems

The mathematical bedrock of the SAWEM ABM-MDP-OPF framework — from the flip of a coin to the noise-driven evolution of a power market.

🎭 The Big Picture — What This Is and Why It Matters
💬 Layman's Introduction

Imagine you're trying to predict where a leaf will land after falling into a river. You know the flow of the river (the deterministic dynamics). But the leaf also gets jostled by random eddies, bumps into other leaves, and catches the wind (stochastic noise). You cannot predict its exact path — but you can describe the probability of where it might be a minute from now.

A stochastic dynamical system is exactly this: a system whose evolution is governed partly by known rules and partly by randomness. You can never say “the system will be at X at time T” — instead you say “there is a 73% probability it will be near X.” This is not a weakness of the model; it is a faithful representation of reality. The price of electricity next hour depends on known costs and dispatch rules and on random outages, wind gusts, demand spikes, and strategic guesses by competitors.

This page climbs the ladder of abstraction from the simplest probability concepts up through the full machinery of stochastic differential equations, Fokker-Planck theory, and noise-driven nonlinear dynamics. Every piece of math here is used somewhere in the SAWEM framework — whether it is the random forced outage of a coal station, the Wiener process underlying an agent's Q-learning exploration, or the Fokker-Planck equation that governs the evolution of the SMP distribution over time.

📚 From Stochastic Processes by Richard F. Bass (Cambridge, 2011):
“Probability theory is about assigning numbers to events in a consistent way, and stochastic processes are about families of random variables indexed by time. The key idea is that we can model uncertainty not as a single number but as a distribution that evolves.”

■ Part 1 — Probability: The Language of Uncertainty
💬 What Is Probability, Really?

When you say “there's a 30% chance of rain tomorrow,” you are making a statement about a probability space. You have a set of possible outcomes (rain, no rain), a collection of events (any combination of outcomes you care about), and a probability measure that assigns numbers between 0 and 1 to each event. The rules are simple: the probability of the whole space is 1, probabilities are never negative, and the probability of disjoint events add. That's it — that's the entire axiomatic foundation of probability theory (Kolmogorov, 1933).

1.1 The Probability Space (Ω, ℐ, P)
SymbolNameMeaningSAWEM Example

Key axioms (Kolmogorov, 1933): (i) P(A) ≥ 0 for all A ∈ ℐ. (ii) P(Ω) = 1. (iii) If A1, A2, ... are pairwise disjoint (mutually exclusive), then P(∤ Ai) = Σ P(Ai).

1.2 Conditional Probability & Bayes' Rule

The probability of event A given that event B occurred is:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)   provided P(B) > 0

Bayes' Theorem lets us update probabilities when we see evidence:

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B)

SAWEM use: A generator agent updates its belief about the probability that competitors are withholding capacity (A) given that it observes a high SMP (B). This is the core of Bayesian learning in the MDP.

1.3 Expectation & Variance

The expected value (mean) of a random variable X is the probability-weighted average of its possible values:

𝓷[X] = Σ xi · P(X = xi)   (discrete)
𝓷[X] = ∫ x · f(x) dx   (continuous, where f(x) is the PDF)

Expectation is linear: 𝓷[aX + bY] = a𝓷[X] + b𝓷[Y]. This is crucial — the expected profit of a portfolio of generators is the sum of their expected individual profits, even if they are correlated.

The variance measures spread:

Var(X) = 𝓷[(X − 𝓷[X])2] = 𝓷[X2] − (𝓷[X])2

Standard deviation: σ = √Var(X). In SAWEM, the volatility of SMP is precisely the standard deviation of hourly SMP over a year — a critical input to investment decisions.

1.4 Joint, Marginal & Conditional Distributions

When we have two random variables X and Y (e.g., SMP and wind output), their joint distribution f(x,y) describes the probability of both taking specific values. The marginal of X is:

fX(x) = ∫ f(x,y) dy   (integrate out y)

Two variables are independent iff f(x,y) = fX(x) · fY(y). Their covariance measures linear dependence:

Cov(X,Y) = 𝓷[(X − 𝓷[X])(Y − 𝓷[Y])]

SAWEM use: wind and solar output are negatively correlated with SMP (the merit-order effect). Covariance between renewable output and price is the key risk metric for CfD valuation.

■ Part 2 — Random Variables & The Distributions That Matter
💬 Why Distributions Are Like Fingerprints

Every random phenomenon has a characteristic shape — its distribution. The number of customers entering a store in an hour has a Poisson distribution. Human height is (approximately) Gaussian. Wind speeds follow a Weibull distribution. Once you know the distribution, you know everything probabilistic about that phenomenon. The math that follows is just the machinery for describing, combining, and transforming these shapes.

2.1 Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)
FX(x) = P(X ≤ x)

The CDF is a non-decreasing function from 0 to 1. It always exists for any random variable. From it we derive the probability density function (PDF) for continuous variables:

fX(x) = dFX(x)/dx   ⇒   P(a < X ≤ b) = ∫ab fX(x) dx

2.3 The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Perhaps the most important theorem in all of probability. It says: the sum (or average) of many independent, identically distributed random variables is approximately normally distributed, regardless of the original distribution.

If X1,...,Xn are i.i.d. with mean μ and variance σ2, then
(¯X − μ) / (σ/√n)   →   N(0,1)   as n → ∞

Why this matters for SAWEM: hourly SMP aggregated over a year is the sum of 8,760 correlated random variables. While the CLT technically requires independence, there are versions for weakly dependent processes. So annual average SMP is approximately normal — which is why we can use standard deviation as a risk metric.

2.4 Moment Generating Functions & Characteristic Functions

These are “generators” of all moments of a distribution. The moment generating function (MGF):

MX(t) = 𝓷[etX]

The k-th derivative of MX(t) evaluated at t=0 gives the k-th moment: M(k)X(0) = 𝓷[Xk]. The characteristic function φX(t) = 𝓷[eitX] always exists and uniquely determines the distribution. This is the tool used to prove the CLT and to derive the distribution of sums of random variables in the OPF output analysis.

■ Part 3 — Stochastic Processes: Randomness Over Time
💬 From Snapshots to Movies

A random variable is a snapshot — one random number. A stochastic process is a movie — a whole family of random variables {Xt}, one for each time t. The price of electricity at 3pm tomorrow is a random variable. The entire trajectory of prices for the next 365 days is a stochastic process. This is what our model actually generates: a random path through the space of possible market outcomes.

3.1 Definition

A stochastic process is a collection of random variables {Xt : t ∈ T} defined on a common probability space (Ω, ℐ, P). The index set T is usually time (discrete: T = {0,1,2,...}; continuous: T = [0,∞)).

Each fixed outcome ω ∈ Ω gives a trajectory or realization t → Xt(ω). The collection of all possible trajectories is the ensemble.

3.2 Key Properties
PropertyDefinitionSAWEM Relevance
Stationarity Joint distribution of (Xt1,...,Xtn) same as (Xt1+τ,...,Xtn+τ) for all τ Hourly SMP is not stationary (has diurnal and seasonal patterns). But de-seasonalized SMP innovations may be weakly stationary — required for many time-series forecasting models.
Markov property P(Xt+1 | Xt, Xt−1, ...) = P(Xt+1 | Xt) The central assumption of the MDP framework — the future SMP depends on the past only through the current state. This is both a simplification and a modeling choice we validate empirically.
Martingale 𝓷[Xt+1 | Xt, ...] = Xt Under risk-neutral pricing, the discounted SMP is a martingale. Used for derivatives pricing on electricity (swaps, options, CfDs).

Autocorrelation ρ(k) = Cov(Xt, Xt+k) / Var(Xt) SMP at hour t is correlated with SMP at hour t+1 (persistence from unit commitment, ramping constraints). Autocorrelation decays over hours to days.
3.3 The Wiener Process (Brownian Motion) — The Atom of Stochastic Calculus

Also called standard Brownian motion, denoted Wt or Bt. This is the fundamental building block of all continuous-time stochastic calculus — the equivalent of the sine function in trigonometry or the exponential in calculus.

Properties that define Wt:

  1. W0 = 0 almost surely.
  2. Wt has independent increments: for 0 ≤ t1 < t2 < ..., the increments Wt2 − Wt1, Wt3 − Wt2, ... are independent.
  3. Wt has Gaussian increments: Wt − Ws ~ N(0, t−s).
  4. Wt has continuous paths (almost surely).

Critical fact: Wt is nowhere differentiable. Its derivative dWt/dt does not exist in the ordinary sense. This is why we need stochastic calculus instead of ordinary calculus.

📚 Bass on the Wiener process:
“Brownian motion is the fundamental continuous-time martingale. Every continuous martingale can be represented as a time-changed Brownian motion. If you understand Brownian motion, you understand the heart of stochastic processes.”
3.4 Gaussian White Noise

Formally, white noise ξ(t) is the (generalized) derivative of the Wiener process: ξ(t) = dWt/dt. Since Wt is not differentiable, ξ(t) exists only as a generalized function (distribution). Its autocorrelation is the Dirac delta:

𝓷[ξ(t)ξ(s)] = δ(t − s)

Physically: white noise has infinite power spread uniformly across all frequencies (analogy: white light has all frequencies). In practice we approximate it with noise whose correlation time is much shorter than the system's dynamics.

3.5 Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) Process — “Colored” Noise

The OU process is the only stationary, Gaussian, Markovian diffusion process. It adds a mean-reverting drift to white noise:

dYt = −θ(Yt − μ) dt + σ dWt

Parameters: θ = mean-reversion rate (1/τ, the correlation time), μ = long-term mean, σ = volatility.

Autocorrelation: 𝓷[ (Yt − μ)(Ys − μ) ] = (σ2/2θ) e−θ|t−s|.

Stationary distribution: Y∞ ~ N(μ, σ2/2θ).

SAWEM use: OU processes model mean-reverting fuel prices (coal, gas), temperature (load driver), and the exploration noise in DDPG actor-critic reinforcement learning (the OU process is the standard choice for continuous action exploration in RL because it produces temporally correlated exploration).

3.6 Poisson Process & Jumps

While the Wiener process has continuous paths, many real systems experience jumps — discrete events at random times. The Poisson process Nt counts the number of events up to time t, with:

  • N0 = 0
  • Independent increments
  • Nt − Ns ~ Pois(λ(t−s))

SAWEM use: generator forced outages are modeled as Poisson events (each unit fails with intensity λ = 1/MTBF). The compound Poisson process adds random jump sizes — used for load spikes (demand jumps up by X MW when a large industrial customer starts a new process) and for fuel price jumps (geopolitical shock to gas price).

3.7 The Power Spectrum & Wiener-Khintchine Theorem

For a stationary process Xt, the power spectral density SX(f) tells us how much variance is at each frequency f. It is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function:

SX(f) = ∫−∞∞ RX(τ) e−i2πfτ dτ

SAWEM use: SMP has known spectral peaks at 24h (diurnal), 12h, 168h (weekly), 8760h (seasonal). The spectrum of residual demand (after renewables) tells us what balancing requirements the system operator must meet. A “red” spectrum (more power at low frequencies) means longer-duration storage is needed.

■ Part 4 — Stochastic Differential Equations: Adding Noise to Dynamics
💬 When the Path Itself Is Random

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) like dX/dt = a(X) tells you exactly how X changes at every instant. A stochastic differential equation (SDE) adds a random kick at every instant: dX = a(X) dt + b(X) dWt. The dWt term is an increment of the Wiener process — a Gaussian random number with variance dt. The result is not a single trajectory but a probability distribution of possible trajectories. This is the mathematical language of our ABM: each agent's state evolves according to an SDE (or its discrete-time analogue, the Markov decision process).

Noise strength independent of state. Easier matematicaly. In SAWEM: fuel price shocks are additive to generator cost.

4.3 Ito vs. Stratonovich Calculus — The Deepest Subtlety

When b depends on X (multiplicative noise), the SDE is ambiguous: how do we interpret the product b(Xt) dWt? The Wiener process is so irregular that the ordinary Riemann-Stieltjes integral doesn't work. Two interpretations dominate:

① Ito interpretation

The integrand is evaluated at the left endpoint of each subinterval: b(Xt) dWt means b(Xt) · (Wt+dt − Wt).

  • 𝓷[b(Xt)dWt] = 0 (martingale property — very useful)
  • Chain rule requires Ito's lemma (extra term − ½b2f'' )
  • Standard in finance, standard in SDE numerical solvers (Euler-Maruyama is Ito)
② Stratonovich interpretation

The integrand is evaluated at the midpoint: b((Xt+Xt+dt)/2) dWt.

  • Obey ordinary chain rule (like regular calculus!)
  • Physically natural for systems where noise is continuous
  • Appears in the limit of colored noise as correlation time → 0
  • Can convert Ito ↔ Stratonovich: add/subtract ½bb' dt
📚 Bass on the Ito/Stratonovich choice:
“The Ito integral has the martingale property, which makes it powerful for financial modeling. The Stratonovich integral is more natural for physical systems. Fortunately, any SDE written in one form can be rewritten in the other. The choice is a modeling decision, not a mathematical truth.”
4.4 Ito's Lemma — The Chain Rule of Stochastic Calculus

If Xt follows dX = a dt + b dW, and we define a new process Yt = f(Xt, t), then Ito's lemma tells us the SDE for Y:

dY = [ ∂f/∂t + a · ∂f/∂x + ½ b2 · ∂2f/∂x2 ] dt + b · ∂f/∂x dW

The term ½ b2 f'' is the Ito correction — it arises because dW ∼ √dt, so second-order terms in the Taylor expansion contribute at order dt. This is why stochastic calculus differs from ordinary calculus.

Example — Geometric Brownian Motion: If dS = μS dt + σS dW, then applying Ito's lemma to f(S) = log(S) gives:

d(log S) = (μ − σ2/2) dt + σ dW

Hence St = S0 exp[(μ − σ2/2)t + σWt]. The −σ2/2 term is the volatility drag — a higher volatility reduces the expected growth rate of the level, even though the drift in the original SDE is μ. This is crucial for modeling commodity prices (coal, gas) in the SAWEM model.

4.5 Numerical Integration of SDEs — Euler-Maruyama

Most SDEs cannot be solved analytically. The standard numerical method is the Euler-Maruyama scheme (the stochastic analogue of forward Euler):

Xt+Δt = Xt + a(Xt, t) · Δt + b(Xt, t) · ΔWt
where ΔWt ~ N(0, Δt) is drawn from a Gaussian random number generator

Key insight: the noise term scales with √Δt, not Δt. This means halving the time step reduces the deterministic change by half but the random change only by √(½) ≈ 0.707. This is why SDEs produce jagged paths that don't smooth out with smaller steps.

Higher-order methods: Milstein scheme (adds a second derivative term from Ito's lemma), Runge-Kutta for SDEs. These matter when b'(x) is large (strong multiplicative noise).

■ Part 5 — The Fokker-Planck Equation: From Trajectories to Densities
💬 From Individual Paths to the River's Current

Instead of tracking one random leaf (one trajectory), imagine dye released into the river. The dye spreads and its concentration at each point evolves according to a deterministic equation — the Fokker-Planck equation. It tells you how the probability density of the system evolves in time. The individual trajectories are random, but the density evolves deterministically. This is the bridge between the micro (SDEs, agent paths) and the macro (market statistics, emergent phenomena).

5.1 Derivation from the SDE

Given the SDE dXt = a(Xt, t) dt + b(Xt, t) dWt, the probability density p(x,t) of Xt satisfies:

∂p(x,t)/∂t = − ∂/∂x [ a(x,t) p(x,t) ] + ½ ∂2/∂x2 [ b2(x,t) p(x,t) ]
TermNameEffect on Density
−∂(ap)/∂xDrift termTranslates the density in the direction of the drift velocity a(x). Like advection in fluid dynamics.
½∂2(b2p)/∂x2Diffusion termSpreads the density out. Like heat diffusion.
5.2 Stationary Solution

Setting ∂p/∂t = 0 gives the stationary (long-run, steady-state) distribution:

ps(x) = (C / b2(x)) exp[ ∫ 2a(x') / b2(x') dx' ]

where C is a normalizing constant. For the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (a = −θx, b = σ), this recovers the Gaussian:

ps(x) = (θ/(πσ2))1/2 exp(−θx2/σ2)

SAWEM use: The stationary distribution of SMP under a given scenario tells us the long-run average price, the probability of extreme prices (tail risk), and the value-at-risk for investment decisions.

5.3 The Chapman-Kolmogorov Equation — The Fundamental Principle

The Fokker-Planck equation is the differential form of the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation, which is the most general consistency condition for Markov processes:

p(x3, t3 | x1, t1) = ∫ p(x3, t3 | x2, t2) · p(x2, t2 | x1, t1) dx2

This says: to go from state 1 to state 3, you can go via any intermediate state 2, and sum (integrate) over all possibilities. This is the Master equation for jump processes, the Liouville equation for deterministic flows, and the Fokker-Planck equation for diffusion processes — all are special cases.

5.4 First-Passage Time — When Will Something Bad Happen?

A critical question: how long does it take for a stochastic process to first reach a threshold? This is the first-passage time problem.

τ = inf{ t ≥ 0 : Xt = xthreshold | X0 = x0 }

The mean first-passage time (MFPT) for a one-dimensional diffusion can be computed from the Fokker-Planck equation:

T(x0) = ∫x0xT [ 2 / b2(y) · ∫−∞y ps(z)−1 dz ] dy

SAWEM use: the time until SMP exceeds a threshold (e.g., R5000/MWh) is a first-passage time. The time until a coal station's cumulative profit drops below zero (triggering retirement) is another. The probability of an “adequacy crisis” (unserved energy > threshold) is a first-passage problem in the multi-dimensional state space of capacities and demands.

5.5 Multi-Dimensional Fokker-Planck

In the SAWEM ABM, the state space is high-dimensional (thousands of agents). The Fokker-Planck equation generalizes to:

∂p/∂t = − Σi ∂(aip)/∂xi + ½ Σi,j ∂2(Dijp)/∂xi∂xj

where D = bbT is the diffusion matrix. In practice, the full multi-dimensional Fokker-Planck equation is computationally intractable for high-dimensional systems — this is why we simulate trajectories (the ABM approach) rather than solving the density evolution directly.

■ Part 6 — Nonlinear Dynamics: When Noise Meets Bifurcations
💬 The Elephant in the Room: Non-linearity

Linear systems are boring — they just grow, decay, or oscillate. The interesting world is nonlinear: where the effect is not proportional to the cause, where small changes can produce large responses, where systems can switch abruptly between different behaviors (bifurcations). Add noise to a nonlinear system and you get genuinely new phenomena that exist in neither the deterministic nor the purely random world: noise-induced transitions, stochastic resonance, and noise-stabilized states. This is the mathematical reality of a power market undergoing a structural transition from coal to renewables.

6.1 Deterministic Dynamical Systems Primer

A deterministic dynamical system is described by an ODE:

dx/dt = f(x; μ)

where μ is a parameter. The fixed points satisfy f(x*) = 0. Their stability is determined by the eigenvalue of the derivative f'(x*): if f'(x*) < 0, the fixed point is stable (attractor); if > 0, unstable.

Pitchfork (supercritical) Stable fixed point becomes unstable; two new stable points appear dx/dt = μx − x3 Market breaks symmetry: two equilibria — high-price (coal dominant) vs. low-price (RE dominant)
Hopf (supercritical) Fixed point → limit cycle (oscillations) dr/dt = μr − r3, dθ/dt = ω Investment cycles: boom (overbuild) → bust (price collapse) → boom

6.2 Noise Can Induce New States — The Horsthemke-Lefever Insight

In their landmark 1984 book Noise-Induced Transitions, Horsthemke and Lefever showed that noise can create new stationary states that have no deterministic counterpart. Consider a bistable system (two stable fixed points, one unstable):

dx/dt = x(1 − x2) + noise

Without noise, the system sits in one of the two wells. With noise, it can transition between wells (random switching). As noise increases, the stationary distribution changes shape — at high noise, the two peaks merge into one. The number of modes of the stationary distribution can change at a critical noise strength, which is a noise-induced bifurcation (or “phenomenological bifurcation”).

SAWEM meaning: the power market has multiple “attractors”: a coal-dominated equilibrium (high prices, high emissions), a gas-dominated equilibrium (medium prices), and a renewables-dominated equilibrium (low prices, high volatility). Policy (carbon tax, RE subsidies) shifts these attractors. Noise (outages, weather, demand shocks) creates transitions between them. The JET P is a deliberate attempt to push the system from the coal attractor to the RE attractor.

6.3 Stochastic Resonance & Coherence Resonance

Stochastic resonance: a weak periodic signal (e.g., daily demand cycle) is amplified by the right amount of noise. Too little noise, the signal doesn't cross the threshold. Too much noise, the signal is buried. There is an optimal noise level where the signal-to-noise ratio is maximized.

📚 Bass on stochastic resonance:
“The counterintuitive result that adding noise can improve signal detection is one of the most beautiful discoveries in nonlinear dynamics. It has been observed in neurons, climate systems, and even stock markets.”

Coherence resonance: even without a periodic signal, an excitable system near a bifurcation can produce self-organized oscillations at an optimal noise level. The noise kicks the system out of the stable state, it takes a characteristic time to return, then gets kicked again — producing a noisy oscillation with a well-defined timescale.

SAWEM connection: the investment cycle in generation capacity is a noise-driven oscillator. Random demand growth + random outages create price spikes, which trigger investment, which depresses prices, which stops investment, which eventually leads to scarcity — and the cycle repeats. The period of the cycle depends on construction lead times and the volatility of demand and fuel prices.

6.4 Large Deviations & Rare Events

In many stochastic systems, the most important events are the rarest: a 1-in-100 year drought, a simultaneous triple outage, a financial crisis. The theory of large deviations (Freidlin-Wentzell, 1984) gives the probability of these rare events in terms of an action functional:

P( trajectory ≈ φ ) ≈ exp( − I[φ] / ε2 )

where I[φ] is the rate function (analogous to the action in classical mechanics) and ε is the noise amplitude. The most likely path for a rare event minimizes I[φ] — the instanton or most probable escape path.

SAWEM use: the probability of a “blackout” event (unserved energy exceeding a threshold) can be estimated via large deviation theory rather than brute-force Monte Carlo. The most likely sequence of events leading to a blackout can be identified — informing system operators about which risks to mitigate first.

7.3 The Feedback Loop as a Stochastic Dynamical System

The entire SAWEM ABM-MDP-OPF framework can be viewed as one giant stochastic dynamical system. The “state” Xt is the vector of all agent states plus the environment (capacities, fuel stocks, financial positions, policies). The “evolution” consists of:

  1. Agents choose actions ai,t = πi(Oi(Xt)) — a deterministic or stochastic mapping
  2. OPF solves: [λt, pg,t, fℓ,t] = OPF({ai,t}, Dt) — a deterministic optimization
  3. Exogenous shocks hit: dWfuel, dWdemand, dWwind, dNoutages — stochastic increments
  4. State updates: Xt+dt = F(Xt, at, OPFt, dWt, dNt)

The resulting process Xt is a piecewise-deterministic Markov process (PDMP): deterministic flows (OPF, dispatch) punctuated by random jumps (outages, investment decisions, policy changes). This class of process has well-defined mathematical properties — the generator of its semigroup can be written, its stationary distribution can in principle be characterized, and the conditions for ergodicity can be checked.

7.4 Recommended Reading Path

If this page was overwhelming (and it should be — this is a graduate-level topic spread over several fields), here is a curated path:

  1. Start with: Bass, Stochastic Processes (Cambridge, 2011) — Chapters 1–4 on probability foundations and discrete-time Markov chains. The book is concise and rigorous.
  2. Then: Gardiner, Handbook of Stochastic Methods (Springer, 4th ed., 2009) — Chapters 1–4 on the Fokker-Planck equation and SDEs. This is the physicist's practical reference.
  3. For the nonlinear dynamics: Strogatz, Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos (Westview, 2nd ed., 2015) — Chapters 2–4 for bifurcations. The most readable textbook in all of applied math.
  4. For noise in nonlinear systems: Horsthemke & Lefever, Noise-Induced Transitions (Springer, 1984) — the classic reference, still unmatched.
  5. For the Scholarpedia article: Longtin (2010), Stochastic dynamical systems — a concise 15-page summary that covers the essential taxonomy.
  6. For reinforcement learning connections: Sutton & Barto, Reinforcement Learning (MIT Press, 2nd ed., 2018) — the connection between MDPs, Bellman equations, and Fokker-Planck is implicit but deep.

Stochastic Dynamical Systems — the mathematical language of uncertainty, evolution, and emergence in the SAWEM ABM-MDP-OPF framework.
