⚡🔗 TDP 2025–2034

Transmission Development Plan — The Blueprint for South Africa's Future Grid

The Transmission Development Plan (TDP) 2025–2034 is the single most important infrastructure planning document most South Africans have never heard of. Published by the National Transmission Company of South Africa (NTCSA) — the newly independent transmission entity unbundled from Eskom — the TDP maps out every high-voltage transmission line, every substation, every transformer, and every grid reinforcement project the country needs over the next decade. Without the TDP, the Integrated Resource Plan (IRP) is just a wish list — because generation capacity is worthless if you can't move the power from where it's produced to where it's consumed. This page unpacks the plan: the existing network, the corridors being built, the billions of rand it will cost, the constraints that threaten it, and why getting this right is the difference between ending load shedding permanently or locking it in forever.

Part 1: The Network South Africa Has — 33,000 km of High-Voltage Backbone

South Africa's transmission grid is one of the largest and most interconnected on the African continent. It consists of approximately 33,000 kilometres of high-voltage transmission lines, operating at voltages from 132 kV up to 765 kV, connecting Eskom's power stations in Mpumalanga to load centres across all nine provinces. The network was built primarily between the 1960s and 1980s, when Eskom was constructing its coal-fired power stations and needed to move electricity from the Mpumalanga coal fields to the rest of the country.

The Geographic Reality

The existing grid was designed around a simple premise: generation is in the northeast, load is everywhere. Eskom's coal-fired power stations are concentrated in Mpumalanga (with a few outliers — Koeberg nuclear in the Western Cape, peaking plants elsewhere). The transmission network radiates outward from Mpumalanga like spokes from a hub, carrying power to Gauteng (the largest load centre), KwaZulu-Natal, the Western Cape, and the Eastern Cape. This design made sense when all generation was coal-fired and located in one province. It makes far less sense when the future generation mix will be dominated by solar and wind — resources that are strongest in the Northern Cape, Eastern Cape, and Western Cape — areas far from the existing grid's strongest nodes.

Why this matters: The existing network was built to move power FROM Mpumalanga TO the rest of the country. The future requires moving power FROM the Cape provinces (solar, wind) and FROM distributed sources across the country TO the load centres — including back toward Mpumalanga as coal plants retire. This is not a capacity problem; it is a direction-of-flow problem. The grid is strong where the coal plants are and weak where the renewable resources are. Fixing this is the central challenge of the TDP.

Part 2: The Grid Capacity Crunch — Why South Africa Needs to Build 14,000 km of New Lines

The TDP identifies a staggering requirement: approximately 14,000 kilometres of new high-voltage transmission lines must be built over the next decade — roughly a 40% expansion of the existing 33,000 km network. This is not a nice-to-have; it is the minimum required to connect new generation capacity and prevent the grid from becoming the binding constraint on ending load shedding. Here is why the need is so acute:

The Renewable Energy Zones Are Far From the Grid

South Africa's best solar resources are in the Northern Cape (Upington, Postmasburg) — an area with some of the highest solar irradiance on Earth. The best wind resources are in the Eastern Cape and Western Cape. But the existing transmission network in these areas is thin — designed to serve small towns and farms, not to evacuate gigawatts of renewable power. To connect a single 2 GW solar cluster in the Northern Cape to Gauteng's load requires a new 400 kV or 765 kV line stretching 800+ km — and there are multiple such clusters planned across the three Cape provinces. Each one needs its own corridor.

Coal Plant Retirements Will Shift the Power Flows

As Eskom's ageing coal fleet is decommissioned (most stations are scheduled for retirement between 2030 and 2050), the generation centre of gravity moves away from Mpumalanga. The grid that was built to move power FROM Mpumalanga must be reconfigured to move power TO Mpumalanga — or at least to serve a province that will lose most of its generation. This requires new lines, upgraded substations, and new reactive power support (capacitors, STATCOMs) to maintain voltage stability when the large spinning generators at coal stations are taken offline.

Grid Connection Applications Are Overwhelming

NTCSA has received connection applications from IPPs totalling tens of gigawatts — far more than the current grid can accommodate. The "grid capacity crunch" that made headlines in 2022–2023 (when Eskom declared that certain areas had zero remaining grid capacity for new generation) was not caused by a lack of generation — it was caused by a lack of transmission. Generators wanted to connect, but the wires couldn't carry the power. The TDP is the plan to fix this — but building transmission takes 7–10 years from planning to energisation, so the crunch will persist until the new corridors are built.

Part 4: The Corridors Being Built — Where the New Lines Will Go

The TDP identifies multiple priority transmission corridors. These are the routes where new high-voltage lines will be strung. Each corridor involves not just the line itself, but new substations at both ends, intermediate switching stations, transformer capacity, and reactive power compensation (capacitors, reactors, STATCOMs) to maintain voltage stability over long distances.

Cape Corridor (Northern Cape → Western Cape → Eastern Cape)

The single most important new corridor. Will connect the solar-rich Northern Cape and wind-rich Eastern Cape to the national grid at 400 kV and potentially 765 kV. Multiple new substations are planned along the route. This corridor unlocks the largest concentration of renewable energy potential in the country — without it, the IRP's renewable targets cannot be met.

Status: Planning and environmental approvals underway. First sections expected 2028–2030.

West Coast Corridor (Northern Cape → Western Cape)

Connects the Upington/Postmasburg solar hub to the Western Cape grid. The Western Cape currently imports most of its power via a single 400 kV corridor from the north — a critical vulnerability that the West Coast corridor will relieve. Also supports the connection of offshore wind (future) and existing onshore wind farms.

Status: Advanced planning. Target 2027–2029.

Mpumalanga Reinforcement Corridor

As coal plants retire, the Mpumalanga grid must be reinforced to maintain voltage stability. Large spinning generators at coal stations provide inertia and reactive power that keep the grid stable; when they retire, that stability must be replaced by new equipment (synchronous condensers, STATCOMs, battery storage with grid-forming inverters). This corridor upgrades existing lines and substations rather than building entirely new routes.

Status: Ongoing. Linked to coal decommissioning schedule.

Gauteng Ring Reinforcement

Gauteng is the single largest load centre — consuming roughly 25% of national electricity. The existing 400 kV ring around the province is ageing and congested. The TDP plans to reinforce this ring with new 400 kV lines and substations, ensuring that power can flow into Gauteng from multiple directions (north from Limpopo, east from Mpumalanga, south from the Free State, and eventually west from the Northern Cape corridor).

Status: Partial. Some sections under construction.

Part 5: The Money — R200+ Billion and Where It Comes From

Building 14,000 km of high-voltage transmission lines and the associated substations, transformers, and reactive power equipment is extraordinarily expensive. The TDP estimates a total investment requirement of R200+ billion over the 10-year planning period — roughly R20 billion per year, every year, for a decade. To put this in perspective: Eskom's total capital expenditure on transmission averaged less than R5 billion per year in the 2010s. The TDP requires a 4–5× increase in annual transmission investment — sustained for a decade.

Where the Money Comes From

Historically, Eskom funded transmission from its own balance sheet (debt and retained earnings). But Eskom's balance sheet is wrecked — R400+ billion in debt, dependent on government bailouts. The unbundling of NTCSA as an independent entity creates the possibility of new funding sources:

  • NTCSA borrowing: As an independent entity with its own regulated revenue stream (transmission tariffs set by NERSA), NTCSA can borrow on its own balance sheet — potentially at better rates than Eskom, since transmission is a lower-risk business than generation.
  • Development finance: The World Bank, African Development Bank, and other DFIs have expressed interest in funding South Africa's transmission expansion as part of the Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP) — the R150+ billion international climate finance package.
  • Private sector / IPP participation: There is growing discussion of allowing private transmission developers to build lines under Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) or Independent Transmission Project (ITP) models — similar to how IPPs built generation. This would bring private capital and construction expertise into a sector that has been a state monopoly since 1923.
  • Tariff increases: Ultimately, transmission investment is recovered through NERSA-approved tariffs charged to electricity consumers. Every rand spent on transmission shows up in electricity prices. The TDP implies upward pressure on tariffs — the unavoidable cost of building the infrastructure to end load shedding.
The funding gap is real: The R20 billion/year required for transmission is roughly 4–5× what has been spent annually in recent years. NTCSA does not yet have the balance sheet, the institutional capacity, or the regulatory certainty to raise and deploy capital at this scale. Bridging this gap — finding the money, building the procurement systems, and scaling up the construction industry — is arguably the single hardest challenge in the entire TDP.

Part 6: The Constraints — What Could Stop the TDP From Happening

The TDP is a plan, not a guarantee. Multiple constraints threaten its delivery — and if they are not addressed, the grid capacity crunch will persist regardless of what the TDP says on paper.

1. Servitudes & Land Access

A 400 kV transmission line needs a servitude (right-of-way corridor) typically 40–80 metres wide, stretching for hundreds of kilometres. Acquiring these servitudes involves negotiating with thousands of individual landowners, tribal authorities, and municipalities — any one of whom can delay the process by years through legal challenges. The Northern Cape alone requires new servitudes across vast tracts of sparsely populated but legally complex land. Environmental impact assessments (EIAs) add 2–4 years before construction can even begin. The TDP's timelines assume servitude acquisition proceeds smoothly; historical experience suggests it will not.

2. Supply Chain & Manufacturing Capacity

14,000 km of transmission line requires enormous quantities of specialised materials: aluminium conductor steel-reinforced (ACSR) cable, steel lattice towers, insulators, transformer cores, circuit breakers, and protection relays. The global supply chain for high-voltage equipment is constrained — the world is electrifying, and every country wants transformers and switchgear. Lead times for large power transformers (400 kV and 765 kV) can exceed 3 years from order to delivery. South Africa does not manufacture 765 kV transformers domestically; they must be imported, primarily from China, South Korea, and Europe. A global supply crunch could delay the TDP by years regardless of funding availability.

3. Construction Industry Capacity

Building 14,000 km of transmission line in 10 years means constructing roughly 1,400 km per year — every year — for a decade. South Africa's current transmission construction capacity is approximately 400–600 km per year. The TDP requires more than doubling the construction industry's throughput, which means more contractors, more skilled workers (linesmen, riggers, substation technicians), more specialised equipment (tension stringing machines, helicopter lifting for tower erection in inaccessible terrain), and more project management capacity. These cannot be created overnight. The skills shortage is particularly acute: South Africa lost many experienced transmission engineers and technicians during the 2010s when capital expenditure was low and the industry contracted.

4. Regulatory & Tariff Uncertainty

NTCSA's revenue comes from NERSA-approved transmission tariffs. NERSA has historically been slow to approve tariff increases, and the regulatory methodology for transmission is still being developed post-unbundling. NTCSA cannot borrow R200 billion unless lenders have confidence that it will have a predictable, adequate revenue stream to service the debt. If NERSA suppresses tariffs — as it has done with Eskom's generation tariffs for years — NTCSA will be unable to raise the capital needed to execute the TDP. The independence and credibility of the transmission tariff-setting process is therefore directly linked to whether the TDP gets built.

5. The Chicken-and-Egg Problem with IPPs

Private generators (IPPs) will not build power plants unless they have a grid connection. NTCSA will not build transmission lines unless it has committed generation to connect. This is a classic infrastructure coordination problem: both sides need the other to commit first. The solution is for NTCSA to build transmission ahead of demand — "anticipatory investment" — which requires confidence that the generation will materialise. But if the IRP procurement is delayed (as it often has been), NTCSA risks building lines to nowhere. If procurement accelerates, NTCSA risks not having lines ready in time. Getting this balance right — building just enough transmission, just ahead of generation — is the central planning challenge of the TDP.

Part 7: Peak Demand — What the Grid Must Handle

The TDP models multiple demand scenarios, from low growth (stagnant economy) to high growth (rapid electrification and industrial expansion). Peak demand on the South African grid currently sits at approximately 34–37 GW (winter peak). The TDP projects this rising to 40–50 GW by 2034 depending on the scenario — driven by population growth, electrification of transport and heating, and new energy-intensive industries (data centres, hydrogen electrolysis, mineral processing).

⚠️ Winter peak vs. summer: South Africa's electricity demand is highly seasonal — winter peaks can be 5–7 GW higher than summer valleys, driven by electric heating (especially in Gauteng and the Free State, where winter temperatures drop below freezing). The transmission network must be sized for the winter peak, meaning significant capacity sits underutilised in summer. As the generation mix shifts to solar (which produces more in summer), the seasonal mismatch between generation and load becomes a critical planning variable — and one that transmission must bridge.

Part 8: Regional Integration — South Africa as the SAPP Anchor

South Africa's transmission grid is not an island. It is the anchor of the Southern African Power Pool (SAPP), which connects 12 countries from South Africa to the DRC. The TDP includes interconnections with neighbouring countries — existing links to Mozambique (Cahora Bassa HVDC), Zimbabwe, Botswana, Namibia, Lesotho, and eSwatini, plus planned new links to strengthen regional trade.

Regional integration matters for the TDP because:

  • Imports: South Africa imports hydropower from Cahora Bassa (Mozambique) via a 1,400 km HVDC line — one of the first HVDC systems in the world (commissioned 1978). Additional imports are possible from the Grand Inga project (DRC) if it is ever built, from gas-fired generation in Mozambique's Rovuma Basin, and from Zambia/Zimbabwe's hydro resources.
  • Exports: South Africa exports power to its neighbours — Botswana, Namibia, Zimbabwe, Lesotho, eSwatini — and transmission constraints on these interconnectors limit trade volumes.
  • Grid stability: The interconnections provide frequency support and emergency reserves. During South Africa's load shedding crisis, imports from Cahora Bassa have provided crucial marginal supply.

The TDP must plan for these interconnections — ensuring that new domestic corridors also strengthen regional links, and that South Africa's grid can both export surplus renewable power (when the wind blows and the sun shines) and import firm power (hydro, gas) when domestic resources are insufficient.

Part 10: The Bottom Line — Why the TDP Is Make-or-Break

The Transmission Development Plan is the most important infrastructure document in South Africa that almost no one reads.

The IRP tells you what generation will be built. The TDP tells you whether it can actually be connected. Without the TDP, the IRP is a fantasy — gigawatts of solar and wind capacity sitting in the Northern Cape with no wires to carry the power to Gauteng.

The TDP's success or failure will determine:

  • Whether load shedding ends permanently: You can build all the generation you want; if the grid can't move the power, the lights stay off. The TDP is the binding constraint on ending load shedding.
  • Whether the Just Energy Transition happens: The R150+ billion in international climate finance pledged to South Africa depends on the country demonstrating it can build the transmission infrastructure to integrate renewables. No grid, no transition — and no climate money.
  • Whether South Africa's economy grows: Electricity is an input to everything. If the grid is constrained, economic growth is constrained. The World Bank estimates that South Africa's GDP growth is 1–2% lower than it would be if the electricity supply were reliable. Fixing the grid is not an engineering project — it is a precondition for economic recovery.
  • Whether NTCSA succeeds as an independent entity: The unbundling of Eskom's transmission arm into NTCSA is one of the most important institutional reforms in the energy sector. If NTCSA can execute the TDP — raising capital, building lines, connecting IPPs — it validates the unbundling model. If it cannot, the political pressure to re-integrate Eskom will be overwhelming.

The TDP is not just a technical plan for wires and substations. It is the physical manifestation of South Africa's energy future. The next decade will determine whether that future is one of abundant, clean, reliable electricity — or one where the grid itself becomes the bottleneck that keeps the country in darkness.
