Ferrochrome Industry

What ferrochrome is, how it is made, and why it is the product that matters
Overview Mining Transport Smelting Ferrochrome Industry End Markets

Chromite ore is a rock. Ferrochrome (FeCr) is the industrial product. Almost nobody who makes stainless steel buys raw chromite and reduces it themselves. They buy an iron–chromium alloy, dump it in a melt, and the chromium does the corrosion-resistance. That alloy is ferrochrome. South Africa used to be the factory. It is now, mostly, the quarry. The factory story is on Smelting; this page is the commodity itself.

What it is

Ferrochrome is an alloy of iron and chromium, typically 50–70% chromium by mass, with carbon as the other controlled variable. It is not a metal you machine. It is a feedstock: lumpy, silvery, sold by the tonne, priced in US cents per pound of contained chromium.

Grade Typical chemistry What it is for
Charge chrome ~50–55% Cr, high carbon (~6–8% C). Made from Bushveld ore, which is relatively low-grade and iron-rich. The South African speciality. Stainless mills use it as the cheap bulk chromium addition. This is what Lion, Wonderkop, Ferrometals and Tubatse were built to make.
High-carbon ferrochrome (HCFeCr) ~60–70% Cr, high carbon. Often from higher-grade ores (Kazakhstan, Turkey, Albania). Same stainless use, higher Cr unit. SA is less competitive here because Bushveld chromite is charge-chrome ore.
Medium- / low-carbon ferrochrome Carbon refined down (often by further oxygen or silicothermic treatment). Specialty stainless, welding, some superalloys. Small volumes, higher price. Not the Bushveld volume game.

Rule of thumb: ~2 tonnes of chromite + coke + flux + 3,500–4,000 kWh → 1 tonne of ferrochrome, plus slag. Chromium recovery in a well-run submerged-arc furnace is high; the waste is slag (sometimes sold as aggregate) and off-gas.

How it is made

South African ferrochrome is almost all made in submerged-arc furnaces (SAFs). Chromite concentrate (typically >40% Cr₂O₃ after spirals and magnetic separation), reductant (coke, sometimes anthracite or char) and fluxes (quartz, limestone, dolomite) are fed into a furnace whose electrodes sit in the burden. Electric current provides the heat; carbon reduces Cr₂O₃ and iron oxides to metal. Liquid alloy is tapped, cast into chills or granulated, crushed and shipped.

Newer plants (Lion) are closed furnaces with better energy use and environmental controls. Older western-limb plants (Wonderkop, Boshoek) are hungrier per tonne — hence the 40c vs 62c/kWh break-even split. That difference is why Lion is the last furnace you idle and the first you restart.

Why the industry exists in South Africa

Three things used to coincide: the world's best chromite, the world's cheapest coal power, and a decision to smelt at home rather than ship rocks. Samancor in the 1970s–90s and Xstrata/Glencore in the 2000s turned that into a global franchise. Charge chrome from the Bushveld became the default chromium unit in Asian stainless mills.

When power stopped being cheap, the chemistry did not change — only the geography of the furnace. The same 2:1 ore-to-alloy ratio now happens in China, on South African ore. World ferrochrome output is on the order of 16 million tonnes (15.9 Mt in 2025 per Merafe). South Africa's slice is about 1.6 Mt. That is an industry that still exists, not one that still leads.

Who produces, who trades

  • Producers in SA: Glencore–Merafe and Samancor Chrome are the industry. Everyone else is a rounding error or a closed nameplate (Hernic, ASA Metals, Mogale).
  • Producers elsewhere: China (now the volume leader, using imported SA ore), Kazakhstan (Kazchrome / ENRC — high-grade HCFeCr), India, Finland (Outokumpu, more integrated stainless), Zimbabwe (small, Great Dyke), Albania/Turkey (ore and some alloy).
  • Buyers: stainless steel mills — Tsingshan, TISCO, POSCO, Outokumpu, Acerinox, NAS, Aperam, Jindal. They buy FeCr, not lumpy chromite, unless they own a furnace (Tsingshan in Indonesia/China increasingly does both).

Prices are quoted in ¢/lb Cr. The dashboard's ferrochrome series lives in ferrochrome_prices.csv. The commercial joke of 2025–26 is that SA miners made more money on the ore price than the alloy price, which is the opposite of why the furnaces were built.

What "the ferrochrome industry" means now

It means a thin strip of remaining SAFs, a Nersa tariff that might light some of them again, a Chinese furnace park that has already taken the growth, and a policy argument about export taxes that would hurt ore miners to feed smelters that cannot absorb 24 Mt of ore anyway. It still matters: ferrochrome is how chrome enters steel, and steel is how chrome enters the built world. South Africa's choice is whether to sell that entry ticket or just the rock it is printed on.
