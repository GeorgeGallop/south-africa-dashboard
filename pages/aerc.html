📚 African Economic Research Consortium

Nairobi-based capacity-building network founded in 1988. The job is to put African economists in the room where African economic policy is made — through research grants, collaborative master’s and PhD programmes, and policy outreach. Slogan: Bringing rigour and evidence to economic policy making in Africa.

What it is

AERC is a public non-profit devoted to independent, rigorous inquiry into the management of African economies. Headquarters sit in Nairobi (Middle East Bank Towers, Jakaya Kikwete Road). Legal form is a Delaware corporation with US 501(c)(3) status, established August 1988. Operations are funded by a consortium: bilateral and multilateral agencies, African trade and development banks, African central banks, and private foundations. The donors are the Consortium — that is the name.

The mission statement rests on two premises: development is more likely where the economy is soundly managed; sound management is more likely where a locally based cadre of professional economists does policy-relevant research. The organisation then builds that cadre through three programmes that are supposed to feed each other: research (thematic grants and collaborative projects), postgraduate training (CMAP, CPP, CMAAE), and policy outreach (biannual plenaries, Senior Policy Seminars, the Central Bank Governors’ Forum).

Vision, in AERC’s own words: sustained development in sub-Saharan Africa grounded in sound economic management and an informed society.

It is a network, not a campus. Researchers, universities and policymakers across the continent are the product. Alumni sit in central banks, finance ministries, economics departments and policy institutes. The 2024 evaluation treated graduate training as the part that clearly worked.

How it was born

Late 1980s. Structural adjustment was being pushed on African governments. University funding was falling. Economists were leaving. Research that existed was often written for other economies, or written and then ignored. A small group of African scholars and Africanists decided the gap between research and policy was the problem to fix.

The immediate precursor was the 1984 Macroeconomic Analysis Network Project for Eastern and Southern Africa — East African university economists working with the University of Toronto’s economics department, funded by Canada’s International Development Research Centre (IDRC), on structural adjustment. That network taught the method AERC still uses: pool expertise, put experienced economists in as resource persons, let the region set the research questions.

One donor backed the idea, then a small group. That group of agencies became the Consortium. AERC was formally established in Nairobi in 1988. A research programme in 1989. Biannual research-workshop plenaries from 1990. A postgraduate training programme from 1992. Publishing and communications followed. West African researchers, especially Nigerians, pulled the network out of its East African starting set.

William Lyakurwa, later a long-serving Executive Director, wrote that the AERC model was never a preconceived design. It evolved from operational experience. That is still the honest description.

The three pillars

1 · RESEARCH

Thematic + collaborative

Thematic grants for early-career African researchers, organised in five groups, with biannual workshops and resource persons. Collaborative projects put senior African researchers with counterparts off the continent on bigger questions (growth, trade, aid, climate, SDRs).

2 · TRAINING

CMAP · CPP · CMAAE

A shared curriculum across dozens of African public universities. Joint Facility for Electives / Shared Facility for Specialisation. Scholarships. The bridging programme for fragile-state candidates. This is the 4,000+ graduate pipeline.

3 · OUTREACH

Seminars, governors, fellows

Senior Policy Seminars from 1996. Biannual plenaries. The 12-member Central Bank Governors’ Forum from 2014. AERC Fellows (from 2024). Visiting scholars into targeted central banks.

The people

AERC’s most durable product is a cadre. These are the names that built it, ran it, or came out of it into the jobs the mission describes.

The most valuable things to come out of it

1. A continent-wide economics PhD and master’s factory

When AERC started, few SSA universities offered a serious master’s, let alone a PhD, in economics. The 2024 evaluation: more than 4,000 graduate trainees over 35 years, about 283 a year. Universities in SSA went from 178 in 1988 to more than 830. Graduate enrolment from 2.9% to 9.4% (2021). AERC did not cause all of that. It is the specialised economics layer inside it — shared core courses, a Joint Facility for Electives, scholarships, resource persons who actually mark the work.

Makerere’s School of Economics, a partner since 1988, went from one or two PhD faculty to a bench. That is the typical national story AERC tells, and it is the one the evaluation treats as real.

2. The Growth Project (the research that travelled)

Launched 1999. Published 2007–2008 by Cambridge University Press as The Political Economy of Economic Growth in Africa, 1960–2000 — Volume 1 the synthesis, Volume 2 twenty-six country studies covering three-quarters of the region’s population. Coordinated by Benno Ndulu and Stephen O’Connell, with Robert Bates, Paul Collier, Chukwuma Soludo, Augustin Fosu, Jan Willem Gunning, Jean-Paul Azam, Anke Hoeffler and a large African country-study roster. AERC + Harvard + Oxford.

It is still the most ambitious post-independence growth assessment done by African research economists rather than only about them. The frame: Africa’s opportunities were unusually hard (landlocked, resource-scarce, resource-rich traps); politics then failed to harness them; the transmission was “syndromes” — packages of policies, institutions and behaviour. Coastal successes and the 1990s reforms sit in the same book as the failures.

If you need one AERC research object to have read, this is it. Country chapters filled holes that had no prior empirical literature (Burundi, Chad, Togo among them).

3. Thematic research as a career ladder

Thematic research is how AERC began and is still the primary capacity-building modality for early-career researchers. Five groups, reviewed as the policy agenda moves:

  • A — Poverty, labour markets and income distribution
  • B — Macroeconomic policy and growth
  • C — Finance and resource mobilisation
  • D — Production, trade and economic integration
  • E — Agriculture, climate change and natural resource management

Gender, governance and institutions cut across. Biannual workshops are the quality gate: present, get torn apart by resource persons, revise, maybe get published in the AERC Research Paper series. Collaborative (senior) projects have covered aid dependence, Africa in world trade, East Asia comparisons, SDR/RST financing with the Gates Foundation, and climate.

4. People in the jobs

Central bank governors, finance ministers, national treasury secretaries, think-tank directors. Ndung’u, Matlanyane, Ndulu, Asiama, Kganyago’s fellowship, Kenya’s Principal Secretary Chris Kiptoo on the board. The 2014 Governors’ Forum (12 banks, including SARB, CBN, BoG, BoT, BoU, BoZ) is the African-stakeholdership bet: core funding from the continent so the research agenda is not only a donor product.

Where it is now

The 2025–2035 strategy is billed as a reset: make AERC the locus of excellence for research that matches Africa’s current problems; twin African doctoral training with top global universities; embed government and private-sector practitioners in the work. A Private Sector Platform launched December 2025. The Annual Summit is the new flagship week around the old biannual plenary.

Leadership has been a revolving door in the 2020s: Ndung’u out to Kenya’s Treasury (2022), Azomahou in as first francophone West African ED (April 2023), Murinde through the strategy launch, then out on 27 March 2026, Abbi Kedir interim from 31 March 2026. The Board Chair, Aryeetey, is the continuity — he has been in the network since the first year.

Money still comes from the old mix (Hewlett, Gates, IDRC, World Bank, Sida, Norad, FCDO, DAAD) plus African institutions (Afreximbank, TDB, Kenya Treasury, the Governors’ Forum). The 2025 Cause IQ filing: about $8.6 million revenue, $6.4 million expenses, $29 million assets. The evaluation’s caution is the same one AERC was founded to solve: research that is excellent and unused. Outreach and the Governors’ Forum are the current answer to that.

South Africa’s place in it

Pretoria and Cape Town are inside the training machine and the governors’ club. They were not in the 1984 East African seed.

  • UCT — CPP host degree-awarding university. African economics PhDs take core courses in Cape Town.
  • University of Pretoria — CMAAE accredited department and co-host of the Shared Facility for Specialisation and Electives. Matlanyane’s PhD is from here.
  • Stellenbosch — CMAAE network.
  • SARB — member of the 12-bank Governors’ Forum. Kganyago sits on the AERC Board for that forum and was inducted as an AERC Fellow in 2024.
  • Benno Ndulu — sat on Ramaphosa’s Presidential Economic Advisory Council. The founding research director of AERC was, at the end of his life, in Pretoria’s economic-policy room.

On this dashboard, AERC is the African economics-profession layer under Thesis. Power Futures Lab at UCT GSB is the power-sector research shop — a different network, same country, overlapping policy audience (SARB, National Treasury, NERSA, DMRE). Use both.
