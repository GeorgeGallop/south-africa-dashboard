Pricing Formulation

Fundamentals of unconstrained vs constrained non-linear optimization in power markets (Day-Ahead and Real-Time), what “limiting elements” are, how generator shift factors map injections to flows, and what shadow prices reveal about how congestion is created.

1) The optimization view of market clearing

Most power-market pricing rules come from solving a dispatch/clearing problem: choose generator outputs to meet demand as cheaply (or profitably) as possible, while respecting physical and operational constraints.

  • Unconstrained: only enforce power balance (and maybe generator bounds). Prices tend to reflect a single “system marginal” condition.
  • Constrained: additionally enforce network and operational limits. Prices become locational and differ across buses/locations.

2) Unconstrained non-linear optimization (intuition)

Consider a simplified dispatch with non-linear generation costs:

Unconstrained problem (conceptual)
minp   Σi Ci(pi)
s.t.   Σi pi = D
(optional)   pi,min ≤ pi ≤ pi,max

If costs are differentiable and no network limits bind, the key condition from optimality is:

C'i(pi*) = λ
where λ is the shadow price (Lagrange multiplier) of the power-balance constraint.

Interpretation: without constraints, all marginal generators “see” the same marginal value. That’s why unconstrained prices are typically uniform across the system.

3) Constrained non-linear optimization: add network + operating limits

Congestion and locational pricing appear when the optimization must respect constraints like line thermal limits (or transfer limits) and generator capacity limits. In practice, power flows are typically approximated with a linearized network model (e.g., DC power flow), even if costs are non-linear.

Constrained problem (conceptual)
minp   Σi Ci(pi)
s.t.   Σi pi = D
      fk(p) ∈ [fk,min, fk,max]
      pi,min ≤ pi ≤ pi,max

Here’s the critical idea: constrained prices are “unconstrained marginal value” plus “scarcity from active constraints.”

Limiting elements

A limiting element is any constraint that is tight at the optimum (a line hits its limit, a transfer capability is fully used, a generator hits a bound).

Active constraints

Constraints with positive shadow prices are “active” in the KKT sense: relaxing them would reduce total cost (or increase welfare).

Congestion creates locationality

When different locations cannot exchange power freely, marginal value differs by location. That difference is what pricing systems try to express.

4) Day-Ahead vs Real-Time: two-stage market clearing (high level)

In many markets, the clearing process is split into: Day-Ahead (DA) and Real-Time (RT) (or intra-day/real-time). The same optimization ideas appear in both stages, but the uncertainty/recourse differs.

Day-Ahead (DA)
  • Clears using forecast demand and forecast availability.
  • Produces schedules: commitments, dispatch setpoints, and expected flows.
  • Sets an initial price reference for energy and sometimes congestion.
Real-Time (RT)
  • Clears again using actual conditions (load, generator availability, network state).
  • Determines deviations/recourse relative to DA schedules.
  • Shadow prices in RT explain the marginal value of correcting the imbalance under constraints.
Practical takeaway: Day-Ahead congestion and Real-Time congestion can differ because the active constraint set can change when actual conditions differ from the forecast.

5) Shift factors: mapping generator injections to line flows

To enforce line limits inside an optimization, we need a model that converts generator outputs into flows. In many pricing explanations, this mapping uses shift factors.

Common concept (DC-style linear mapping)
fk = Σi SFk,i · pi
where SFk,i is the shift factor of generator i into constraint/element k (often derived from PTDF/GSF).
  • If a generator has high magnitude shift factor into a line, changing that generator’s output changes that line’s flow a lot.
  • Congestion appears when the optimization tries to move power “through” a constrained element but cannot because the element hits fk,max (or fk,min).

6) Shadow prices: what they really tell you

Shadow prices are the Lagrange multipliers on constraints in the market-clearing optimization. They quantify the marginal cost of relaxing (or the marginal value of tightening) each constraint.

Balance constraint shadow price (λ)

The multiplier on Σ pi = D is the system’s marginal energy value. In unconstrained cases, λ often dominates pricing.

Network/limit constraints (μ)

Multipliers on line limits reveal scarcity of transfer capability. When a line is binding, μ becomes non-zero and drives locational differences.

How locational prices emerge (conceptual)

Many LMP formulations can be expressed as:

price(loc n) = λ + Σk ∈ limits (SFk,n · μk)
where active μk terms encode congestion.
Sign intuition: If the optimizer is limited by the upper bound of a line flow, the corresponding μ term indicates that forcing more flow through that constraint is expensive. If the lower bound is active, the interpretation flips accordingly.

7) From shift factors + shadow prices to “congestion creation”

Congestion isn’t just “a line hits a limit.” It’s the outcome of interacting objects:

  1. Demand/production pattern sets injections across the network.
  2. Shift factors determine how those injections drive flows.
  3. The optimization chooses the cheapest feasible dispatch under limits.
  4. Shadow prices tell you which constraints were scarce enough to affect the optimum.
What the multipliers “really tell us”
A non-zero shadow price means the system would benefit (reduce cost or increase welfare) if you could relax that constraint by a small amount. That is exactly the marginal “price of congestion” at that location/time.

In other words: shift factors explain who caused the flow to stress the limit; shadow prices quantify how valuable it was to relieve that stress.

8) A quick mental checklist (DA + RT)

When you see different prices
  • Identify which constraints are active (non-zero μ).
  • Map those constraints back to the locations via shift factors.
  • Confirm whether DA vs RT changes the active set.
When you interpret shadow prices
  • Balance λ ≈ marginal value of energy.
  • Network μ ≈ marginal cost of additional transfer through limited elements.
  • Congestion rent emerges from these locational differences.
Bottom line: Unconstrained optimization gives you a single marginal energy signal. Constrained optimization adds scarcity signals from limiting elements, and shadow prices are the quantitative “scarcity meters” that turn physical limits into locational pricing.
