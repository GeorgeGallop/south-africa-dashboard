History of South African Iron Ore

Early Beginnings (1867-1928)

Iron ore was first smelted in South Africa near Rustenburg in 1867, but commercial development was slow. The country relied on imported steel for most of its heavy industrial needs. In the 1870s, major deposits were discovered at Thabazimbi in the Limpopo region, but there was no domestic steel industry to process them. The turning point came in 1928 when the South African government founded ISCOR — the Iron and Steel Corporation — under the leadership of Hendrik van der Bijl. ISCOR was a state-owned enterprise tasked with making South Africa self-sufficient in steel production.

The Sishen Discovery (1940s-1950s)

In the 1940s, ISCOR prospectors discovered a massive iron ore deposit near Sishen in the Northern Cape, close to the town of Kathu. This deposit proved to be one of the largest on Earth, with billions of tonnes of high-grade hematite ore. ISCOR developed the Sishen open-pit mine through the 1950s, and it became the cornerstone of South Africa's iron ore industry — a position it retains to this day. The mine's output initially fed ISCOR's steel mills in Pretoria and Vanderbijlpark.

The Sishen-Saldanha Railway (1970s)

The decision to export iron ore at scale required a massive infrastructure project. Between 1970 and 1976, South Africa built the Sishen-Saldanha railway line — 861 kilometres of heavy-haul track connecting the Northern Cape mines to a purpose-built deep-water port at Saldanha Bay on the west coast. The railway was one of the largest civil engineering projects in South African history and remains the country's most important bulk export corridor. The first shipment of iron ore left Saldanha in 1976.

Privatisation and Kumba (1989-2001)

ISCOR struggled with inefficiency and overstaffing through the 1980s, and the post-apartheid government moved to privatise it. In 2001, ISCOR was sold for R4.7 billion and renamed Kumba Resources. Kumba quickly became a subsidiary of Anglo American and expanded the Sishen mine while developing the Kolomela mine (formerly Sishen South) near Postmasburg, which began production in 2010. The Kolomela extension added ~9 million tonnes per year of capacity and extended the life of the Northern Cape operations.

The Commodity Boom and Crash (2008-2015)

The 2008 commodity super-cycle pushed iron ore prices from ~$30/tonne to over $200/tonne, creating a boom for South African producers. Production peaked at ~67 million tonnes in 2012. But the 2014 price crash — prices collapsed to ~$40/tonne as Chinese demand slowed and global supply surged — put intense pressure on higher-cost operations. South African mines, constrained by rail costs and deep-pit economics, struggled to remain competitive with Australian and Brazilian producers.

The Transnet Crisis (2017-2024)

For iron ore, no issue has been more consequential than the decline of Transnet's rail performance on the Sishen-Saldanha line. Locomotive shortages, theft of signalling equipment and copper cable, and maintenance backlogs have reduced the railway's capacity well below its design potential. Kumba Iron Ore has reported billions of rands in lost revenue because it could not get ore to port. In response, Kumba has explored private rail solutions (the BOOT model) where mining companies finance and operate their own locomotives on the Transnet network — a dramatic shift in South Africa's transport model.

The Sishen Mine: A National Asset

Sishen mine, located near Kathu in the Northern Cape, is a massive open-pit operation spanning ~12 kilometres in length. It produces high-grade hematite ore averaging 64-67% iron content — among the best quality in the world. The mine uses a combination of drilling, blasting, loading, and hauling in a conventional truck-and-shovel operation. Sishen's size and quality make it one of the world's premier iron ore mines, comparable to Rio Tinto's Pilbara operations in Australia.

The Future: Green Steel and Logistics

Iron ore's future is tied to two factors: Chinese steel demand (which drives global prices) and the transition to green steel (which could increase demand for high-grade ore like Sishen's). The green steel revolution — where hydrogen replaces coal as the reducing agent — requires higher-quality ore to be economical, potentially benefiting South Africa. But none of this matters if the ore cannot reach ports. Solving the Transnet rail crisis is the single most important factor determining whether South Africa's iron ore sector grows or stagnates.
