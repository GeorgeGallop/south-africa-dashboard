📖 The Gas Story

You could not write a more frustrating story if you tried. South Africa sits on gas it has barely touched — offshore basins that were always promising, a power crisis that was always urgent, and a fleet of peaking plants built to burn gas that never saw a mole of it delivered. Discoveries were made, then shelved on a moratorium and a commercial exit. FSRUs were rushed in during the load-shedding crisis, then fell through before a single cargo arrived. Now the load is falling and the grid is stable — and the decision point is finally here: build the LNG import grid at Richards Bay, Coega and Durban, wire up the domestic gas market, and remember that Mozambique is right there.

Act I
We found it here

The resource was never the problem. In February 2019, TotalEnergies drilled Brulpadda-1AX — 175 km off the southern coast in 1,800 m of water — and hit 57 m of net gas-condensate pay in the Outeniqua Basin's Block 11B/12B. In October 2020, Luiperd-1X landed 40 km east with an even better 73 m of net pay in superior reservoir. Together: ~3.4 Tcf of recoverable gas and ~200 MMbbl of condensate — enough to supply South Africa for over a decade.

It wasn't a fluke on top of nothing. The gas was already known: PetroSA's F-O and FA fields offshore Mossel Bay have fed the gas-to-liquids plant since 1992, and the Orange Basin discoveries point to a much bigger west-coast story still being appraised. The geology was there.

Act II
We didn't extract it — moratorium, then a commercial exit

The discovery should have triggered a decade of development: a subsea tie-back, a gas-to-power programme, an industrial feedstock. Instead it went nowhere. Onshore, the Karoo shale — a massive 13–390 Tcf technically recoverable resource — remains undeveloped under the hydraulic fracturing moratorium, a long-term (post-2035) option at best. Offshore, the development never happened commercially: no development plan, no take-or-pay, no pipeline, no offtake.

By July 2024 TotalEnergies exited Block 11B/12B. The reasons were commercial, not geological: $2–4 billion capex for a two-well subsea tie-back, another $500M–$1B for a 175 km subsea pipeline, the fast Agulhas Current limiting weather windows — versus Coral South FLNG in Mozambique achieving $7B for 16 Tcf. And the market wasn't there: South Africa's gas market is only ~0.16 Tcf/yr, with no gas-fired power fleet, no gas-to-power offtaker, and LNG imports available faster and cheaper. The breakeven of $8–12/MMBtu was competitive but unbankable without signed offtake.

World-class geology, stranded commercial asset. The gas is there — 3.4 Tcf proven. But getting it to market requires billions in a country with no gas infrastructure or offtake certainty. TotalEnergies didn't exit because the resource was bad; they exited because the market doesn't exist yet. Act II · the shelving
Act III
Then gas plants got built — running on diesel

Here's the paradox at the centre of the story. South Africa built open-cycle gas turbines to meet peak demand (Ankerlig, Gourikwa, Avon, Dedisa) — and then lit them with liquid fuel, because the gas never came. The fleet was gas-capable but gas-starved. The FA line fed only part of the Western Cape, and it draws from a declining field.

The Gas Master Plan's current state says it all: domestic production is <1 bcm/yr, imports from Mozambique via ROMPCO are ~4.3 bcm/yr at maximum capacity since ~2021, and gas is only ~4% of the primary energy mix — against ~70% coal, ~15% oil and ~6% renewables + nuclear. The peakers proved the country wanted gas-fired capacity fast; what it lacked was the gas. That gap is what every FSRU scheme after this was trying to close.

Act IV
Load-shedding crisis → the FSRU rush

Then the grid broke. The load-shedding crisis of 2022–23 made waiting impossible. The answer the market reached for was the FSRU — a Floating Storage and Regasification Unit that receives imported LNG, stores it in its cryogenic tanks, regasifies it and sends gas ashore. No drilling, no basin development, no long pipeline. Just a ship, a berth, and gas from the open market. FSRUs can be operational in 2–3 years versus 5–7 for an onshore terminal, with typical storage of 125,000–170,000 m³, and they're redeployable.

The Gas Master Plan treats LNG imports as the near-term anchor supply (2027+), with Ngqura (Coega) and Richards Bay identified as the active FSRU sites. Karpowership was in active discussions for FSRU-based gas-to-power at Richards Bay; Golar LNG had vessels available to redeploy; TNPA handles port readiness and DMRE / PetroSA coordinate offtake. It was the fastest possible bridge to dispatchable, low-carbon backup.

Act V
They didn't get built

The fast-track FSRU bids were the fastest fix on the table — and they fell through before a single cargo arrived. Commercial close slipped, grid access was withdrawn, and the licences were set aside. The emergency bridge never made landfall. The 2 000 MW of risk-mitigation gas the country had banked on never materialised as a fleet of floating ships.

Meanwhile the gas cliff was already on the horizon: the Pande–Temane fields feeding the ROMPCO line were depleting, with the inland market facing a supply squeeze around 2028. The one source that did feed the industrial heart was running out, just as the emergency imports failed to arrive.

The FSRU was the right idea at the wrong time. The crisis demanded instant gas; the process demanded paperwork that could not close in time. By the time the courts finished, the blackouts were already fading. Act V · the bridge that wasn't
Act VI
Now: load falls, grid stabilises → build the ports

The load-shedding crisis is over. Load is decreasing and the grid is stable — which changes everything. The moment demanded emergency 450 MW ships; this moment demands a permanent gas import strategy. Because the domestic fields were shelved, the strategy is now import-based, not extraction-based: build the ports, bring in the LNG, regasify it, and feed a domestic gas market.

The Gas Master Plan's corridors are the blueprint. Corridor A — Coega (Ngqura) LNG terminal → Mossel Bay → the existing PetroSA / Sasol network — is prioritised because it connects to existing infrastructure and can immediately feed PetroSA's GTL plant, preventing its shutdown. Corridor B — Richards Bay LNG (FSRU) → Empangeni → Vryheid → Gauteng — is the ~500 km route to the inland load. The ports at Richards Bay, Coega and Durban form the import triangle, with Vitol and others as the market players; the GIPPPP gas IPP programme is the procurement vehicle.

Act VII
And Mozambique is right there

The genius of the import strategy is that the supply is already on the doorstep. The same gas that built the Pande–Temane fields and fed them through the ROMPCO line (865 km, 36-inch, ~4.3 bcm/yr at ceiling) has been delivering to Secunda and the Sasol-Gas network (~2 900 km, ~1 600 industrial, commercial and residential customers) for two decades. That's the declining southern supply.

The real prize is to the north, in the Rovuma Basin — one of the world's great gas provinces off Mozambique's coast. Area 1 and Area 4, operated by TotalEnergies and ExxonMobil, hold the volumes that make the South African ports viable. The planned second Mozambique–South Africa pipeline (Rovuma → Richards Bay / Gauteng) is the most cost-effective incremental supply option after LNG — but it's conditional on a Rovuma FID and security in Cabo Delgado, where an insurgency has delayed development since 2021.

The supply was never the problem. It was always two days' sail away — the delivery was the missing piece. Act VII · Mozambique
Act VIII
Build the domestic market

Ports and supply are only half of it. A gas market needs demand — and the demand is the domestic industrial grid, not just power. Gas-to-power is the anchor load; the moat is industrial feedstock, process heat, ferrochrome and PGM smelters, fertiliser and the SEZs. The Gas Master Plan's Base Case projects 600–900 PJ/yr of gas demand by 2040, requiring 10–15 MTPA of LNG and 8–12 GW of gas-to-power — roughly a 5–7× increase over current consumption.

Act IX
Where this lands

The whole story reduces to a single, uncomfortable truth: South Africa never needed to extract its own gas to build the market — it needed the terminals. The discovery proved the basin. The moratorium and commercial exit killed the timeline. The crisis demanded a bridge, and the bridge failed. Now the grid is stable enough to build the real thing, and the real thing is a gas import economy — ports at Richards Bay, Coega and Durban, a Mozambican supply two days' sail away, and a domestic market that wants the molecules for power and industry alike.

💡 The synthesis, in one breath

We found it (Brulpadda / Luiperd) → we shelved it (moratorium + commercial exit) → we built gas plants that burn diesel (Ankerlig, Gourikwa, Avon, Dedisa) → we rushed FSRUs in the crisis (Karpowership, Golar, Ngqura, Richards Bay) → they didn't get built → and now that load is falling and the grid is stable we build the massive LNG import grid at Richards Bay, Coega and Durban, wire up the domestic gas market, and keep Mozambique in the frame — because it was always the supply next door. The gas was never the missing piece. The delivery was.

What to watch
Mozambique security & Rovuma FID

Area 1 / Area 4 and the second SA pipeline are conditional on FID and stability in Cabo Delgado.

LNG price

The 10–15 MTPA base case works only if delivered LNG stays below ~$10/MMBtu; at $8–12/MMBtu gas-to-power competes with new coal and diesel OCGTs but not existing coal.

The financing gap

Phase 1 alone is R50–80bn. Without anchor offtake (Eskom gas-to-power, Sasol/PetroSA) developers won't reach FID — the classic chicken-and-egg.

Market structure & TPA

Sasol controls the network, the ROMPCO contracts and is the largest user; third-party access is the single most important reform for a real market.

GMP–IRP alignment

The Gas Master Plan is a standalone strategy; if the IRP under-allocates gas-to-power, the demand projections won't materialise and the infrastructure is overbuilt.
