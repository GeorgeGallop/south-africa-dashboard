Eskom Tariffs

How electricity tariffs are structured, who sets them, and what typically drives tariff changes in South Africa.

Overview

Electricity tariffs in South Africa are usually made up of multiple components: network (distribution/transmission) charges, energy charges (kWh), and regulatory/market-related line items. While tariff frameworks are set by regulators, billing is often administered through municipalities and Eskom’s electricity supply arrangements.

In this page, “Eskom tariffs” is used as a practical catch-all for the tariff framework and typical bill components that apply when Eskom’s supply and related charges are involved.

What’s inside an electricity bill
  • Energy charge (kWh): the cost per unit of electricity consumed.
  • Network charges: transmission and/or distribution costs (influenced by allowed revenue and investment needs).
  • Demand / capacity charges (where applicable): may apply based on maximum demand or supply constraints.
  • Taxes and levies: VAT is typically applied on the billed amount; other statutory charges may apply depending on the context.
  • Time-of-use structure (where applicable): some customers are billed differently across peak and off-peak periods.
Who sets tariffs?

Tariff methodologies and approvals are typically regulated through South Africa’s electricity regulatory framework. In practice, the key regulator is the National Energy Regulator of South Africa (NERSA), which reviews tariff applications, cost pass-throughs, and allowable revenue requirements.

Tariff outcomes depend on inflation expectations, capital expenditure needs, operational cost levels, and policy-driven goals (including energy security and system recovery).

What drives tariff increases?
  • Recovering system reliability: maintenance, spares, and fleet recovery costs.
  • Capital investment: new generation, grid reinforcement, and network resilience.
  • Fuel and operational costs: volatility in key cost inputs and efficiency performance.
  • Exchange rates and inflation: costs linked to imported equipment/services can move with FX.
  • Debt and financing constraints: pressure on cost recovery and affordability.
  • Policy and social objectives: balancing cost-reflective tariffs with affordability and support mechanisms.
Customer categories (typical)
Residential
Often includes lifeline/affordability considerations and may use a time-of-use or standard structure depending on the municipality.
Commercial & industrial
More likely to reflect demand/capacity patterns and time-of-use tariffs where applicable.
