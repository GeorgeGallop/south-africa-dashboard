State-owned

Transnet is South Africa's largest freight logistics company — a fully state-owned enterprise that dominates the movement of bulk commodities via rail, operates the country's eight major commercial ports, and manages petroleum and gas pipelines. It is foundational to the mining-dependent economy, but chronic underperformance has become a major drag on GDP growth. Now, a historic reform programme is breaking up its monopoly — forcing vertical separation, introducing open access for private operators, and creating an independent regulator — the biggest shake-up in South African freight logistics since the 1988 deregulation.

Owner: Government of South Africa (100%)

Operating Divisions

🚂 Transnet Freight Rail (TFR)

The largest division — over 20,000 km of track. Handles bulk exports: coal to Richards Bay, iron ore on the Sishen-Saldanha line, manganese, chrome, and general freight. Once dominated land freight; now struggles with declining market share as road haulage took over after deregulation.

⚓ Transnet National Ports Authority (TNPA)

The "landlord" of South Africa's 8 major ports. Owns and manages harbour infrastructure — channels, breakwaters, berths, navigational aids. Does not handle cargo directly; provides the infrastructure platform for terminal operators.

📦 Transnet Port Terminals (TPT)

Operates cargo-handling terminals inside ports — containers, bulk commodities, automotive, break-bulk. Major bottlenecks in container handling at Durban have caused shipping delays and demurrage costs for exporters. Terminal concessions to private operators are underway.

🛢️ Transnet Pipelines

~3,000 km network transporting crude oil, refined petroleum products, and gas from Durban to Gauteng and beyond. Critical for national energy supply security — without it, South Africa's inland economy would depend entirely on road tankers for fuel.

🔧 Transnet Engineering

Manufacturing, maintenance, and refurbishment of locomotives, wagons, coaches, and port equipment. Historically built much of South Africa's rolling stock domestically; now competing with international suppliers under open procurement.

🏗️ Transnet Property

Manages Transnet's vast real estate portfolio — land holdings, station precincts, commercial properties, and development sites across the country, many in prime urban locations.

Historical Timeline

1850s–1880s: Mining Boom Origins

First railway line: Point to Durban (1860). Diamond discovery in Kimberley (1867) and gold on the Witwatersrand (1886) drove explosive rail expansion. British colonial and mining-house capital built a network connecting the interior mines to coastal ports — the foundation of South Africa's mineral export economy.

1910–1980s: Union & State Control

Formation of the Union of South Africa (1910) brought railways under central state control as South African Railways and Harbours (SAR&H). Post-WWII and apartheid-era expansion supported mining and industrial growth. By the 1980s, rail dominated freight with ~89% market share — a protected state monopoly.

1981–1990: Commercialisation & Transnet's Birth

SAR&H restructured into South African Transport Services (SATS). In 1990, as apartheid ended, Transnet Limited was incorporated as a state-owned company. Commuter services were separated (eventually becoming PRASA). Transnet focused on freight, ports, and pipelines.

1988–1990s: Deregulation & Road Shift

The Transport Deregulation Act (1988) ended rail's protected monopoly and liberalised road freight. Trucking companies flooded the market. General freight shifted rapidly from rail to road due to flexibility and speed — even though rail was more efficient for long-haul bulk. Transnet lost volume and pricing power.

2000s: Expansion & Early Strain

Transnet invested in coal and iron ore export lines to meet Chinese commodity demand. But underinvestment in general freight infrastructure accumulated. Maintenance began slipping.

2010s: State Capture — The Locomotive Scandal

At the height of the Zuma-Gupta era, Transnet was systematically looted. The centrepiece: a ~R50+ billion locomotive procurement programme. Contracts awarded to CRRC and CSR (Chinese state firms) at massively inflated prices — an estimated R16 billion escalation on 1,064 locomotives. Kickbacks flowed to Gupta-linked entities and politically connected intermediaries. The Zondo Commission later documented how the Transnet board was captured, procurement rigged, and competent engineers sidelined. Combined with the Eskom looting, Transnet's state capture cost the economy hundreds of billions of rand.

2020s: Collapse, Theft & The Reform Pivot

COVID-19 exacerbated all pre-existing problems. Widespread cable theft and vandalism rendered lines inoperable. Coal exports via Richards Bay collapsed from ~76 million tonnes to under 50 million. Iron ore and manganese exports were similarly throttled. Debt ballooned past R130 billion, requiring repeated government guarantees (R51 billion in 2025 alone). In response, government launched the Freight Logistics Roadmap, the National Rail Policy (2022), and the Economic Regulation of Transport Act (2024) — a historic pivot towards vertical separation, open access, and independent regulation.

The Monopoly Breaks Up: Vertical Separation & Open Access

The single most consequential change underway at Transnet is the dismantling of its vertically integrated monopoly. Instead of one state-owned entity controlling rails, ports, terminals, and pipelines as a closed system, South Africa is transitioning to an open-access, multi-operator market structure — the biggest reform since 1988, but this time with managed competition under an independent regulator.

How the old monopoly worked

Transnet owned the rail infrastructure (tracks, signalling, stations), operated the trains, ran the terminals, and managed the ports — all under one roof. A mining company wanting to move manganese had no choice but to contract Transnet Freight Rail. There was zero competitive pressure: no alternative, no incentive for efficiency, no consequence for failure. When Transnet's operational performance collapsed in the 2010s, exporters were trapped — billions of rand in mineral export revenue was stranded because there was no Plan B.

The new model: three pillars of reform
  1. Vertical separation — Transnet's infrastructure (tracks, signalling, ports) will be functionally separated from train operations. An independent Infrastructure Manager will allocate train slots, manage the network, and set access tariffs on a non-discriminatory basis. Modelled on the UK's Network Rail / open-access operator structure, adapted for a developing-country bulk-export context.
  2. Third-party access by right — any licensed operator can apply for train paths on Transnet's network. The Infrastructure Manager must grant or deny access on transparent, published terms. Over 100 applications have already been received. The first private trains are expected on the Durban–Gauteng corridor by late 2025 / early 2026.
  3. Independent economic regulation — the new Transport Regulator (established under the 2024 Act) sets access tariffs, monitors competition, resolves disputes, and enforces compliance. This removes tariff-setting from political control inside Transnet and puts it in the hands of an independent, expert body.

Who is entering the market
Traxtion

The most advanced entrant. Backed by African Infrastructure Investment Managers (AIIM) and the Development Bank of Southern Africa. Has imported 40 locomotives, secured access for general freight on the Durban–Gauteng corridor, and is building a maintenance depot in Pretoria. Plans to run container trains between City Deep (Johannesburg) and Durban Container Terminal.

Mkango Rail

A consortium targeting manganese exports from the Northern Cape to the Port of Ngqura (Coega). Manganese is one of the highest-value export commodities critically constrained by Transnet's poor performance. Private rail is seen as the fastest fix to unlock stranded volume.

Barloworld Logistics / Value Logistics

Exploring rail slots to move automotive parts and consumer goods, shifting volume off the N3 highway where truck congestion and road safety are major concerns. Represents the "road-to-rail" shift the policy explicitly targets.

International Terminal Operators

DP World, APM Terminals, and ICTSI are engaged in port terminal concessions that give them control over container and bulk handling at Durban, Ngqura, and other ports — breaking TPT's terminal monopoly from the ports side.

Mining Majors Considering Own Rail

Anglo American, Kumba Iron Ore, and South32 have all publicly stated they are evaluating building or operating their own rail fleets on Transnet's network under open-access rights — rather than waiting indefinitely for Transnet to restore service levels. This is the ultimate validation of the open-access model: if the biggest customers would rather run their own trains than rely on the state monopoly, the old model is truly dead.

Priority corridors: where reforms bite first
  • Durban–Gauteng (container corridor): The N3 highway carries ~8,000 trucks per day between the Port of Durban and Gauteng's industrial heartland. Private rail operators aim to shift 20–30% of container volume back onto rail within 3–5 years — dramatically reducing logistics costs and road congestion.
  • Sishen–Saldanha (iron ore corridor): Currently a dedicated Transnet heavy-haul line. The Department of Transport has flagged this as a priority for concession or private operating agreement, given the strategic value of iron ore exports to the national balance of payments.
  • Hotazel–Ngqura (manganese corridor): Northern Cape manganese mines need ~35 million tonnes per year of rail capacity. Transnet currently moves less than half that. Private operators are being courted to fill the gap on existing track with their own locomotives and crews.

Risks & obstacles
  • Transnet resistance: internal constituencies within Transnet — management, unions, supply-chain incumbents — have strong incentives to slow-walk or sabotage unbundling. The locomotive procurement scandal showed how deeply the patronage system is embedded.
  • Labour opposition: unions fear job losses as private operators bring their own crews and potentially lower-cost workforces. Any restructuring that triggers strikes could shut down the entire network.
  • Infrastructure decay: private operators cannot run trains faster or heavier if the track is in poor condition. Catch-up maintenance spending is measured in tens of billions of rand, and Transnet hasn't budgeted it.
  • Cable theft & security: until the network is physically secure, private operators face the same vandalism risks that crippled Transnet. Security is a prerequisite for private investment, not an afterthought.
  • Cream-skimming: private operators will target high-volume, high-margin corridors and avoid low-density rural routes. The regulator must design access pricing so Transnet can still cross-subsidise loss-making lines, or government must budget explicit subsidies for them.

In short: the old Transnet monopoly is being broken up — not by selling the company, but by forcing it to compete. The direction is clear, but the transition will take years and face fierce resistance from those who benefited from the closed system.
