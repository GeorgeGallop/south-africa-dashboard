⏱ Timeline
1923 — Founding

ESCOM (Electricity Supply Commission) established by the South African government to provide cheap, reliable electricity for mining and industrial development. The first power stations were coal-fired, built near the Witbank (now eMalahleni) coal fields in what is now Mpumalanga.

1940s–1970s — Industrial Expansion

Massive expansion during South Africa's industrialisation. Eskom built some of the world's largest coal-fired stations — Camden, Hendrina, Grootvlei, Arnot, Duvha, Matla, Kriel, Tutuka — providing cheap power to gold and platinum mines and manufacturing. The 1970s oil crisis reinforced coal dependence.

1980s — Overcapacity & Mothballing

Demand growth slowed. Eskom built ahead of demand under a "build now, grow into it" strategy. Stations like Camden and Grootvlei were mothballed. Koeberg Nuclear (1,860 MW, Africa's only nuclear station) was commissioned 1984–1985. The transmission grid was extended to urban and industrial centres.

1994 — Post-Apartheid Electrification

The new democratic government prioritised electricity access. Eskom connected millions of new households (from 36% electrified in 1994 to >90% by 2020). But the low-consumption residential customers did not cover the marginal cost of supply. The utility's financial model began to weaken.

2000s — Maintenance Deferred, Crisis Looms

The reserve margin (available capacity above peak demand) shrank as demand grew and maintenance was deferred. In 2005, President Mbeki announced a new build programme (Medupi and Kusile). In 2008, South Africa experienced the first round of load shedding. The utility had run out of spare capacity.

2010s — State Capture & Theft

The Zondo Commission documented systematic looting of Eskom under the Zuma-Gupta administration. Coal contracts were inflated (Tegeta overcharges estimated at R20–30bn). Medupi and Kusile ballooned from a combined R160bn budget to >R450bn. Maintenance budgets were slashed. Cadre deployment replaced engineers with loyalists. Load shedding became chronic.

2022–2023 — The Peak Crisis

South Africa experienced 329 days of load shedding in 2023 alone, reaching Stage 6 (6,000 MW shed). GDP losses estimated at R500bn. The coal fleet EAF fell below 55%. Emergency diesel burn at OCGTs cost R12bn+ per year. The government announced R254bn debt relief and accelerated the unbundling process.

2024–2025 — Recovery & Reform

Load shedding stopped in March 2024 (first extended break since 2020). The NTCSA (transmission entity) was legally established. Kusile units came online, adding ~2,400 MW. Rooftop solar installations reached ~5,000 MW. But the coal fleet remains fragile (EAF still ~60%) and diesel consumption remains a structural cost. Full unbundling completion is expected by 2026–2027.

🔎 Root Causes of the Eskom Crisis
  1. State capture (2013–2018): Systematic looting through inflated coal contracts, political appointments, and procurement fraud documented by the Zondo Commission.
  2. Deferred maintenance: Budgets cut to hide financial losses; coal mills, boilers, and turbines degraded to the point where >40% of capacity was routinely unavailable.
  3. Medupi/Kusile cost overruns: Two stations consumed >R450bn (vs R160bn budget), absorbing capital needed for maintenance and diversification.
  4. Governance collapse: 5 CEOs in 6 years; board politicised; engineering competence replaced by political loyalty; no accountability for performance.
  5. Coal quality decline: Depletion of high-grade reserves and state-capture-era contracts forced Eskom to burn low-grade coal (18–22 MJ/kg vs 25–27 MJ/kg design), reducing plant output and damaging equipment.
