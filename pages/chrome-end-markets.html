Chrome End Markets

Who needs chrome, why they buy it, and where demand goes next
Overview Mining Transport Smelting Ferrochrome Industry End Markets

Chrome is not a jewellery metal and not a battery metal. It is a stainless-steel metal. Roughly four-fifths of all chromite that leaves the ground ends up as chromium in stainless. The rest is chemicals, foundry sand, refractories and a thin high-value tail of superalloys and plating. If you know who is pouring 300-series stainless, you know who needs Bushveld chrome.

Who needs it — and why

End use Share (order of magnitude) Why chrome Who buys
Stainless steel ~80–90% of chrome units At ≥~10.5–12% Cr, steel forms a passive chromium-oxide film. That is stainless: food plant, tanks, kitchens, architecture, exhausts, desalination, hospitals. Chinese mills first (Tsingshan and the rest of the stainless belt), then India, Indonesia, Korea, Japan, Europe (Outokumpu, Acerinox, Aperam), US (NAS). They buy ferrochrome, not ore — unless they own a furnace.
Alloy / engineering steels Small but steady Hardness, hardenability, high-temperature strength. Tools, bearings, some oil-country goods. Carbon-steel and specialty mills; much smaller FeCr lots than stainless.
Chemicals Low- to mid-single digits and falling in the West Sodium dichromate, pigments, leather tanning, wood preservatives. Hexavalent chromium is toxic; regulation has shrunk this book in Europe and the US. Chemical plants, mostly in China and a few remaining Western sites. Not the volume driver.
Foundry sand & refractories Small Chromite sand does not wet with molten metal; refractory chrome-magnesia bricks survive steel and cement kilns. Foundries and refractory makers. Often lumpy ore or concentrate, not FeCr.
Superalloys, plating, aviation Tiny tonnes, high value Chromium in nickel alloys (turbine blades) and hard chrome plating. Aerospace and industrial-gas-turbine supply chains. Irrelevant to Bushveld mine volumes; relevant to a few specialty refiners.

The customer that actually moves the ships

China. Stainless production concentrated there over the last twenty years. China imported about 19.6 million tonnes of South African chrome ore in 2025 (17.0 Mt in 2024). That ore is smelted in Chinese (and increasingly Indonesian, Tsingshan-linked) furnaces into ferrochrome, then into 200- and 300-series stainless. South Africa's ferrochrome exports, meanwhile, shrank from a peak near the start of 2023 toward a trickle — TIPS put FeCr export earnings at ~R5 billion in Q3 2025 versus almost R20 billion off the peak, with FeCr's share of SA exports falling from 3.2% to 1% while chrome ore's share rose from 2.1% to 5%.

So the "who" has split:

  • Who buys South African ore: Chinese (and Indonesian) ferrochrome smelters, plus traders.
  • Who buys South African ferrochrome: whoever is left among stainless mills that still want charge chrome from SA rather than Chinese alloy — a shrinking list while SA furnaces are off.

Europe, Japan and the US still need chromium units. They increasingly get them as Chinese stainless sheet or as FeCr that is no longer South African.

Why they cannot easily substitute

There is no stainless without chromium. Nickel, molybdenum and nitrogen change the grade (304 vs 316 vs 200-series); they do not replace the chromium-oxide film. Recycled stainless is a real second supply of chrome units — scrap is a large part of the melt in Europe and the US — but global stainless growth still needs primary ferrochrome, and primary ferrochrome still needs chromite. Kazakhstan, Turkey, Albania, India and Zimbabwe produce ore. None of them has the Bushveld. That is South Africa's remaining leverage: not the furnace, the rock.

The future

Demand for chromium units grows with stainless, not with SA smelters. Stainless follows construction, process plant, kitchens, water and food infrastructure. "Green" capex — desalination, hydrogen kit, some renewable structures — is stainless-intensive. EVs use less chrome in the powertrain than a catalytic converter uses PGMs, but they still sit in a stainless-and-steel world. That is a modest tailwind, not a new commodity supercycle of the lithium sort.

Where the units are made is the fight. If Chinese and Indonesian FeCr stay cheap, South Africa remains an ore province. If the 62c/kWh tariff plus private power puts 40-odd furnaces back, SA claw back some alloy tonnes — still not the 24 Mt of ore. Export taxes, if imposed, would try to force that shift and would hit PGM-chrome and Tharisa on the way through.

Risks on the demand side: Chinese stainless overcapacity and a property-led slowdown; 200-series grades that use less nickel (not less chrome); tighter hexavalent-chromium rules that kill chemical uses; a long scrap loop in mature economies. Risks on the South African side: rail and port, illegal mining (~0.6 Mt), and a furnace fleet that may never again match the ore fleet.

The durable fact: the world will keep needing chromium for stainless. The open question is whether it buys that chromium as a Bushveld rock, a Middelburg alloy, or a Chinese coil that started as both.
