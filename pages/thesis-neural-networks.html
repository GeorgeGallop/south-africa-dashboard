🧠 Neural Networks

Overview

A method for learning non-linear mappings from data — explained through short-term load forecasting, the industrial problem that every power market must solve before it can clear a day-ahead price.

⚡ The Industry Problem — Why Load Forecasting Exists
Electricity cannot be stored at grid scale (yet) the way a warehouse stores grain.

Generation must equal demand plus losses in every settlement period. If the system operator (or a Balance Responsible Party in SAWEM) guesses tomorrow's demand wrong, the market still has to balance — but it balances in the expensive, last-minute world of the balancing mechanism, not in the cheaper, planned world of the day-ahead market. Load forecasting is the demand-side input that makes economic dispatch, unit commitment, and the System Marginal Price possible.

In a wholesale power market the load forecast is not a weather app curiosity. It is an operational and financial instrument. It tells the market operator how much energy must be scheduled; it tells generators which units to commit overnight; it tells traders how much to buy or sell; and it tells the system operator how much reserve to hold. A 2% error on a 30 GW peak is 600 MW — a mid-sized power station of surprise, paid for at imbalance prices rather than at the day-ahead SMP.

Very short term
Minutes – 6 hours
Real-time dispatch, AGC, balancing. Feeds the System Operator's nowcast.
Short term (STLF)
Day-ahead (24–48 h)
The workhorse. SAWEM DAM gate closes 10:00 SAST on D−1. This is where neural nets earn their keep.
Medium term
Weeks – months
Maintenance windows, coal and LNG offtake, hydro scheduling, seasonal hedging.
Long term
Years (IRP / TDP)
Capacity expansion. IRP 2025's 255 TWh in 2030 is a load forecast with GDP, population and efficiency inside it.

Classical tools — ARIMA, exponential smoothing, linear regression on temperature and calendar dummies — still work on a quiet winter Tuesday. They fail when the mapping is non-linear and state-dependent: a 2 °C change at 18:00 in July does not move load the same way as a 2 °C change at 14:00 in January; a Gauteng weekday with a public holiday on Friday is not a weekend; rooftop PV turns gross demand into a duck-shaped net load; and years of load shedding mean the historical series is not unconstrained demand. That is the regime in which neural networks became the industry standard for short-term load forecasting.

🧠 What Is a Neural Network? — Told as a Load Forecast
💬 Layman's Introduction

Imagine you are a trader at a Balance Responsible Party. At 09:30 tomorrow morning you must tell the SAWEM Market Operator how many megawatts your customers will draw in every hour of the following day. You have a pile of clues: it will be 12 °C in Johannesburg, it is a Thursday, last Thursday at 18:00 the load was 31.2 GW, it is not a public holiday, and sunset is at 17:41.

A linear model would say: “add 400 MW per degree below 18 °C, subtract 8% on weekends, done.” Reality is messier. Heating load saturates. Air-conditioning kicks in only above a threshold. The evening peak is a shape, not a number. Mining shifts, soccer matches, and load-shedding recovery all warp the curve in ways that no single equation captures.

A neural network is a stack of simple calculators (neurons) that learn that messy mapping from examples. You show it thousands of historical days: the clues that were known the day before, and the load that actually materialised. It adjusts internal numbers (weights) until its guess of tomorrow's megawatts is as close as possible to what happened. After training, it is a function:

D̂t = fθ( hour, day-type, weather, lagged load, calendar, … )

The name is a metaphor, not a claim of biological fidelity. Each “neuron” computes a weighted sum of its inputs, then squashes the result through a non-linear activation. Stack enough of them and the network can approximate, to any accuracy you can afford, any continuous function from features to load. That is the universal approximation theorem, and it is why the same architecture that classifies handwritten digits can forecast Eskom system demand.

Three ideas do all the work:

  1. Representation. Raw clues (hour = 18, temp = 12 °C) are encoded as numbers the network can multiply.
  2. Composition. Layers of non-linear units compose simple functions into a complicated one — the daily load shape.
  3. Learning from error. The difference between predicted MW and metered MW is a loss. Gradients of that loss wiggle every weight in the direction that would have made yesterday's forecast better.
■ Anatomy of a Network — From Features to Megawatts
The neuron, as a tiny load model

A single neuron with inputs x, weights w, bias b and activation σ computes

z = w · x + b    →    a = σ(z)

Suppose we are predicting the 18:00 system load and this neuron only sees three clues, already scaled to roughly [−1, 1]:

Feature x Meaning for the 18:00 forecast Example value Weight w Contribution w·x
temp_norm How far temperature is from 18 °C (heating drives winter peak) −0.40  (12 °C) −1.20 +0.48
is_weekday Commercial and industrial load is in (1) or out (0) 1.00 +0.70 +0.70
load_lag168 Same hour last week, scaled — the strongest single predictor in STLF 0.85 +1.10 +0.94
Bias b +0.10
Pre-activation z 2.22

Pass z through ReLU, σ(z) = max(0, z), and the neuron fires at 2.22. A linear output neuron then rescales that hidden activity into megawatts. One neuron is a half-baked regression. A layer of them is where the method becomes a network: each hidden unit can specialise — one learns “winter evening heating”, another “Friday-before-a-holiday rundown”, another “hot Highveld afternoon air-conditioning”. The output layer mixes those specialised detectors into a single MW number (or a 24-hour vector).

A feedforward net for day-ahead load

The diagram below is the architecture you would actually ship for a first-cut SAWEM day-ahead forecast: calendar and weather features in, one hidden layer, predicted demand out. Deep STLF models add recurrence or attention (next section). The computation does not change in kind — only in depth and in how time is represented.

t
Forward pass: features → hidden detectors → predicted demand (MW) for one settlement period. Repeat for each hour of D+1, or emit a 24-dimensional output in one shot.
Why the non-linearity is not optional

If every activation were linear, stacking layers would collapse to a single matrix multiply — ordinary linear regression. Load is not linear in temperature: the response is a U-shape (heating below ~15 °C, cooling above ~24 °C, a dead band in between). Load is not linear in hour: the evening ramp is a steep S-curve. ReLU, tanh and GELU exist so the network can bend. That bend is the whole reason the industry moved off ARIMA.

The activation function is that bend. It is the sauce: without it there is no network, only a stacked regression. Activation Functions →

■ How They Learn — Yesterday's Error Trains Tomorrow's Forecast

Training is supervised regression. For each historical hour you have the features that would have been known the day before (never leak the actual temperature of 18:00 if your DAM bid is due at 10:00 — use the weather forecast available at gate opening), and the metered load that later arrived. The network emits D̂t; the meter reads Dt; the loss is the distance between them.

Point forecast (most common STLF loss)
LMSE = (1/N) Σt ( Dt − D̂t )2
Industry-facing metric (what the control room quotes)
MAPE = (100/N) Σt |Dt − D̂t| / Dt
Probabilistic forecast (what reserve procurement actually needs)
Lpinball(τ) = (1/N) Σt [ τ (Dt − D̂t) ]+ + [ (1−τ)(D̂t − Dt) ]+

MAPE of 1.5–3% day-ahead is a serious operational model on a large, well-behaved system. Below 1% is excellent and usually means you have a stable industrial base and good weather. South Africa is harder than that: load shedding, rooftop PV, municipal unreliability and a winter-peaking, mining-heavy mix push errors up. A BRP that can hold MAPE under ~3% on its portfolio is not leaving much money on the imbalance table.

Gradient descent, in market language

Back-propagation is the chain rule applied to the stack of matrix multiplies. The scalar loss L depends on D̂, which depends on the last layer's weights, which depend on the hidden activations, which depend on the first layer. Differentiating through that chain yields ∂L/∂θ for every weight θ. The update

θ ← θ − η ∇θ L

is the same idea as a generator agent in the thesis MDP updating its bid from last hour's profit — except here the “action” is a forecast, the “reward” is negative squared error, and the policy is a differentiable function rather than a Q-table. Stochastic gradient descent (Adam, in practice) does this on minibatches of hours so a year of hourly data is thousands of updates, not one giant matrix solve.

Train

Historical days used to fit weights. Typical split: 70% of the last 3–5 years of hourly load, weather and calendar.

Validate

Held-out months used to pick architecture and early-stop. Never tune on the test year. Seasonality means you must hold out whole seasons, not random hours.

Test / produce

Walk-forward on the most recent year, then freeze weights and run every morning before 10:00 SAST. Retrain weekly as new metered data lands.

■ Architectures the Industry Actually Uses

“Neural network” is a family, not a single model. Load is a sequence with daily, weekly and annual periods, driven by exogenous weather and calendar. Different architectures buy different inductive biases.

Architecture What it is, in load-forecasting language When a SAWEM desk would pick it
MLP (feedforward) A bag of features for hour t maps to D̂t. You hand-craft lags (t−1, t−24, t−168), Fourier terms for hour and week, and weather. Baseline. Fast to train, easy to explain to a risk committee, surprisingly strong if the feature set is good.
RNN / LSTM / GRU The hidden state carries a memory of the last many hours, so the network sees the shape so far today when it predicts the next hour. LSTM gates learn what to keep (the morning ramp) and what to forget (yesterday's lunch dip). Very-short-term and rolling intra-day updates, when the morning actuals should revise the afternoon forecast.
CNN (1-D / 2-D) Convolution over time (or over a time×feature map) detects local motifs: the evening ramp, the lunch trough, a cold-front signature. Same idea as an image net, applied to a load curve. When you have many similar series (substations, municipalities) and want shared local detectors.
Seq2seq An encoder reads history; a decoder emits the next 24 (or 48) hours in one go. Matches the DAM product: you need a vector, not a one-step-ahead scalar. Day-ahead submission. Avoids the error cascade of rolling a one-step model out 24 times.
Transformer / attention Instead of a single hidden state, every hour can attend to any other hour (last week's 18:00, this morning's 07:00, last year's similar-temperature Tuesday). Positional encodings carry clock and season. Current SOTA for STLF on large datasets. Heavy to train; worth it if you are NTCSA or a large BRP, overkill for a single mine.
Hybrid / ensemble A statistical model (SARIMAX, TBATS) plus a neural net on the residuals; or several nets blended. Gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost/LightGBM) remain fierce competitors on tabular STLF and are often in the same ensemble. Production. Almost nobody ships one model. The ensemble is what hits the MAPE target on weird days.
Quantile / density nets Output P10 / P50 / P90 (or a full distribution) instead of a single MW. Pinball or CRPS loss. The P90 minus P10 is a data-driven reserve requirement. System Operator reserve sizing; BRP credit-cover and imbalance-risk; any desk that has to buy options on forecast error.

Related, not the same: graph neural nets over the transmission network for nodal load; physics-informed nets that hard-wire power-balance; and reinforcement-learning policies that use a forecast to bid. Those last two sit closer to the Methods and Agent Architecture pages.

■ The Industrial Pipeline — Features, Horizons, Evaluation
What actually goes into the network

A production STLF feature store, South African flavour:

hour sin/cos day-of-week month / season SA public holidays school calendar sunset / photoperiod temp, lag temp, CDD/HDD humidity / wind (Cape) load t−1, t−24, t−168 rolling 7-day profile embedded PV estimate load-shedding stage (ex post) mining shift flags major events (sport, elections)

Two encoding details that separate a toy notebook from a desk that makes money:

  • Cyclical time. Hour 23 and hour 0 are neighbours. Encoding hour as an integer 0–23 tells the net they are opposites. Use sin(2πh/24) and cos(2πh/24). Same for day-of-year.
  • No leakage. At 10:00 on D−1 you do not know D's 18:00 temperature. You know a weather forecast. Train on weather forecasts, not on the ERA5 reanalysis of what the temperature turned out to be, or the model will look brilliant in backtest and average in production.
How the industry keeps score
MetricWhat it punishesWhy a power-market desk cares
MAPE Relative error, equally across hours Board-level KPI. Misleading at night (small denominator) and during deep load-shedding troughs.
RMSE / MAE (MW) Absolute megawatts Peak-hour errors cost more. A 500 MW miss at 18:00 is a unit-commitment problem; a 500 MW miss at 03:00 is not.
Peak MAPE / timing error Missing the evening peak, or being an hour early The System Operator's reliability metric. Peak timing errors cause the wrong OCGT to start.
Pinball / CRPS / coverage Miscalibrated uncertainty If your P90 is always too low, you systematically under-procure reserve and eat SBP.
Imbalance cost (R) The only loss that hits the P&L MAPE can be 2.1% and still lose money if errors are one-sided on high-SBP hours. Evaluate in rand, not only in percent.

Always beat a naive baseline before celebrating. The unbeatably honest one is same hour last week (seasonal naive). On a stable system it is already within a few percent. The neural net has to earn everything inside that gap.

■ Application to Industry — Load Forecasting in SAWEM and the RSA System

Neural networks are a general method. The reason they belong on this dashboard is that every institution in the South African power market already depends on a load forecast, whether it admits to using a neural net or still runs a regression with calendar dummies.

Who forecasts, and why the method matters
Actor Forecast they must produce What error costs
Balance Responsible Parties SAWEM Market Code Part 3, Section 3.3: demand-side participants submit load forecasts as price-sensitive bids or fixed demand schedules into the Day-Ahead Market. Gate closure is 10:00 SAST on D−1. Imbalance. Short positions pay SBP (System Buy Price — often SMP plus a 10–30% uplift); long positions receive SSP. A persistently biased net is a transfer from the BRP to the balancing stack.
Market Operator / NTCSA System Operator System-level STLF for SCUC/SCED: the unconstrained and constrained schedules that produce the SMP. Also reserve requirements (Section 3.4) which scale with forecast uncertainty. Wrong unit commitment: too little coal or OCGT left online, then emergency diesel and load shedding; or too much, then extra start-up and no-load cost socialised into the SMP.
Eskom Generation / IPP portfolios Residual (net) demand after must-run nuclear, IPP take-or-pay, and variable renewables. This is the curve coal and gas actually chase. Over-commitment of inflexible plant; or under-commitment and a scramble that shows up as OCGT burn and, historically, as load-shedding stages.
NTCSA planning (TDP) 10-year spatial demand. The TDP is worthless if the MW are in the wrong province. See TDP 2025–2034. Building 400 kV into a load that never arrives; or not building into Gauteng / coastal industrial growth and rationing by congestion.
IRP / DMRE Annual energy (TWh) and peak (GW) under GDP and efficiency assumptions. IRP 2025's ~255 TWh in 2030 is a long-term load forecast. A high forecast justifies surplus capacity (expensive). A low forecast repeats the 2008–2015 underbuild. Neural nets are not the right tool at this horizon — structural models are — but STLF accuracy is how you notice the structural model is drifting.
Municipalities & large users Own-load STLF for NERSA tariff design, wheeling, and (for mines and smelters) shift scheduling against TOU and, soon, against SMP. Buying the peak they could have shifted; under-hedging a smelter potline; municipal under-collection when demand was mis-estimated.
Why South African load is a hard neural-net problem
The meter is not the demand

For most of 2014–2023, observed Eskom sent-out was constrained demand. Training a net on load-shedding years teaches it that winter evenings are smaller than they would have been. Unless you reconstruct unconstrained demand (or condition on stage), the model under-forecasts the first healthy winter and the System Operator is short. This is concept drift with a political cause.

Net load is not gross load

Distributed generation — rooftop PV, wheeling, municipal IPPs — is chewing a hole in the midday meter. The System Operator dispatches net load. A network that does not see a PV proxy (irradiance, installed capacity, time of year) will over-forecast noon and then miss the 17:00–19:00 ramp, which is exactly when OCGTs are expensive and the grid is tight. See Distributed Generation.

Two peaks, many shapes

National peak has historically been a winter weekday evening (heating + lighting + cooking). Coastal metros add a summer afternoon air-con peak. Mining load is flatter and shift-driven; Gauteng commercial is weekday-shaped; Western Cape weather is marine and wind-correlated. A single national MLP averages these into mush. Hierarchical forecasting (node / province / sector, then reconcile to the system total) is how serious desks do it.

The calendar is local

Heritage Day, the December builder's holiday, Ramadan in Cape Town, a Saturday rugby test at Loftus, and an election Wednesday all move gigawatts. Off-the-shelf US or European holiday lists will silently fail. The feature store is a South African data product, not a GitHub default.

■ From Forecast to Price — Where the Network Touches SMP

A load forecast is not the end of the pipeline. In SAWEM it is the demand constraint of the co-optimised SCUC/SCED that prints the System Marginal Price. The chain is:

1. Features
weather, calendar, lags, PV
2. Neural net
D̂1..24 (± quantiles)
3. DAM bids
BRP demand + SO reserve
4. OPF / SCUC
λt = SMP
5. Real time
imbalance × SBP/SSP

Because the SMP is the dual on the power-balance constraint (Σ generation = Dt + losses), a biased load forecast is a biased price. Forecast too high and the day-ahead market commits extra mid-merit and peaking plant: SMP prints high, consumers overpay, generators who cleared may be constrained off in real time and collect lost-opportunity payments. Forecast too low and the DAM looks cheap, real-time is short, OCGTs and emergency energy set SBP, and BRPs who believed the forecast pay the spread.

The money equation the network is really minimising
Costimbalance ≈ Σt [ (Dt − D̂t)+ · SBPt − (D̂t − Dt)+ · SSPt ]

SBP and SSP are themselves functions of how many other BRPs missed in the same direction. Correlated forecast errors (everyone using similar weather and similar nets) are systematically more expensive than idiosyncratic ones. That is why a desk's “secret sauce” is often a proprietary feature — a mine's shift plan, a municipality's load-control schedule — not a deeper transformer.

This is also why probabilistic forecasts belong in a market, not just a MAPE bake-off. The System Operator's reserve requirement Rt should scale with the predicted error band, not with a static percentage of peak. A quantile network that widens P10–P90 ahead of a cut-off low over the Highveld is doing the same job as a well-calibrated options book.

■ Failure Modes — When the Network Is the Wrong Tool
  • Black box vs. a NERSA hearing. A linear model can say “we added 380 MW because the forecast was 4 °C colder.” A 4-million-parameter LSTM cannot, unless you add SHAP / integrated-gradients and still speak carefully. For operational STLF this is tolerated. For tariff filings and IRP assumptions it is not: those stay structural.
  • Regime change. Networks interpolate. They do not invent a new economy. A sudden cluster of data centres in Johannesburg, a smelter closure, or the end of load shedding is a break the training set did not contain. Detect drift (rolling MAPE, residual control charts) and retrain; do not wait for a spectacular Tuesday.
  • Weather-forecast error is not model error. If the 10:00 SAST temperature forecast is 4 °C off, even a perfect mapping from temperature to load will miss. Attribute errors, or you will “improve” the net when you should have bought a better weather vendor.
  • Overfitting the last crisis. A net trained hard on 2022–2023 stage-6 evenings will expect troughs that are no longer there. Regularise, use more years, and consider an explicit “constrained vs unconstrained” flag rather than hoping the hidden layer notices.
  • Long-term energy (TWh) is not STLF. You cannot roll a day-ahead LSTM out to 2035 and call it an IRP. Long-term load is GDP, population, efficiency, electrification of transport and the death or birth of energy-intensive industry. Neural nets can nowcast the deviation from that structural path. They should not replace it.

The grown-up production stack is therefore hybrid: a structural / statistical backbone, a neural net on the residual and on the short horizon, an ensemble, and a human who can override a Heritage Day the feature store forgot.

■ Where This Sits in the Thesis Stack

The SAWEM evolutionary framework already has a demand object: the Macro Economy ABM produces Dt, which the OPF takes as given. A neural load-forecasting model is how agents inside the market form that Dt when they do not have the omniscient simulator's copy.

  • BRP / load agents observe a feature state and emit D̂ as their DAM bid. Their MDP reward is minus imbalance cost, so a better network is a better policy — or the policy can be the network, trained by backprop rather than by Q-learning.
  • The System Operator agent uses a system-level STLF (and its quantiles) to set Rt and to run SCUC. Forecast error is one of the stochastic processes on the Stochastic Dynamical Systems page.
  • Generator agents do not forecast load directly, but they forecast residual demand (load minus renewables) when they decide availability and start-up. The same architectures apply, with irradiance and wind as extra inputs.
  • Closed loop. Actual Dt realises, SMP prints, imbalance settles, agents update. The neural net is retrained on the new residual. That is the same adaptation loop as the rest of the CAS/ABM stack, implemented with gradients instead of with replicator dynamics.

In one sentence: optimal power flow is how the market turns a demand number into a price; a neural network is how a participant who cannot see the future produces that demand number from the clues the world leaves lying around.
