🌍 Agenda 2063 — The Africa We Want

The African Union’s 50-year blueprint (2013–2063). The first decade closed at about 39% of its targets. The second decade, 2024–2033, is billed as the Decade of Acceleration. Continental performance on the new plan sits around 53% — visible, and not yet at the speed the moonshots need.

What it is

Agenda 2063 is Africa’s long-term development blueprint. The AU calls it the master plan for turning the continent into “the global powerhouse of the future.” The slogan is The Africa We Want. The official vision is an integrated, prosperous and peaceful Africa, driven by its own citizens, representing a dynamic force in the international arena.

It is a 50-year horizon, 2013 to 2063, because 2013 was the golden jubilee of the Organisation of African Unity. At that summit African heads of state signed the 50th Anniversary Solemn Declaration (May 2013) and asked the Commission to write a people-driven 50-year agenda. The former Commission chair, Nkosazana Dlamini-Zuma of South Africa, framed the destination in a “letter from the future.” The 24th Assembly in Addis Ababa adopted the Agenda in January 2015 as Assembly/AU/Dec.565(XXIV). The First Ten-Year Implementation Plan was endorsed at the June 2015 summit.

It is meant to shift the continental project from the OAU’s decolonisation-and-apartheid struggle onto inclusive growth, integration, democratic governance and peace. It sits on top of older frameworks rather than replacing them: the Lagos Plan of Action, the Abuja Treaty, the Minimum Integration Programme, PIDA (infrastructure), CAADP (agriculture), NEPAD (now AUDA-NEPAD), plus REC and national plans. The UN SDGs (Agenda 2030) run in parallel; AU and UNECA report on both together.

It is five ten-year plans, not one document. 2014–2023 was the decade of convergence (getting national plans to talk to the continental one). 2024–2033 is the Decade of Acceleration. Then 2034–43, 2044–53, 2054–63. Each plan has its own targets. Scores from different reports are not the same ruler.

Seven aspirations

These are the political promises. Twenty goals hang under them. The second-decade “moonshots” are the 2033 versions of the same seven.

1

Prosperity

Inclusive growth and sustainable development — living standards, skills, health, jobs, agriculture, the blue economy, climate-resilient economies.

2

Integration

A politically united continent on Pan-Africanism and African Renaissance — world-class infrastructure, continental financial institutions, the union project.

3

Governance

Democracy, human rights, justice and the rule of law — capable institutions and transformed leadership.

4

Peace

A peaceful and secure Africa — silencing the guns, a working African Peace and Security Architecture.

5

Culture

A strong cultural identity, common heritage, shared values and ethics — cultural renaissance as a development instrument.

6

People

Development driven by African people, especially women and youth, and caring for children — gender equality and an engaged generation.

7

Global player

A strong, united, resilient and influential partner — Africa financing its own development and speaking with a common voice.

The seven moonshots (2024–2033)

The Second Ten-Year Implementation Plan, adopted at the 37th Ordinary Session of the Assembly (17–18 February 2024, Addis Ababa), turns the aspirations into ten-year bets. AUDA-NEPAD’s language is that the first decade was about convergence; this one is about acceleration, with African citizens as the centre of a theory of change.

Moonshot by 2033What it asks for
1. Every member state at least middle-incomeHeadline target: GDP per capita of US$3,048 in every AU state. Industrialisation, value addition, agricultural productivity, governance. South Africa is already upper-middle income; this moonshot is about the whole membership, not Pretoria.
2. Africa more integrated and connectedInfrastructure, energy security, trade in goods and services, internet, roads, air. AfCFTA, SAATM, high-speed rail, AfSEM sit here.
3. Public institutions more responsiveThe governance moonshot — delivery, not declarations.
4. Africa resolves conflicts amicablyThe successor to “Silencing the Guns by 2020,” which the continent missed.
5. African culture and values explicit and promotedMuseum, Encyclopaedia Africana, cultural renaissance programmes.
6. Citizens more empowered and more productiveSkills, health, youth, gender, jobs.
7. A strong and influential global playerCommon positions, seats in global institutions, Africa financing Africa.

Fifteen flagship projects — and where each actually is

These are the named accelerators. Executive Council papers in 2025–26 still flag slow ratification, thin project finance, and thin skills as the three brakes.

# Flagship Aim Where it is now
1 Integrated High-Speed Train Network Connect capitals and commercial centres. Vision and scoping study done. Audit of the Union of African Railways launched. Detail engineering unfunded. National rail projects exist; a continental HSR does not. Scoping
2 African Commodities Strategy Stop exporting raw rocks; add value and local content. Strategy adopted; country uptake uneven. The same problem this dashboard tracks in chrome, manganese, PGMs and fertiliser. Uneven
3 AfCFTA A single continental market. Double intra-African trade. The furthest along. 54 of 55 signed (Eritrea unsigned). Afreximbank’s 2026 African Trade Report: 49 of 54 ratified by July 2025. Secretariat in Accra is live. Guided Trade Initiative expanded past 39 countries. 49 provisional tariff schedules submitted, 48 adopted — only 17 full schedules covering all categories. Intra-African trade still about 16% (DIRCO, May 2026). PAPSS is live for local-currency settlement. AfCFTA Digital Trade Corridor JV signed in Accra, 31 August 2026. Furthest along
4 African Passport & free movement Africans travel, work and live on their own continent. Protocol adopted 2018. About 32–33 signatures. Four ratifications (Rwanda, Niger, Mali, São Tomé and Príncipe). Fifteen needed to enter into force. South Africa has not ratified. Stuck
5 Silencing the Guns End wars, coups, genocide, gender-based violence — originally by 2020. The 2020 deadline was missed and rolled forward. Sahel juntas, Sudan, eastern DRC and a cluster of unconstitutional changes of government are the opposite of the target. Africa Center (Nov 2025): serious setbacks on democratisation and constitutionalism. Missed
6 Grand Inga Dam ~43,200 MW on the Congo, feeding the power pools. Still at technical and environmental-impact work. No generation at Grand Inga scale. Ties to the CMP, power pools and CMP interconnectors already on this dashboard. Studies
7 SAATM — Single African Air Transport Market One liberalised African sky. Open routes, drop restrictive bilaterals. Launched 2018. 38 states signed by mid-2026 (ICAO: >80% of intra-African traffic). Only 26 have detailed implementation memoranda. Intra-African connectivity 14.5% (2022) → about 23% (late 2025). Intra-African routes 59 (2023) → 124 (March 2026). Lomé Ministerial Declaration, June 2026, to put a clock on it. Africa is still ~2% of world air traffic. Partial
8 African Economic Forum Annual political–private–civil meeting on transformation. Convening happens; it is not a binding delivery vehicle. Institutional
9 African financial institutions African Investment Bank, Pan-African Stock Exchange, African Monetary Fund, African Central Bank. Treaties exist; operationalisation is incomplete. Resource mobilisation is still the soft underbelly of the whole Agenda. Incomplete
10 Pan-African e-Network Broadband, e-services, an African e-society. Digital policy and cybersecurity work has moved; a single continental network has not. Partial
11 Africa Outer Space Strategy Satellites for agriculture, disaster, climate, finance, security. Strategy in place; national space agencies (including SANSA) are the real capacity. Strategy
12 African Virtual and e-University Open, distance and e-learning at continental scale. Named as a STYIP high-impact project; not a finished university. Early
13 Cyber security AU Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection. Legal instrument exists; ratification and capacity lag. Legal
14 Great African Museum A focal centre for African cultural heritage (Algiers is the designated site). Political commitment; construction and collections are the long game. Slow
15 Encyclopaedia Africana An African-authored record of African life and history. Editorial project underway; not a finished encyclopaedia. In progress

Where it is now

Scoreboard — three different rulers

Do not mash these into one number. Each report measures a different year and a different target set.

  • First continental report (FTYIP vs 2019 targets, 31 of 55 states): about 32%.
  • Second continental report (vs 2021 targets, 38 of 55 states): about 51%. East Africa 53%, Southern Africa 50%, West 45%, Central 42%, North 39%. Aspiration 2 (integration) and Aspiration 4 (peace, then) were the stronger scores on that cut.
  • FTYIP evaluation presented to the 37th Assembly (2024): overall 39% against the decade’s own targets. Aspiration 2 at 90%, Aspiration 4 at 73%, Aspiration 3 (governance) at 17%. Ghana’s NDPC later cited that 39% as the first-decade success rate. The evaluation rated the plan very relevant, weak on efficiency, moderately effective, weak on sustainability.
  • First biennial on the STYIP (2024–2025): AU Commission Chairperson Mahmoud Ali Youssouf told the Ministerial Follow-up Committee (June 2026) that continental performance stands at 53%. Progress is visible. It is not at the speed or scale the moonshots need. Governance reforms and citizen empowerment are the brighter patches; several moonshots are uneven.
What the Assembly is saying in 2026

The February 2026 Assembly decision on the Champion’s 5th continental report (2024–25) is blunt: measurable progress, insufficient pace and depth to hit 2033, especially on structural transformation, human capital, productive employment, peace and security, and domestic resource mobilisation.

Member states are told to fold the STYIP into national development plans, medium-term expenditure frameworks and sector strategies — not leave it as a DIRCO speech. The biennial performance report is now a standing accountability product.

Three brakes the Chairperson named
  1. Regional integration — push AfCFTA, air liberalisation, energy-market integration and digital, and close the protocol-implementation gap.
  2. Peace and security — without it there is no investment and no trade.
  3. Financing — domestic resource mobilisation, tax, public-financial management, illicit financial flows, innovative instruments.
The Union is running the plan on a thin machine. At the 52nd PRC in Addis Ababa (3 June 2026) the Commission said it is operating at about 30% of required staffing and about 25% of its global budget, including programmes funded from statutory contributions. Executive Council papers also note a special fund of US$5 million to implement STYIP tools (M&E, indicator handbook, communication, domestication guidelines, domestic-resource-mobilisation strategy). That is not the financing of Grand Inga or a high-speed rail. It is the financing of the spreadsheet.
Who owns delivery
  • AU Commission — political coordination. Chairperson Mahmoud Ali Youssouf.
  • AUDA-NEPAD — the development agency that implements. NEPAD was founded in 2001 by Algeria, Egypt, Nigeria, Senegal and South Africa; the five founders met again in El Alamein on 3 October 2026 for the 25-year stocktake (Al-Alamein Declaration).
  • Champion of Agenda 2063 — President Alassane Ouattara of Côte d’Ivoire. Mandate: political leadership, domestication, M&E, resource mobilisation.
  • Ministerial Follow-up Committee — last public session in this record, mid-2026, on the first STYIP biennial. South Africa chairs the AU ministerial committee on Agenda 2063 (DIRCO, May 2026).
  • RECs — SADC, ECOWAS, EAC, ECCAS, AMU, COMESA, IGAD and the others are the regional pipes. National development plans are the last mile.
  • Public dashboard — AUDA-NEPAD country platforms and the Agenda 2063 progress dashboard.

South Africa’s place in it

Pretoria is inside the architecture, not a spectator. Dlamini-Zuma chaired the Commission when the Agenda was written. South Africa is a NEPAD founder and still sits on AUDA-NEPAD’s political story. DIRCO’s May 2026 budget vote said South Africa chairs the AU ministerial committee on Agenda 2063 and has been nominated to the AfCFTA committee, while noting intra-African trade at 16% and SADC chairship from August 2026.

AUDA-NEPAD’s country page treats the National Development Plan 2030 as South Africa’s domestication vehicle. That is the honest mapping: Agenda 2063 is continental language; the NDP is the domestic plan it is supposed to be folded into. Alignment is a claim governments make in biennial reports. Delivery is electricity, rail, ports, visas and industrial policy — the same constraints this dashboard already tracks.

Where SA is on the flagships that bite
  • AfCFTA — signed and ratified. The Guided Trade Initiative and tariff schedules are the live work. DTIC owns the commercial end; see Bilateral Trading and DTIC.
  • Free movement / African Passport — South Africa has not ratified the AU Protocol. Large labour-market and undocumented-migration politics sit behind that. Pretoria has separately been pressing SADC members to ratify the SADC free-movement protocol (10 signatures, 7 ratifications, 11 needed as of August 2026). Those are two different instruments.
  • SAATM — South Africa is a large aviation market; the continental gap is still bilateral restrictions and thin intra-African connectivity.
  • Grand Inga and the power pools — SA’s interest is SAPP, the CMP interconnectors, and whether Inga ever becomes firm energy rather than a slide. See Africa Continental Master Plan, Power Pools and SAPP.
  • Commodities / value addition — the same fight as ferrochrome, manganese, PGMs and fertiliser on this dashboard.

The tension that will not go away: South Africa wants to lead a more integrated Africa while running a tight immigration policy. AfCFTA without movement of people is a goods agreement with a services and skills hole in it.

What “on track” would actually look like by 2033

AUDA-NEPAD and the Africa Center’s 2025 stocktake are aligned on the next milestones: operational pieces of the high-speed rail, wider energy access, a real lift in intra-African trade, and visa-free travel that is widespread rather than a protocol in a drawer. AfCFTA, the African Passport, SAATM and the African Single Electricity Market (AfSEM) are the named STYIP priorities.

The ISS African Futures work is the caution: even a combined “Agenda 2063 scenario” only closes a slice of the gap by the third ten-year plan (2043) unless trade integration, governance, agriculture, manufacturing and infrastructure move together. The first decade proved the Agenda is politically relevant. The second decade is the test of whether ministries budget for it.
