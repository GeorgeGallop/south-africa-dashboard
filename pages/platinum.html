Platinum (Pt)

South Africa produces ~70% of the world's mined platinum
Overview Platinum Palladium Rhodium Ruthenium Iridium Osmium
What is it?

A dense, silvery-white precious metal (atomic number 78). Chemically inert, extremely heat-resistant, and one of the most effective industrial catalysts known.

Where is it found?

Bushveld Igneous Complex — mainly the Merensky Reef, UG2 chromitite, and Platreef in North West, Limpopo and Mpumalanga. Zimbabwe's Great Dyke is a distant second.

What is it used for?

Diesel and gasoline catalytic converters, jewellery, glass manufacturing, petroleum reforming, chemical catalysts, medical devices, and hydrogen fuel cells / PEM electrolysers.

Supply Chain

Mined as a 4E/6E PGM basket → concentrated and smelted to matte → refined in South Africa (Rustenburg, Springs) → sold as sponge or ingot to catalyst fabricators, jewellers and industrial users.

What platinum is

Platinum is the namesake metal of the platinum group. It is denser than gold, melts at 1,768°C, and does not oxidise in air. Those properties — plus an unmatched ability to catalyse oxidation and reduction reactions — are why a few grams of platinum in a vehicle exhaust can convert carbon monoxide, hydrocarbons and NOx into carbon dioxide, water and nitrogen.

South Africa's Bushveld Complex holds the bulk of known platinum reserves. The Merensky Reef is relatively platinum-rich; the UG2 carries more of the minor PGMs (rhodium, ruthenium, iridium) alongside platinum; the Platreef in Limpopo is a bulk, lower-grade deposit that can be mined open-pit (Mogalakwena, and Ivanhoe's Platreef project). There are no standalone "platinum mines" in the ordinary sense: every ounce of platinum comes out of a mixed PGM basket with palladium, rhodium and the minor metals.

The metal is typically sold as sponge (porous powder preferred by industrial users) or ingot (the form used in jewellery and investment). Fabricators such as Johnson Matthey, BASF, Umicore and Heraeus convert sponge into catalyst coatings, chemicals and mill products.

What it is used for

Johnson Matthey's May 2026 PGM Market Report puts 2025 world platinum demand at 8.02 million ounces. The split is:

  • Automotive (~3.33 Moz, ~41%): Diesel oxidation catalysts and gasoline three-way catalysts. Platinum loadings are higher on diesel and heavy-duty engines; some gasoline formulations have been shifting metal from palladium back into platinum as the Pd/Pt price ratio moved.
  • Industrial (~2.63 Moz, ~33%): Glass (bushings that draw fibreglass and LCD glass), petroleum reforming, nitric acid and silicone production, chemical process catalysts, pollution-control catalysts, electrical contacts, and biomedical devices (pacemakers, chemotherapy drugs such as cisplatin).
  • Jewellery (~1.51 Moz, ~19%): Fabrication is concentrated in China, Japan and India. High gold prices in 2025 pushed Chinese workshops toward platinum, lifting jewellery demand to a multi-year high.
  • Investment (~0.56 Moz, ~7%): Bars, coins and ETFs. China accounted for the bulk of physical bar demand in 2025; North America and Japan are smaller but established markets.

Hydrogen is still a small slice of annual ounces, but it is the fastest-growing new use: platinum is the cathode catalyst in PEM fuel cells and a co-catalyst (with iridium) in PEM electrolysers that make green hydrogen.

Who buys it

Almost none of South Africa's platinum is consumed at home. The buyers sit in the auto, jewellery, glass and chemical supply chains of East Asia, Europe and North America.

  • Catalyst fabricators — Johnson Matthey, BASF, Umicore and Heraeus — are the largest physical offtakers. They coat ceramic or metallic substrates for vehicle OEMs (Toyota, Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz, BMW, Hyundai, Ford, Stellantis and the Chinese majors).
  • China is the swing buyer: jewellery fabrication, industrial glass, and (in 2025) a surge of bar and ETF buying after PGM futures launched on the Guangzhou Futures Exchange. USGS data for 2021–24 show South Africa supplying about 49% of US platinum imports, with Belgium, Germany and Italy as the main European entrepôts.
  • Japan remains a core jewellery and industrial market, and a consistent destination for South African refined metal.
  • Glass and chemical companies in China, Europe and the US lease or buy platinum for high-temperature equipment that is recycled in a closed loop — so their net new-metal demand is much smaller than the metal sitting in plants.
  • Investors — Chinese retail, Western ETFs, and some strategic stockpiling — absorbed more than half a million ounces in 2025.

On the supply side the metal is sold by Valterra Platinum (formerly Anglo American Platinum / Amplats), Implats, Sibanye-Stillwater, Northam and smaller Bushveld producers, typically under long-term contracts and spot sales to the fabricators above.

Production and consumption

South Africa is the world's dominant primary producer. Johnson Matthey estimates South African platinum supplies at 3.96 million oz in 2025 (about 123 tonnes), down just under 5% on 2024. Underlying mine output was closer to 3.86 Moz after first-quarter flooding closed parts of Valterra's Amandelbult mine. Russia (~650 koz) and Zimbabwe (~513 koz) are the next sources. Secondary supply — mainly autocatalyst recycling — added 1.55 Moz, still well below pre-COVID recovery rates.

The market has been in deficit for four consecutive years. Combined supply of 7.10 Moz against demand of 8.02 Moz left a 915,000 oz shortfall in 2025 — filled from above-ground stocks. The Minerals Council reports a 2025 average platinum price of US$1,288/oz (+34% year-on-year); the metal then spiked above $2,300/oz in December 2025 on Chinese futures speculation before ending the year just over $2,000. USGS puts South African PGM reserves at 63,000 tonnes — about 91% of the global total.

South African production is already well below the ~5 Moz a year that was normal a decade ago. Northam has flagged a path toward ~3 Moz by 2035 if closures continue. Employment in the PGM sector is contracting with output. Power costs, deep-level mining and end-of-life shafts are the binding constraints, not geology.

The future

Platinum sits at the centre of two opposing transitions. Battery-electric vehicles need no exhaust catalyst, so every BEV that replaces an ICE car is lost automotive ounces. That threat is real, but slower than the forecasts of 2021–23: ICE production proved resilient in 2025, and Johnson Matthey now sees automotive platinum demand easing only gradually (3.33 Moz in 2025 → 3.23 Moz in 2026).

The offset is the hydrogen economy. PEM fuel-cell trucks, buses and some passenger FCEVs use platinum on the cathode; PEM electrolysers use it with iridium on the anode. If green hydrogen scales for heavy transport, ammonia and steel, platinum demand from energy applications could grow from a rounding error into a material end-use. Jewellery and industrial glass provide a floor that palladium and rhodium do not have.

On the supply side, Ivanhoe's Platreef (Phase 1 target ~100,000 oz PGM) is the main new South African project. Against that, shaft closures, flooding risk and Eskom/private-power costs point to a structurally smaller industry. Recycling will have to fill a larger share of the gap — and recycling has been slow to recover. The most likely 2030s picture is: fewer South African ounces, still-dominant South African reserves, a smaller autocatalyst market, and a hydrogen/industrial bid that decides whether platinum remains a growth metal or a managed-decline one.
