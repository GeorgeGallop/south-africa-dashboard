🚛 Transportation & Freight Logistics

The Movement of Goods — South Africa's Great Modal Shift Story

South Africa's freight logistics system tells one of the most consequential and least-told stories in its economic history. From a rail-dominated system that moved ~89% of land freight in the 1980s, the country underwent a dramatic modal shift to road haulage following deregulation in 1988. Decades later, the consequences — congested highways, sky-high logistics costs, stranded mineral exports — have forced a national reckoning. Now, the country is trying to reverse course: to shift freight back from road to rail through the most ambitious transport reforms since the formation of Transnet. This is the story of how South Africa moves its goods, why the system broke, and what the fix looks like.

Part 1: The Golden Age of Rail (1850s–1980s)

South Africa's transport system was built around rail from the beginning. The first railway line — Point to Durban in 1860 — was constructed to move goods from port to interior. The diamond rush in Kimberley (1867) and gold discovery on the Witwatersrand (1886) triggered explosive rail expansion. British colonial and mining-house capital laid tracks connecting the interior mines to coastal ports at Durban, Cape Town, Port Elizabeth, and East London. This was not passenger rail — it was freight infrastructure for mineral exports, and it defined the country's economic geography for a century.

By the formation of the Union of South Africa in 1910, railways were consolidated under state control as South African Railways and Harbours (SAR&H). Through the apartheid era, SAR&H — and later Transnet — enjoyed a protected statutory monopoly over land freight. Road haulage was heavily regulated. The result: by the 1980s, rail carried an estimated 89% of land freight. It was efficient for bulk long-haul commodities (coal, iron ore, manganese, maize, and manufactured goods) and kept logistics costs low relative to the size of the economy.

Why rail dominated: South Africa's economic geography naturally favours rail. The country's mineral wealth is concentrated in the interior (Mpumalanga coal fields, Northern Cape iron and manganese, Bushveld platinum) while its export ports are on the coast (Richards Bay, Durban, Saldanha, Ngqura). These are long-haul, high-volume, low-value-per-tonne movements — exactly the conditions where rail is overwhelmingly more efficient than road. A single train on the Sishen–Saldanha iron ore line can carry the equivalent of ~200 trucks, on a dedicated heavy-haul railway with lower fuel consumption per tonne-kilometre and far lower carbon emissions.

Part 2: Deregulation & The Great Modal Shift (1988–2010s)

In 1988, on the eve of South Africa's political transition, the Transport Deregulation Act was passed. It liberalised road freight — ending the protected monopoly that SAR&H/Transnet had enjoyed for decades. The intention was sound: introduce competition, improve efficiency, lower prices. But the execution was asymmetric.

Road freight was liberalised. Rail was not.

The deregulation created an uneven playing field. Road hauliers could enter the market freely, set their own prices, and compete on service. Transnet, by contrast, remained a state-owned monopoly — subject to political interference, bloated cost structures, and no competitive pressure to improve. The predictable result: freight shifted massively from rail to road, especially for higher-value, time-sensitive general freight (containers, automotive parts, manufactured goods, foodstuffs).

Why road won:
Flexibility

Trucks go door-to-door. A manufacturer in Johannesburg can load a container at its factory and deliver it directly to Durban port — rail requires trucking to and from rail terminals on both ends, adding time and cost for the "first and last mile".

Speed & Reliability

A truck from Johannesburg to Durban takes ~8 hours, door-to-door, departing when the customer wants. Rail takes days — subject to locomotive availability, signalling failures, cable theft, and Transnet's operational unpredictability. For time-sensitive goods, the choice was obvious.

Transnet's Own Failures

As Transnet's operational performance deteriorated in the 2000s–2010s (underinvestment, state capture, locomotive scandals), rail became increasingly unreliable. Customers who would have preferred rail for cost reasons were forced onto road by sheer necessity — they couldn't wait for Transnet.

The consequence: a road-dominated freight system

Today, South Africa's freight moves predominantly by truck. The N3 highway between Durban and Gauteng — the country's most important freight corridor — carries approximately 8,000 trucks per day. The N1, N4, and N12 corridors are similarly congested. This road dominance has produced negative consequences:

  • High logistics costs: At ~12% of GDP, South Africa's logistics costs are among the highest in the world. Peer emerging economies like Brazil, India, and Turkey operate at 8–9%.
  • Road damage: Heavy trucks cause disproportionate wear on road surfaces. The national road maintenance backlog is estimated at over R200 billion, and every heavy truck accelerates the deterioration. Road users — including private motorists — effectively subsidise freight hauliers through fuel levies and tolls.
  • Safety: Truck-involved accidents account for a large share of fatalities on major highways. Driver fatigue, overloaded vehicles, and poor vehicle maintenance are endemic problems in the highly competitive, low-margin road freight industry.
  • Carbon emissions: Rail is approximately 3–4 times more fuel-efficient per tonne-kilometre than road freight. The modal shift from rail to road has substantially increased South Africa's transport-sector carbon footprint — at a time when the country has international climate commitments to meet.
  • Export bottlenecks: When Transnet's rail service collapsed in 2022–2023, coal exporters could not simply switch to trucks — a single coal train carries ~8,000 tonnes; replacing it would require ~250 trucks. That's not feasible. So exports were simply lost. The Minerals Council estimated R50+ billion in lost mineral export revenue in 2022–2023 alone.

Part 3: Why Bulk Commodities Must Move by Rail

The commodities that drive South Africa's export economy — coal, iron ore, manganese, chrome — are physically impossible to move at scale by road. This is not a policy preference; it's a matter of physics and economics:

The Iron Ore Corridor: Sishen → Saldanha

The 861 km Sishen–Saldanha heavy-haul railway is one of the world's longest dedicated freight trains. Each train carries ~36,000 tonnes of iron ore — the equivalent of approximately 1,050 trucks. The trains run on a dedicated line at ~60 km/h, 24 hours a day. To replace one day of rail traffic on this corridor with trucks would require roughly 3,000 truck movements on the N7 and N14 highways — an impossibility. The rail line is not optional; it is the only way the ore reaches port.

The Coal Line: Mpumalanga → Richards Bay

The coal export line to Richards Bay is the largest single export corridor in the country. At its peak (2017), it moved approximately 76 million tonnes of coal per year — about 2,000 trainloads. After the line's deterioration (cable theft, maintenance collapse), volumes fell below 50 million tonnes in 2022–2023. The lost tonnes could not be diverted to road — a single coal train carries ~8,000 tonnes, equivalent to ~250 trucks. The line's capacity is determined by rail infrastructure, not by whether trucks are available. When the trains stop, the exports stop.

This is the central insight that underpins the reform push: South Africa's mineral export economy cannot function without a working rail system. Road can take some general freight. It cannot take bulk. The deregulation of 1988 inadvertently created a situation where road captured the higher-value freight that cross-subsidised the rail network, leaving rail with only the low-margin bulk traffic — while simultaneously not investing enough in the infrastructure that bulk traffic depends on. By the 2020s, the entire system was failing: road was overwhelmed, rail was collapsing, and exports were stranded at the mines.

Part 4: The Push Back to Rail — Reform and Resistance

South Africa is now attempting the most ambitious transport reform in its history: to reverse the modal shift, move freight back from road to rail, and structurally restructure the rail monopoly so the failure of the 2010s cannot repeat. The reform architecture has three components:

1. Vertical Separation

Transnet's track infrastructure is being legally separated from its train operations. An independent Infrastructure Manager will control access to the network and allocate slots to all operators — including Transnet Freight Rail — on equal, non-discriminatory terms. This breaks the conflict of interest where Transnet was both the referee (owning the track) and the player (running the trains).

2. Open Access / Third-Party Operators

Private rail operators (Traxtion, Mkango Rail, and potentially mining companies themselves) are being granted rights to run their own trains on Transnet's network. This introduces competition — the same competitive pressure that made road freight efficient — into a rail system that has never faced it. Over 100 access applications have been received.

3. Independent Regulation

The Transport Economic Regulator (established by the Economic Regulation of Transport Act, 2024) sets access tariffs, monitors market conduct, and enforces non-discriminatory access. This removes tariff-setting from Transnet's control and places it with an independent, expert body accountable to Parliament.

Why this matters for the modal shift: The reason road captured freight was that road was competitive and rail was not. The solution is not to re-regulate road (which would cripple the economy) — it is to make rail competitive. Vertical separation, open access, and independent regulation are designed to do exactly that: introduce the same competitive dynamics into rail that made road efficient, while preserving rail's inherent cost advantage for bulk and long-haul freight. If successful, the market — not government mandate — will shift freight back to rail because rail will offer a better service at a lower price on corridors where it makes economic sense.

Part 5: The Three Corridors Where the Battle Will Be Won or Lost

The modal shift back to rail will not happen everywhere at once. It will be won — or lost — on three specific corridors where rail has the strongest economic case and where the road congestion is most acute:

Durban–Gauteng (Container Corridor)

The N3 highway carries ~8,000 trucks per day between the Port of Durban and Gauteng's industrial heartland. This is the corridor where road-to-rail shift for general freight is most viable — and most urgent. Traxtion, the first private rail entrant, is targeting this corridor with container trains between City Deep (Johannesburg) and Durban Container Terminal. A shift of 20–30% of container volume back to rail within 3–5 years would dramatically reduce road congestion, logistics costs, and carbon emissions on this overburdened route.

Sishen–Saldanha (Iron Ore)

The 861 km heavy-haul railway is a dedicated Transnet line carrying iron ore from Kumba's Sishen mine to the Saldanha export terminal. This is the most efficient rail corridor in Africa — but even it has suffered from underinvestment. The Department of Transport has flagged this as a priority for a concession or private operating agreement. Given that road replacement is physically impossible at these volumes, the only question is whether the line is operated by Transnet alone or by a competitive operator.

Hotazel–Ngqura (Manganese)

Northern Cape manganese mines need ~35 million tonnes per year of rail capacity. Transnet currently moves less than half that — and manganese is one of South Africa's highest-value export commodities. Private operators like Mkango Rail are being courted to fill the gap on existing track with their own locomotives and crews. This corridor is the most acute test of whether open access can unblock a rail bottleneck faster than waiting for Transnet to fix itself.

Part 6: The Obstacles — Why This Is Hard

Shifting freight back to rail sounds obvious. The economics are clear. The policy is in place. The legislation has been passed. So why hasn't it happened yet?

  • Infrastructure decay: Private operators cannot run trains faster or heavier than the track condition allows. Decades of deferred maintenance mean the rail network needs tens of billions of rand in catch-up investment before private operators can deliver the service levels that compete with road.
  • Transnet resistance: Elements within Transnet — management, unions, supply-chain incumbents who benefit from the closed system — have strong incentives to slow-walk reform. The locomotive procurement scandal showed how deeply the patronage system is embedded.
  • Cable theft & vandalism: The same security failures that crippled Transnet will affect private operators. Security on the rail network is a prerequisite for private investment, not an afterthought.
  • Labour opposition: Unions fear that private operators will bring lower-cost workforces and reduce Transnet's headcount. Any restructuring that triggers a national rail strike could shut down the entire network — a risk that makes politicians cautious.
  • The first/last mile problem: Even if long-haul rail becomes competitive, goods still need to get from the factory to the rail terminal and from the terminal to the port. Road will always handle the first and last mile. The question is whether the long-haul middle is handled by rail or by truck — and that depends on rail reliability.
  • Cream-skimming risk: Private operators will target the most profitable high-volume corridors (Durban–Gauteng, export bulk lines) and avoid low-density rural routes. The regulator must design pricing so the network can still sustain loss-making lines that serve poorer provinces.
The Stakes: What Happens If This Works — And If It Doesn't
✅ If reform succeeds:

Logistics costs fall from ~12% to ~9% of GDP (saving ~R150 billion/year). Rail regains 40–50% of land freight share. Coal, iron ore, and manganese exports recover to 2017 peak levels. The N3 highway congestion eases as 20–30% of container volume shifts to rail. Carbon emissions from freight transport decline. The economy gains 1–2% of additional annual GDP growth — the difference between stagnation and recovery.

❌ If reform fails:

Coal exports decline below 40 million tonnes/year as rail infrastructure deteriorates further. Manganese and iron ore exports are permanently strangled. Road congestion worsens — the N3 becomes a permanent traffic jam. Logistics costs remain the highest in the world, eroding the competitiveness of every South African exporter. The R50+ billion annual export losses become structural. Mining companies accelerate divestment from South Africa because they cannot get their product to port.

The story of South African transportation is a story of a country that built one of the world's great rail networks to serve its mines — then deregulated road haulage without reforming rail, watched its freight shift to trucks, and is now racing to rebuild a competitive rail system before the bulk export economy collapses under its own logistics weight. The next five years will determine which direction the modal shift goes.
