⚡ The Economic Freedom Fighters

A radical left-wing party born from the ANC's internal contradictions: Marxist-Leninist, anti-imperialist, and unapologetically confrontational. The EFF has reshaped South African politics since its founding in 2013.

📚 Julius Malema, EFF Manifesto Launch, 2013:
“We are not asking for the land. We are taking the land. We are not asking for the mines. We are taking the mines. Economic freedom in our lifetime — this is a struggle, not a negotiation.”
📚 Mpho Tshabalala, political analyst, 2024:
“The EFF has achieved what no other black-led opposition party has: it has forced the ANC to acknowledge that the freedom charter's promise of economic transformation remains unfulfilled. Whether its methods are productive or destructive is the central question.”

The Economic Freedom Fighters (EFF) is a Marxist-Leninist and Fanonist political party founded in 2013 by Julius Malema and other former ANC Youth League members. It has rapidly grown from a fringe movement to the third-largest party in South Africa's Parliament, winning approximately 9.5% of the vote in the 2024 general election. The EFF positions itself as a radical alternative to the ANC, which it accuses of betraying the liberation struggle by embracing neoliberal economics and failing to redistribute wealth and land. This page examines the EFF's founding, ideology, leadership, policies, electoral record, controversies, and its complex relationship with the ANC and other parties.

🎓 Founding (2013) — From ANC Youth League to EFF
2010 — Malema Expelled from ANC

Julius Malema, President of the ANC Youth League since 2008, is expelled from the ANC in 2010 after a series of disciplinary hearings. The charges: bringing the party into disrepute through inflammatory statements (including singing "Shoot the Boer"), and sowing division within the ANC. Malema's expulsion is widely seen as the culmination of a factional war with the Zuma faction of the ANC. Malema had been a vocal critic of Zuma's leadership and had called for regime change in Botswana, embarrassing the ANC government. [SA History]

2011–2013 — The Wilderness Years

After his expulsion, Malema faces tax evasion charges (of which he is eventually acquitted) and a corruption trial related to government tenders. He travels to Zimbabwe, Libya, and other countries, developing his ideological views. He studies the writings of Frantz Fanon, Karl Marx, and Mao Zedong. During this period, he and his allies — including Floyd Shivambu — begin planning a new political party. [Daily Maverick]

July 2013 — EFF Founded

The Economic Freedom Fighters (EFF) is launched at a rally in Soweto. The founding manifesto, titled "Radical Economic Transformation," calls for the nationalisation of mines, banks, and other strategic industries; the expropriation of land without compensation; and the establishment of a welfare state with free education, healthcare, and housing. The party adopts a uniform of red berets, overalls, and worker attire — deliberately evoking images of revolutionary movements in Cuba, Venezuela, and the Soviet Union. Malema is elected Commander-in-Chief. [SA History]

2014 — First Election: 6.4%

In its first electoral test, the EFF wins 6.4% of the national vote (1.1 million votes) and 25 seats in Parliament. It becomes the third-largest party, overtaking much older opposition parties like the IFP and Cope. Malema becomes a member of Parliament. The EFF's parliamentary debut is dramatic: its members disrupt President Zuma's State of the Nation Address in 2015 by repeatedly shouting "Pay back the money!" — referencing the Public Protector's finding that Zuma had unduly benefited from non-security upgrades to his Nkandla homestead. [IEC]

📜 Ideology — Marxism-Leninism, Fanonism & Anti-Imperialism
📚 Frantz Fanon, The Wretched of the Earth (1961):
“National liberation, national renaissance, the restoration of nationhood to the people, commonwealth: whatever may be the headings used or the new formulas introduced, decolonisation is always a violent phenomenon.”

The EFF's ideology combines several strands of left-wing thought, adapted to the South African context. Its core ideological commitments include:

1. Marxism-Leninism

The EFF explicitly identifies as a Marxist-Leninist party, meaning it believes in the revolutionary overthrow of capitalism and the establishment of a dictatorship of the proletariat. In practice, the party advocates for state ownership of the means of production (mines, banks, land) and a command economy. It rejects the ANC's embrace of neoliberal economics (GEAR, the Growth, Employment and Redistribution strategy) as a betrayal of the Freedom Charter. The party's internal structure is highly centralised and hierarchical, with Malema as Commander-in-Chief wielding substantial power. Critics argue the party's internal democracy is weak, with little tolerance for dissent. [EFF Official]

2. Fanonism

The EFF draws heavily on the work of Martinican psychiatrist and revolutionary philosopher Frantz Fanon, particularly his critique of colonialism and his argument that decolonisation must involve not just political independence but the complete overthrow of colonial social and economic structures. Fanon's ideas resonate with the EFF's view that South Africa's 1994 political settlement left colonial-era economic structures intact — the economy remains white-owned and controlled, and black South Africans remain subjects of a "neo-colonial" system. The party's uniform — red berets, worker overalls — is a deliberate reference to Fanon's call for the "wretched of the earth" to reclaim their dignity. [SA History]

3. Anti-Imperialism & International Solidarity

The EFF is staunchly anti-imperialist, opposing what it calls "Western imperialism" (particularly the United States, NATO, and the International Monetary Fund) and supporting countries it sees as resisting imperialism: Cuba, Venezuela, Russia, China, Iran, and Palestine. The party has called for South Africa to leave the International Criminal Court (ICC) and to form closer ties with BRICS countries. Its foreign policy positions have drawn criticism for aligning with authoritarian regimes. The EFF is a strong supporter of the Palestinian cause, frequently drawing parallels between apartheid South Africa and the Israeli occupation of Palestine. [EFF Foreign Policy]

4. Radical Economic Transformation

The party's flagship concept — "Radical Economic Transformation" — envisions a complete restructuring of South Africa's economy: nationalisation of all mines, banks, and strategic industries; free land expropriation without compensation; a national minimum wage (significantly higher than the current level); free education from primary to tertiary level; free healthcare; and the establishment of a state-owned pharmaceutical industry. The party argues that only such transformative measures can undo the legacy of apartheid and colonialism. Critics argue these policies are economically unfeasible and would destroy foreign investment. [EFF Land Policy]

5. Pan-Africanism

The EFF is a pan-Africanist party. It envisions a united Africa with open borders, a single currency, and a continental government. It advocates for the repatriation of Africa's stolen resources (diamonds, gold, oil) and reparations for colonialism and the slave trade. The party has called for the removal of statues of colonial figures across South Africa and for the renaming of streets, towns, and cities with African names. [EFF Pan-Africanism]

👤 Leadership — Malema, Shivambu & the Central Command
Julius Malema — Commander-in-Chief

Born 3 March 1981 in Seshego, Limpopo. Malema joined the ANC at age 9, served as President of the ANC Youth League (2008–2012), and was expelled from the ANC in 2010. He is the EFF's founder, chief strategist, and dominant personality. A gifted orator who speaks in a mix of English, isiZulu, Sesotho, and Afrikaans, Malema commands fierce loyalty from supporters and intense dislike from opponents. His personal wealth has grown since entering politics; his business interests include a game farm, farming operations, and a luxury lifestyle that critics say contradicts his socialist rhetoric. The EFF's structure grants him near-total control over the party's direction and candidate selection. [SA History]

Floyd Shivambu — Deputy President

Born 18 January 1983 in Mbiza, Eastern Cape. Shivambu was a political science lecturer before entering politics. He served as a Member of Parliament and as the EFF's Deputy President and chief ideologue. He is widely regarded as the intellectual backbone of the party — less charismatic than Malema but more policy-focused. Shivambu is a prolific writer and speaker, and has authored several policy documents. He faced controversy in 2018 when it emerged that his younger brother had received payments from the controversial VBS Mutual Bank (which collapsed due to fraud). Shivambu was not personally implicated in wrongdoing but the association damaged his reputation. [SA History]

Other Key Figures

Godrich Gardee — Former Secretary-General (resigned 2022 amid a scandal involving his son's alleged sexual misconduct).
Marshall Dlamini — Current Secretary-General.
Lehlogonolo Masoga — Treasury-General (fired in 2022 after criticising Malema).
Mbuyiseni Ndlozi — National Spokesperson and a prominent parliamentary performer, known for his eloquent debates and doctoral qualifications.
Veronica Mente — Deputy President (alongside Shivambu), focused on women's issues.

📖 Key Policies
🏛 1. Land Expropriation Without Compensation

The EFF's most prominent policy is the expropriation of all land without compensation for redistribution to black South Africans. The party argues that land was stolen from black people through colonialism (1913 Land Act) and apartheid, and that no compensation should be paid to the current (largely white) owners. The EFF wants to cap land ownership at 1,000 hectares per person (or per entity) and redistribute all land above this threshold for free. It has proposed a Land Tribunal to adjudicate claims. This policy has generated intense debate: supporters say it is necessary for genuine land reform and economic justice; critics argue it would destroy agricultural productivity, trigger capital flight, and violate property rights protections in the Constitution. [EFF Land Policy]

⚑ 2. Nationalisation of Mines & Banks

The EFF demands the nationalisation of all mines, banks, and strategic industries (including telecommunications, energy, and transport). The party argues that South Africa's mineral wealth — gold, platinum, coal, diamonds, chrome, manganese, iron ore — belongs to all South Africans, not to private shareholders (most of whom are foreign). Nationalisation would, in the EFF's view, capture resource rents for the state to fund social programmes. Critics argue that the mining industry is capital-intensive, requires foreign investment and expertise, and that nationalisation would destroy it — as has happened in Zimbabwe and Venezuela. The party counters that the mines generate enormous profits that leave the country and that South Africa has not benefited from its mineral wealth. [EFF Nationalisation Policy]

🏫 3. Free Education

The EFF advocates for free, decolonised education from primary school through to tertiary level, including postgraduate study. The party argues that the current system excludes the poor and perpetuates inequality. It has been a vocal critic of university fee increases and has supported student protests (including the #FeesMustFall movement of 2015–2016). The EFF wants to "decolonise" the curriculum, arguing that South African universities teach Eurocentric content that ignores African knowledge systems and history. [EFF Education Policy]

🚑 4. Free Healthcare

The party demands the establishment of a free, universal, publicly-funded National Health Insurance (NHI) system, with no role for private medical aids. It argues that healthcare is a human right, not a commodity. The EFF opposes the existing two-tier system (private for the rich, public for the poor) and wants to abolish private healthcare entirely. [EFF Health Policy]

💰 5. Social Welfare & Minimum Wage

The EFF proposes a universal basic income grant (known in South Africa as a Basic Income Grant, or BIG) of R1,500 per month for all South Africans. It also demands a national minimum wage of R5,000 per month (the current national minimum wage is approximately R27.58 per hour, or about R4,400 per month for a 40-hour week). The party argues these measures are essential to address South Africa's extreme poverty and inequality.

The EFF's electoral trajectory shows rapid initial growth (from 6.4% in 2014 to 10.8% in 2019) followed by a decline in 2024 to approximately 9.5%. This decline is attributed to the emergence of the uMkhonto we Sizwe (MK) Party led by former President Jacob Zuma, which split the radical opposition vote. The MK Party, formed in late 2023, won about 14.6% in 2024, overtaking the EFF. The EFF's strongholds include urban townships (Soweto, Alexandra, Diepsloot) and some rural areas in Limpopo, Mpumalanga, and the North West province. The party performs best among young, unemployed, and disenfranchised voters.

⚠ Controversies
📚 Mamphela Ramphele, 2014:
“Julius Malema is playing a dangerous game. He speaks the language of revolution but offers no practical solutions. His inflammatory rhetoric and anti-white statements are creating a climate of fear and division that will destroy the very fabric of our society.”
1. Malema's Statements & Hate Speech Allegations

Julius Malema has been repeatedly accused of hate speech and incitement to violence. In 2022, the South African Equality Court found him guilty of hate speech for singing "Dubula iBhunu" (Shoot the Boer), a liberation struggle song. Malema defended the song as part of South Africa's heritage, but the court ruled that it incited violence against white South Africans. In 2023, Malema was accused of inciting violence during a speech in which he told supporters that foreign nationals should "pack their bags and leave" and that the EFF would "remove them." He has also made inflammatory statements about white farmers, Jews, and Indian South Africans. [SA History]

2. Shane Davids Murder & Intimidation Allegations

In 2021, EFF members were convicted of the murder of Shane Davids, a 24-year-old man who allegedly stole an EFF banner during an election campaign in the Western Cape. The EFF's Mandlenkosi Khohlakala and Mthetheleli Mbelo were found guilty of murder and sentenced to 12 and 10 years in prison respectively. The case raised serious concerns about the EFF's culture of violence and intolerance. The party's leadership initially defended the accused, saying they were protecting party property, before eventually condemning the murder. [News24]

3. VBS Mutual Bank Scandal

In 2018, VBS Mutual Bank collapsed after losing approximately R2 billion due to fraud. The bank had been used by politically-connected individuals and municipalities to park money. Floyd Shivambu's brother, Brian Shivambu, received payments linked to the scandal. Floyd Shivambu was not charged, but the association damaged the EFF's anti-corruption credentials. The EFF was accused of hypocrisy: it had built its reputation on exposing ANC corruption, yet its own leadership was linked to a major corruption scandal. [AmaBhungane]

4. Economic Impact Debate

Critics argue that the EFF's radical policy proposals — particularly land expropriation without compensation — damage investor confidence and deter foreign investment. The party's influence over ANC policy (the ANC adopted a motion in 2017 supporting land expropriation without compensation) is seen as contributing to economic uncertainty. The EFF counters that the policies of the ANC and the DA have failed to address poverty and inequality, and that radical transformation is the only option. The debate is unresolved. Some economists argue that the EFF's proposals would destroy the economy; others argue that the current trajectory is also unsustainable and that fundamental change is necessary.

5. Internal Party Democracy

The EFF is a highly centralised party with Malema as Commander-in-Chief. Critics say the party lacks internal democracy and tolerates no dissent. Members who have criticised Malema — including Lehlogonolo Masoga (former Treasury-General, fired in 2022) and Dr. Dali Mpofu (prominent EFF lawyer) — have been expelled or marginalised. The party's Central Command Team (CCT) is dominated by Malema loyalists. Supporters argue that the party's tight discipline is necessary to avoid the factional wars that have destroyed the ANC. [Daily Maverick]

🤝 Relationship with ANC & Other Parties
🟤 Relationship with the ANC

The EFF's relationship with the ANC is complex and ambivalent. On one hand, the EFF presents itself as the true heir of the liberation struggle — more radical, more principled, and less corrupt than the ANC. The party frequently attacks ANC leaders as "sell-outs" and "counter-revolutionaries." On the other hand, the EFF has often worked with the ANC in Parliament: supporting the ANC's motion to amend the Constitution to allow land expropriation without compensation; voting with the ANC on the NHI Bill; and supporting the ANC's foreign policy positions on Palestine and Western Sahara. In critical moments, the EFF has sided with the ANC against opposition parties — most notably in 2016 when the EFF refused to support opposition-sponsored motions of no confidence in President Zuma. The relationship between the two parties has been described as "frenemies": rivals in competition for the same radical base, but sharing a common ideological heritage and occasionally collaborating on issues of mutual interest. [SA History]

🤝 Relationship with the DA & Other Opposition

The EFF's relationship with the Democratic Alliance (DA) is characterised by deep hostility. The EFF views the DA as a party of "white monopoly capital" and frequently accuses it of racism and defending apartheid-era privilege. The DA, in turn, accuses the EFF of being an authoritarian, race-baiting party that would destroy the economy. In 2024, when the ANC formed a Government of National Unity (GNU) that included the DA, the EFF refused to join, calling the DA an "enemy of the revolution." The EFF has been in coalition with smaller parties in various municipalities (e.g., Johannesburg, Tshwane) but these coalitions have been unstable and often ended in acrimony.

🤝 Relationship with the MK Party

The emergence of the uMkhonto we Sizwe (MK) Party in late 2023 — led by Jacob Zuma — has strained the EFF. The MK Party draws from the same pool of radical, disenfranchised voters that the EFF has cultivated. In the 2024 election, the MK Party won 14.6% of the vote, overtaking the EFF as the third-largest party. The relationship between the EFF and MK is hostile: Malema has accused Zuma and the MK Party of being a diversion created by the ANC to split the radical vote. Zuma, in turn, has dismissed Malema as an "ANC agent." The tension between these two radical opposition forces is one of the key dynamics shaping South African politics. [News24]

⚠ The Central Dilemma: Radical Voice or Destructive Force?

The EFF is one of the most significant political forces to emerge in post-apartheid South Africa. It has given voice to millions of South Africans who feel abandoned by the ANC's neoliberal turn — the unemployed youth, the landless, the poor in townships and rural areas. The party has forced the ANC to confront its failures and has pushed issues like land reform and economic transformation to the centre of political debate.

But the EFF has also been accused of embracing authoritarianism, promoting racial division, and proposing policies that would destroy the economy. Its internal structure is deeply undemocratic, with all power concentrated in Malema. Its members have been convicted of violent crimes. And the party's relationship with the ANC — oscillating between confrontation and cooperation — has led some to question whether it is genuinely oppositional or merely a radical pressure group within the broader ANC ecosystem.

The 2024 election result — in which the EFF lost ground and was overtaken by the MK Party — has raised existential questions: can the EFF continue to grow, or has it reached its ceiling? Can it adapt its strategy to a changing political landscape? The answers will shape South African politics for years to come.
