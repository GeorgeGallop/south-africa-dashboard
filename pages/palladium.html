Palladium (Pd)

South Africa is the world's second-largest miner, after Russia
Overview Platinum Palladium Rhodium Ruthenium Iridium Osmium
What is it?

A silvery-white PGM (atomic number 46), lighter than platinum, with exceptional catalytic activity in gasoline exhaust chemistry and a unique ability to absorb hydrogen.

Where is it found?

Bushveld Complex (especially Platreef and parts of the western and eastern limbs) as a co-product of platinum mining. Russia's Norilsk-Talnakh nickel-copper deposits are the world's largest primary palladium source. Also Montana (Stillwater) and Canada (Sudbury).

What is it used for?

Gasoline catalytic converters (the dominant use), multilayer ceramic capacitors and semiconductor plating, hydrogen purification, dentistry, and some chemical catalysts.

Supply Chain

Mined in a PGM or nickel-copper basket → smelted and refined → sold as sponge to autocatalyst coaters and electronics fabricators. A large secondary stream comes from end-of-life catalytic converters.

What palladium is

Palladium is the most widely used PGM by ounces. It is less dense than platinum, melts at 1,555°C, and is the metal of choice for three-way catalytic converters on gasoline engines — it oxidises carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons while working with rhodium to reduce NOx. It also absorbs up to 900 times its own volume of hydrogen, which is why it is used in hydrogen purification membranes and some fuel-cell and sensor applications.

Unlike platinum, palladium is not a South African monopoly. The Bushveld is platinum-dominant; Russia's Norilsk Nickel nickel-copper ores are palladium-dominant. That geology is why Russia is usually the world's largest palladium miner and South Africa is second — even though South Africa dominates the rest of the PGM basket. In the Bushveld, the Platreef (Mogalakwena) is relatively palladium-rich compared with Merensky and UG2.

From the 1990s, gasoline catalyst formulations shifted heavily into palladium because it was cheaper than platinum. That substitution, plus tightening US and Chinese emissions rules, drove palladium above platinum for years and to a record near $2,900/oz in 2021. The ratio has since reversed as miners and fabricators substituted platinum back into gasoline catalysts.

What it is used for

Johnson Matthey puts 2025 world palladium demand at 10.14 million ounces — the largest of any PGM. Roughly four-fifths is automotive:

  • Automotive (~8.23 Moz, ~81%): Gasoline three-way catalysts on cars and light trucks, plus some hybrid systems. Battery-electric vehicles do not use palladium. Hybrid vehicles still do, which is why slower BEV adoption has supported demand.
  • Electrical and electronics (~0.54 Moz): Electroplating of connectors and lead frames, multilayer ceramic capacitors (MLCCs), and chip-to-board interconnections. This is the strategic link to the semiconductor supply chain.
  • Chemical (~0.52 Moz): Process catalysts, including acetylene hydrogenation and some fine-chemical routes.
  • Dental and biomedical (~0.16 Moz): Alloys in crowns and bridges, now in long-term decline as ceramics replace metal.
  • Jewellery (~0.08 Moz) and investment (~0.38 Moz): White-gold alloying and a smaller bar/ETF market than platinum. Investment was strongly positive in 2025.

Who buys it

  • Autocatalyst fabricators — BASF, Johnson Matthey, Umicore, Heraeus, and Chinese coaters — buy the majority of newly refined ounces and deliver coated substrates to vehicle plants in China, Europe, North America, Japan and Korea.
  • Vehicle OEMs are the economic buyers, even when they do not take physical metal. Gasoline-heavy markets (United States, China, Japan) matter more for palladium than for platinum.
  • Electronics manufacturers in Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, China and the US use palladium plating and MLCCs. CSIS notes that the United States produces only about 5% of global palladium (Montana, owned by Sibanye-Stillwater) and relies on imports for semiconductors.
  • Import geography: USGS (2021–24) sourced US palladium imports from South Africa 37% and Russia 36%, with Belgium and Canada as smaller hubs. After 2022, Western fabricators tried to reduce Russian metal; South African and recycled ounces filled part of the gap.

South African sales come from the same producers as platinum — Valterra, Implats, Sibanye-Stillwater, Northam — because palladium is a basket metal, not a separate mine product. Mogalakwena alone is often cited as ~6% of global palladium supply.

Production and consumption

In 2025 South Africa supplied 2.25 million oz of palladium (~70 tonnes), about 34% of primary mine supply. Russia supplied 2.95 Moz. Combined primary output was 6.59 Moz; recycling added 3.14 Moz, mostly from spent auto catalysts. Demand of 10.14 Moz left a 416,000 oz deficit.

USGS estimated 2025 South African palladium mine production at 70,000 kg and Russian at 84,000 kg. The Minerals Council's 2025 average palladium price was US$1,162/oz (+17%), still far below the 2021 peak. The metal ended 2025 around $1,600 after the Chinese GFEX futures launch. South African output was hit by the same Q1 2025 floods that cut platinum; SFA (Oxford) put South African palladium closer to 2.12 Moz on a mine-production basis in some 2025 estimates.

The future

Palladium is the PGM most exposed to the death of the gasoline engine. There is no jewellery franchise on platinum's scale, and hydrogen technologies prefer platinum and iridium. The bull case is that BEV adoption is slower than advertised, hybrids keep catalysts in the fleet for another decade, and recycling cannot ramp fast enough to replace declining mine supply in South Africa and North America.

The substitution risk is already visible: when palladium was expensive, fabricators put platinum into gasoline catalysts. That trade can run in reverse if palladium cheapens again. Electronics and semiconductors are a genuine second pillar — palladium plating is hard to design out — but they are an order of magnitude smaller than auto demand.

For South Africa, palladium will remain a basket by-product of platinum mining. Mogalakwena and Platreef matter because they are palladium-heavier and open-pittable, which is why Valterra's and Ivanhoe's northern limb assets are strategically important even if the country's overall PGM ounces keep falling. Geopolitics cuts the other way: Western efforts to diversify away from Russian palladium increase the option value of every Bushveld ounce, regardless of the EV transition.
