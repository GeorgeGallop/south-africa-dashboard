🔥 Richards Bay LNG-to-Power Project

South Africa's largest proposed gas-to-power project — a 3,000 MW combined-cycle gas turbine plant, an LNG import terminal at the Port of Richards Bay, and a gas pipeline network spanning KwaZulu-Natal. One of the most consequential and controversial energy infrastructure projects in the country's history.

📖 What Is the Richards Bay LNG-to-Power Project?

The Richards Bay LNG-to-Power Project is a flagship initiative under South Africa's Gas Master Plan and the country's broader Just Energy Transition. The project aims to establish a gas-to-power hub centred on the deep-water Port of Richards Bay in northern KwaZulu-Natal — South Africa's largest bulk cargo port and a strategic industrial zone.

The project comprises three integrated components:

  1. The LNG Import Terminal: A floating storage and regasification unit (FSRU) or land-based LNG terminal at the Port of Richards Bay to receive, store, and regasify liquefied natural gas imported by sea — primarily from Mozambique's Rovuma Basin, Nigeria, Qatar, or the global spot market.
  2. The Gas-to-Power Plant: A combined-cycle gas turbine (CCGT) power station with a target capacity of 3,000 MW — enough to supply approximately 5% of South Africa's peak demand. The plant would be built in phases (likely 1,000 MW × 3 phases) and would be South Africa's largest gas-fired power station by a significant margin. For comparison, the existing Ankerlig and Gourikwa OCGT peaking plants together total ~2,100 MW.
  3. The Gas Pipeline Network: A transmission pipeline system to distribute natural gas from Richards Bay to industrial users and other power plants across KwaZulu-Natal and potentially into Gauteng, linking to the existing Rompco pipeline (which brings gas from Mozambique's Pande and Temane fields to Secunda).

⏳ Timeline & History

2010–2015 — Early Conception

The Richards Bay gas-to-power concept emerged from South Africa's growing recognition that its coal-heavy grid needed diversification. Eskom's Medupi and Kusile projects were already delayed and over-budget. Gas was identified in the IRP 2010 as a potential mid-merit fuel source. Initial feasibility studies for a Richards Bay LNG terminal were conducted by Transnet and private sector partners.

2016 — Gas-to-Power IPP Programme Announced

The Department of Energy (now DMRE) announced a Gas-to-Power Independent Power Producer (IPP) Procurement Programme, targeting 3,126 MW of gas-fired generation. Richards Bay was identified as the primary location. The programme was structured similarly to the highly successful Renewable Energy IPP Programme (REIPPPP). An initial Request for Information (RFI) was issued in 2016, with 23 responses received.

2016–2018 — Bid Preparation & Grid Connection

Multiple international consortia formed to bid for the project. Eskom confirmed that the Richards Bay area could accommodate 3,000 MW of new generation capacity into the transmission grid, with upgrades planned to the Empangeni–Athena 400 kV corridor. Environmental Impact Assessments (EIAs) were initiated.

2019–2021 — Stalled / Delayed

The programme stalled. The IRP 2019 reduced the allocation for gas-to-power from earlier projections, refocusing on renewables and battery storage. Political instability, the Zuma-era state capture crisis, and Eskom's financial collapse created uncertainty for gas infrastructure investment. The COVID-19 pandemic further delayed regulatory processes. The Gas Amendment Bill and Gas Master Plan development slowed.

2022–2024 — Revived Under Energy Crisis

As South Africa descended into Stage 6 load shedding (and briefly Stage 8), gas-to-power was revived as a crisis response. The DMRE, under Minister Gwede Mantashe (and later Kgosientsho Ramokgopa as Electricity Minister), prioritised gas as a "transition fuel" — cleaner than coal, more reliable than intermittent renewables, and faster to deploy than nuclear. The Gas Master Plan (released for public comment in 2024) explicitly identifies Richards Bay as the cornerstone of South Africa's gas infrastructure. The IRP 2023/2024 draft increased gas allocations. The project is now in active procurement preparation.

2025–2026 — Expected RFP Release

The DMRE is expected to release the formal Request for Proposals (RFP) for the Richards Bay gas-to-power programme. This will trigger binding bids from consortia and final investment decisions. First gas imports are targeted for 2029–2030, with Phase 1 (1,000 MW) coming online by 2030–2032 and full 3,000 MW capacity by approximately 2035.

🏢 Who Is Involved — Key Players

Government & State Entities
  • Department of Mineral Resources and Energy (DMRE): Lead government department. Responsible for the Gas-to-Power IPP Programme, the Gas Master Plan, and licensing.
  • Eskom: The offtaker — Eskom will sign the Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) to buy electricity from the gas plant. Eskom is also responsible for transmission grid upgrades in the Richards Bay–Empangeni area.
  • Transnet National Ports Authority (TNPA): Owner and operator of the Port of Richards Bay. The LNG terminal requires port infrastructure — berthing facilities, dredging, breakwaters, and jetty infrastructure — that fall under TNPA's jurisdiction.
  • Transnet Pipelines (TPL): Potential operator or partner for the gas pipeline network connecting Richards Bay to industrial users.
  • NERSA: The energy regulator will approve tariffs, licences, and the PPA framework.
  • iGas / CEF (Central Energy Fund): State-owned gas development company. Involved in gas infrastructure planning and potential state equity participation in the project.
  • Industrial Development Corporation (IDC): Potential development financier for local content and BEE components.
Private Sector & International Players
  • TotalEnergies: The French energy major has expressed strong interest in the South African gas market. TotalEnergies is already active in Mozambique's Rovuma LNG project and could supply LNG to Richards Bay while also participating in the power plant and terminal.
  • Shell: Shell has a long-standing interest in South African gas. Was involved in early feasibility studies. Shell's global LNG trading capacity makes it a natural partner for the import terminal.
  • Sasol: South Africa's largest gas consumer (via the Rompco pipeline from Mozambique). Sasol could be an anchor customer for LNG imports as its Mozambican gas fields (Pande and Temane) decline. Sasol's existing gas infrastructure and industrial customer base in Secunda make it a critical stakeholder.
  • Eskom Holdings / NTCSA: The newly established National Transmission Company of South Africa could take a role in grid infrastructure and potentially as a co-investor.
  • Global LNG Suppliers: QatarEnergy, ENI (Italy), BP, and ExxonMobil are potential LNG suppliers for the terminal, competing with TotalEnergies and Shell.
  • Engineering & Construction: Siemens Energy, GE Vernova, Mitsubishi Power — the three global heavy-duty gas turbine manufacturers — are expected to bid for the power plant construction. KBR, Fluor, and other engineering firms are expected to bid for the terminal and pipeline EPC contracts.
  • South African IPP Consortia: Local developers including Peakers Energy, African Infrastructure Investment Managers (AIIM), and various BEE consortia are expected to bid.

🔧 The Technology — FSRU, CCGT & Pipeline Infrastructure

Floating Storage and Regasification Unit (FSRU)

The most likely configuration for the LNG import terminal is an FSRU — a specialised ship permanently moored at the Port of Richards Bay. LNG carriers offload their cargo to the FSRU for storage (typically 170,000–260,000 m³ of LNG capacity). The FSRU regasifies the LNG — converting it from liquid (-162°C) to gaseous form — and sends it to shore via a subsea or jetty pipeline. FSRUs are faster to deploy (2–3 years) and cheaper than land-based terminals. An alternative is a land-based LNG terminal with storage tanks — more expensive and slower to build (4–6 years) but with larger capacity and longer asset life. The final choice between FSRU and land-based terminal is a key decision point in the RFP.

Combined-Cycle Gas Turbine (CCGT) Power Plant

The power plant will use CCGT technology, which combines a gas turbine (Brayton cycle) with a steam turbine (Rankine cycle). Exhaust heat from the gas turbine generates steam to drive a second turbine — achieving thermal efficiencies of 58–63%, compared to ~35% for Eskom's coal fleet and ~33% for simple-cycle diesel OCGTs. The plant will use advanced-class gas turbines — likely GE's 9HA.02, Siemens' SGT5-9000HL, or Mitsubishi's M501JAC — each rated at 500–600 MW per unit. A 3,000 MW CCGT plant would consist of approximately 5–6 gas turbine units in a multi-shaft configuration, with a design life of 30+ years. The plant can operate flexibly — ramping up and down to complement variable renewable energy — and can achieve start-up times of 30–60 minutes from cold start.

Pipeline Network

The gas will be transported from Richards Bay via a high-pressure transmission pipeline (likely 600–900 mm diameter, operating at 80–100 bar). The pipeline would run from Richards Bay inland to the Empangeni–Durban corridor, potentially connecting to the Sasol gas network near Secunda (linking to the existing Rompco pipeline). Total pipeline length is estimated at 400–600 km, with compressor stations every 150–200 km. The pipeline would also supply industrial gas users — manufacturers, chemical plants, and potentially gas-fired peaking plants along the route. Transnet Pipelines or a private consortium would build and operate the pipeline under a regulated tariff structure.

Revenue Model

The power plant will operate under a Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) with Eskom, likely structured similarly to the REIPPPP model — a 20-year contract with a tariff that covers capital recovery, fuel costs (LNG), operations and maintenance, and a return on investment. The PPA structure for gas is more complex than for renewables because the fuel is a variable cost (LNG prices fluctuate on global markets). The PPA will need a fuel pass-through mechanism — the tariff adjusts based on the actual cost of LNG purchased. This shifts fuel price risk to Eskom and ultimately to electricity consumers through tariffs.

LNG Supply & Price Risk

LNG is a globally traded commodity. When international LNG prices spike (as they did in 2022 after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, when Asian spot LNG hit $70/MMBTU), the cost of generating electricity from the Richards Bay CCGT would skyrocket. The project's viability depends on securing long-term LNG supply contracts at stable prices. Mozambique's Rovuma Basin (operated by TotalEnergies, with reserves of 65+ trillion cubic feet) is the most attractive supply source due to proximity (reduced shipping costs). However, the Rovuma LNG project has been repeatedly delayed due to security concerns in Cabo Delgado province (Islamic State insurgency). Nigerian LNG, Qatari LNG, and U.S. Gulf Coast LNG are alternative sources but involve higher shipping costs and exposure to volatile global spot prices.

Development Finance Institutions (DFIs)

Given the scale of the project and South Africa's sovereign credit rating challenges, development finance institutions are expected to play a critical role in financing. Key DFIs engaged:

  • World Bank / IFC: Potential partial risk guarantees for the PPA and debt financing for the power plant.
  • African Development Bank (AfDB): Infrastructure financing, particularly for the port and pipeline components. AfDB has prioritised gas as a "transition fuel" for Africa.
  • Development Bank of Southern Africa (DBSA): South Africa's state-owned infrastructure bank. Expected to be a major local financier.
  • New Development Bank (BRICS Bank): South Africa's BRICS membership provides access to NDB financing, which has funded Eskom projects previously.
  • Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP): The $8.5 billion climate finance package pledged by the US, UK, EU, France, and Germany could potentially support gas-to-power as a "transition" technology — though this is politically contested (European partners are reluctant to fund fossil gas).
⚠️ The stranded asset risk: A 3,000 MW gas plant with a 30-year design life would operate until approximately 2065 — well beyond South Africa's 2050 net-zero target (if adopted). As global climate policy tightens and renewable + battery storage costs continue their rapid decline, the Richards Bay CCGT plant risks becoming a stranded asset — an expensive piece of fossil fuel infrastructure that cannot recover its investment before being forced to close or operate at a loss. This is the central tension of gas-to-power in developing countries: it addresses an immediate energy crisis while potentially locking in decades of fossil fuel dependence and carbon emissions. The project's financiers are acutely aware of this risk and are structuring financing to recover investment within the first 15–20 years of the PPA — after which the plant's continued operation is uncertain.

🔍 What the Richards Bay Project Means for South Africa

✅ Grid Stability

A 3,000 MW CCGT plant provides dispatchable, flexible baseload power — capable of running 24/7 or ramping to follow demand. Unlike renewables (intermittent) and coal (slow to ramp), gas can respond to grid frequency changes within seconds. For a grid plagued by load shedding and plant breakdowns, gas provides the reliability that batteries and solar cannot yet deliver at scale. It is the most practical short-to-medium-term solution to South Africa's generation capacity deficit.

⚠️ Carbon Lock-In

A 3,000 MW gas plant operating at 60% capacity factor would emit approximately 10–12 million tonnes of CO₂ per year — equivalent to adding roughly 2.5 million cars to South Africa's roads. South Africa is already the world's 14th-largest carbon emitter (primarily from coal). Adding large-scale gas generation complicates the country's climate commitments under the Paris Agreement and could jeopardise access to green financing and carbon border adjustment mechanisms (CBAM) from the EU — South Africa's largest trading partner.

🌍 Geopolitics

The project ties South Africa's energy security to global LNG markets and Mozambican stability. If Mozambique's Rovuma LNG comes online, South Africa gains a secure, nearby gas supply — but becomes dependent on a neighbour with an active insurgency. If the project relies on global spot LNG, South Africa's electricity prices become linked to Asian and European gas markets — a vulnerability painfully demonstrated in 2022. Energy independence through gas is possible; energy independence through imported gas is not.

🏭 Industrialisation

Gas is not just about electricity. Natural gas is a feedstock for industrial processes — chemicals, fertilisers, steel, aluminium, glass, ceramics. A reliable gas supply at Richards Bay could anchor an industrial cluster in northern KwaZulu-Natal — creating jobs, attracting foreign investment, and reversing the deindustrialisation that has hollowed out South Africa's manufacturing base. The gas pipeline network would extend this benefit along the entire route. This is the strongest argument for the project: it enables economic development beyond the power sector.

🏘️ Local Impact — Richards Bay & Empangeni

Richards Bay is already a major industrial centre — home to the world's largest coal export terminal (Richards Bay Coal Terminal), two aluminium smelters (Hillside and Bayside, operated by South32), a fertiliser plant (Foskor), and heavy mineral processing (Richards Bay Minerals). The LNG project would reinforce this industrial concentration, creating thousands of construction jobs and hundreds of permanent operational jobs. But it also raises environmental justice concerns: the surrounding communities already suffer from some of the worst air quality in South Africa, and adding a gas plant and LNG terminal would increase emissions, noise, and industrial risk.

🌱 The Transition Paradox

The Richards Bay project embodies the central paradox of the energy transition in developing countries: the countries that have contributed least to climate change are being asked to forgo the cheapest, most reliable energy sources — while the countries that caused the problem (Europe, North America) built their wealth on fossil fuels and are now expanding renewables from a position of energy abundance. South Africa needs power today — not in 2035 when renewables and storage may be sufficient. Gas, for all its flaws, is the fastest, most reliable way to close the generation gap. The question is whether South Africa can use gas as a bridge — or whether the bridge becomes a destination.
