🏪 Informal Market

The uncounted shop: spazas, hawkers, taxi-rank stalls and township wholesale. It is how most South Africans buy food, airtime and paraffin — and it is the door almost everyone who cannot get a formal job tries to walk through.

How big it actually is

Stats SA’s Quarterly Labour Force Survey is the cleanest official count. The informal sector — people running or working in unregistered businesses, including own-account traders — has sat around 3.0–3.3 million in recent years, roughly 18–20% of total employment. That is not a rounding error. It is a labour market the size of a mid-sized European country, sitting next to a formal economy that cannot absorb the working-age population.

GDP is harder. Stats SA’s national accounts put the informal sector at about 5–6% of GDP. Academic and industry estimates that try to capture unrecorded cash trade, stokvels and under-invoiced wholesale often land higher — commonly 8–15%. The gap is the point: a large share of township and inner-city retail never hits a till that SARS or Stats SA can see. The QLFS counts the people. The SATS undercounts the money.

Retail is the visible core of that informal economy. Estimates of spaza shops run from about 100,000 to 140,000 outlets, depending on whether you count a fridge and a counter in a front room. Add street hawkers, taxi-rank tables, flea-market stalls, shebeens, backyard salons, mashonisas and airtime/electricity kiosks and you are looking at hundreds of thousands of micro-enterprises. Stokvels — rotating savings clubs — involve on the order of 11 million members and tens of billions of rand a year. A large slice of that money is spent in exactly these shops, often in bulk at month-end.

Geography matters. Informal retail is densest where formal retail was historically thin: townships, informal settlements, rural nodes, CBD pavements (Johannesburg, Durban, Cape Town, Gqeberha) and taxi ranks. The wholesale belt that feeds it is equally specific — Fordsburg, the Oriental Plaza, China City / China Mall clusters, Mai Mai, Faraday, Durban’s Warwick Junction, Jumbo Cash & Carry, Trade Centre, Makro. A Somali- or Ethiopian-run spaza in Soweto or Diepsloot is typically stocked from those cash-and-carries, not from a Shoprite DC.

Who is already in it

South African-owned spazas and hawkers are the original layer — often a household business, thin margins, long hours, stocked from the nearest cash-and-carry. Many are survivalist: the shop exists because there is no wage job, not because a business plan said so.

Migrant traders — Somali, Ethiopian, Bangladeshi, Pakistani, Nigerian and Chinese networks — have taken a large and politically explosive share of township grocery retail since the 1990s. They typically run tighter stock turns, longer hours, lower mark-ups and pooled buying. That efficiency is why they won share. It is also why they have been the target of xenophobic attacks, including during the July 2021 unrest, and of campaigns such as Operation Dudula. The competition is real; so is the violence. Any serious map of informal retail has to hold both facts at once.

Street traders occupy the pavement economy: fruit and vegetables, cooked food, clothes, cosmetics, cigarettes, airtime. Municipal by-laws (permits, restricted zones, confiscation) are the daily regulator. Inner-city markets — Warwick, the Johannesburg CBD, Bellville, Marabastad — are as important as any mall for fresh produce into poor households.

On the formal side looking in: Shoprite’s Usave and Boxer (Pick n Pay’s value banner) are explicit spaza competitors. Township malls (Maponya Mall, Jabulani, Mall of Thembisa, Chris Hani Crossing, and a long list of smaller centres) were built to capture the same wallet. PEP and Ackermans already live at that price point for clothing. Flash, Kazang, Pay@ and Blue Label put electricity, airtime and bill payments into spaza tills — the informal shop as a payments node, not just a grocery shelf. Yoco, PayShap, Capitec and the banks’ township offerings are trying to put a digital rail under cash businesses that still think in notes.

How people are trying to get involved

Because formal unemployment is stuck in the 30s, the informal market is the default entry point for millions of South Africans who will never see a payroll. The routes in are practical, not theoretical.

Start a stall or a spaza. Lowest capital path: a table at a taxi rank or pavement, stock from Jumbo or a China mall, cash only. Next step: a container or a converted garage, a fridge, a prepaid-electricity meter, a Flash terminal. Working capital, not a bank loan, is the constraint. Family, stokvels and mashonisas (informal lenders) still finance more of this than the banks do.

Buying groups and cooperatives. Spaza owners who cannot match Somali/Ethiopian bulk-buying have tried to form buying groups to get cash-and-carry prices. Some work. Many collapse on trust, transport and who holds the money. The logic is sound; execution is the hard part.

Wholesale and distribution. This is where more serious capital goes. Supplying spazas — branded FMCG, bread, cold drinks, paraffin, airtime — is a business with scale. Listed and unlisted wholesalers, independent distributors and the “bakkie brigade” all sit on this layer. If you want exposure to informal retail without standing behind a counter, you usually enter here.

Township malls and value banners. Developers, REITs and the listed grocers keep opening smaller-format stores and neighbourhood centres aimed at LSM 3–6. The bet is that as incomes rise a fraction, or as crime and stock-outs hit spazas, the shopper walks into Boxer. It works at the margin. It has not killed the spaza.

Payments and fintech. Flash, Kazang, PayShap QR, Capitec Business, Yoco and similar products are the cleanest “get involved” play for people who are not traders: put rails under the cash economy, take a fee, stay out of the fridge. This is also how SARS and the banks slowly pull informal turnover onto a ledger.

State programmes. The Department of Small Business Development’s Township and Rural Entrepreneurship Programme (TREP), SEDA, NYDA and various provincial township-economy strategies offer grants, stock, training and sometimes premises. Uptake is real; conversion into durable businesses is mixed. Municipal informal-trader policies (bays, markets, permits) matter more day-to-day than a national grant. CIPC registration, a SARS tax number and a health certificate are the paperwork wall between a pavement stall and a shop that can take a supplier account.

Franchise and “formalise the spaza” models. Periodically a supermarket group, a bank or a development agency announces a branded spaza franchise: uniform stock, credit, a till. A few cohorts get stood up. Scaling has repeatedly stalled on working capital, crime, and the fact that the independent owner’s edge is flexibility, not a planogram.

What actually constrains it

Crime — robbery, extortion, and periodic xenophobic violence — is the operating environment, not a side risk. Electricity (load-shedding, illegal connections, prepaid meters) decides whether the fridge runs. By-laws and confiscation decide whether the pavement trader is still there on Tuesday. Working capital decides whether the shop has bread on the 25th. Foreign-trader politics is a live national issue: local owners, civic groups and some politicians want the spaza layer “returned”; migrant networks and the Constitution sit on the other side. Policy that pretends the informal market is a tidy SMME pipeline, rather than a contested cash economy, will keep missing it.

The size is not in dispute: millions of jobs, a GDP slice Stats SA can only partly see, and the primary food-and-airtime channel for poor households. The fight is over who gets to stand in it — unemployed South Africans trying to enter, migrant networks already inside, listed retailers trying to pull the wallet into a mall, and the state trying to register, tax and “develop” something that works precisely because it is hard to see.
