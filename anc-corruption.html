💀 The Corruption of the ANC: How It All Went Wrong

A movement that liberated a nation from apartheid became, within a single generation, the vehicle for the largest looting of public resources in South African history. This is the inside story of how it happened — the people, the schemes, the narrative shifts, and the slow surrender of principle to power.

🎓 How It All Went Wrong — The Anatomy of Institutionalised Theft
📚 Former ANC MP, speaking anonymously to the Daily Maverick, 2020:
“It didn't happen overnight. It was gradual. First, we told ourselves we deserved the good life — we had suffered under apartheid. Then it was small things: a car, a travel allowance, a tender for a cousin. Then the numbers got bigger. By the time Zuma came, the party was already hollowed out. He just accelerated what was already there. The money was too good, the accountability too weak. Nobody wanted to be the one to stop the party.”

The corruption of the African National Congress is not a story of a few bad apples. It is the story of how an entire liberation movement was systematically captured by self-interest, how its institutions were repurposed from serving the public to serving a political elite, and how a culture of impunity was deliberately cultivated over three decades. The mechanisms were specific, the actors were identifiable, and the consequences are measurable in trillions of rand, hundreds of thousands of preventable deaths, and a nation that went from hope to despair.

This page traces that trajectory through six phases — each building on the last, each making the next inevitable. It names names. It explains exactly how the money moved. And it confronts the uncomfortable question: how did the party of Mandela become the party of tender fraud, stolen pensions, murdered whistleblowers, and gangsters in cabinet?

① Phase 1: The Original Sin — Cadre Deployment (1994–1999)
📚 Thabo Mbeki, ANC National Executive Committee meeting, 1997:
“The ANC must deploy its cadres to all centres of power. The state must be transformed to serve the objectives of the national democratic revolution. There is no such thing as a neutral civil service.”

The corruption of the ANC begins not with money but with a philosophy: cadre deployment. The idea that the party, not the constitution, should decide who runs the country. Under Mandela, the Government of National Unity included a "sunset clause" — a guarantee that apartheid-era civil servants would keep their jobs for five years. This was a necessary compromise to ensure a peaceful transition. But the ANC's leadership saw it as a temporary obstacle.

The Mechanics of Cadre Deployment

The ANC established a Deployment Committee chaired by the party's Secretary-General. Its mandate: vet and place ANC loyalists into every position of power in the state. This was not hidden — it was official party policy, formalised in ANC conference resolutions. The committee maintained lists of "deployable comrades" and matched them to positions: Directors-General of government departments, CEOs of state-owned enterprises, municipal managers, ambassadors, board members of SOEs, even university vice-chancellors.

The criteria for deployment were never competence, qualifications, or experience. They were: loyalty to the ANC, factional alignment, and willingness to follow instructions from Luthuli House (ANC headquarters). Competence was, at best, a secondary consideration. At worst, competence was seen as a threat — a competent, independent-minded official might resist political instructions.

📍 How Cadre Deployment Enabled Corruption: The Cycle
  1. Step 1: ANC deploys a loyalist as municipal manager or SOE CEO.
  2. Step 2: That loyalist controls procurement — the awarding of government contracts worth millions or billions.
  3. Step 3: The loyalist awards contracts to companies owned by ANC-connected figures — often with inflated prices.
  4. Step 4: A percentage of the contract value flows back to the ANC as "donations" or to individual politicians as kickbacks.
  5. Step 5: Anyone who objects is removed and replaced. Whistleblowers are isolated, fired, or worse.
  6. Step 6: The ANC's deployment committee protects those involved from accountability.

By the late 1990s, the principle was embedded: the state was not a neutral institution serving all citizens. It was an instrument of the ruling party. And the ruling party saw state resources as its own. The distinction between party funds and public funds had been deliberately erased. Every scandal that followed — every stolen tender, every looted SOE, every municipality bankrupted by corruption — traces back to this original decision.

🗣 How the Narrative Shifted — "Transformation" as Cover

The ANC framed cadre deployment as "transformation" — the necessary replacement of apartheid-era officials with representatives of the previously oppressed majority. This framing was powerful because it contained a kernel of truth: the civil service was dominated by white apartheid-era appointees, and it did need to change. But the ANC never replaced them with qualified black professionals through an open, merit-based process. It replaced them with party loyalists through a closed, political process. The "transformation" narrative was used to delegitimise any criticism: if you opposed a particular appointment, you were opposing transformation itself. This rhetorical trap silenced internal dissent and external scrutiny for over two decades.

Judicial reckoning: In 2023, the Gauteng High Court — in a case brought by the DA, the Helen Suzman Foundation, and others — declared the ANC's cadre deployment policy unlawful and unconstitutional. Judge Brendan Wepener found that the policy violated the Constitution by subordinating the public service to party interests. The ANC was ordered to hand over its deployment committee records — documents that revealed decades of political vetting of civil service appointments. The ANC appealed. The policy remains in practice despite the ruling.

② Phase 2: The Arms Deal — The Original Sin of ANC Corruption (1999)
📚 Judge Willie Seriti, Arms Deal Commission of Inquiry (2016):
“The commission finds that there was no evidence of corruption in the Strategic Defence Procurement Package.”

📚 Judge President Dunstan Mlambo, setting aside the Seriti findings (2019):
“The Seriti Commission was a whitewash. It ignored evidence of bribery, suppressed testimony that implicated senior ANC officials, and was designed from the start to protect the political elite. The findings are irrational and must be set aside.”

In 1999, the ANC government signed a R30 billion arms procurement deal — the Strategic Defence Procurement Package — to buy corvettes, submarines, helicopters, and fighter jets from European defence contractors. It was the largest single procurement in South African history. It was also the single most corrupt transaction in post-apartheid South Africa — and it established the template for everything that followed.

What Happened

Multiple European defence companies — including ThyssenKrupp (Germany), BAE Systems (UK), Saab (Sweden), and Thales (France) — competed for contracts worth billions. The winners secured their deals through bribery. The details, uncovered over two decades by investigators, journalists, and whistleblowers, are staggering:

  • Schabir Shaik and Jacob Zuma: Shaik, a Durban businessman with close ties to the ANC, acted as Zuma's "financial advisor." Shaik's company, Nkobi Holdings, received millions in "consulting fees" from Thales (then Thomson-CSF), the French arms company that won the contract for the Navy's four corvettes. In exchange, Shaik facilitated meetings between Thales executives and Zuma, who was then KwaZulu-Natal's MEC for Economic Development — and who later, as Deputy President, protected Thales from investigation. Shaik was convicted of corruption and fraud in 2005. The court found that Zuma and Shaik had a "generally corrupt relationship." [SA History]
  • Tony Yengeni: The ANC's Chief Whip and former chair of the Parliamentary Defence Committee, Yengeni received a 47% discount on a luxury Mercedes-Benz from DaimlerChrysler Aerospace — a subsidiary of one of the arms deal bidders. He was convicted of fraud in 2003 and served four months of a four-year sentence before being released on parole. Yengeni later returned to the ANC's National Executive Committee — a convicted fraudster sitting on the party's highest decision-making body. [SA History]
  • Joe Modise: The former MK commander who became Minister of Defence under Mandela, Modise personally negotiated the arms deal. After leaving government, he became a director of Conlog, a defence contractor that received a R300 million sub-contract from the arms deal. Modise died a wealthy man. Investigative journalists traced payments from arms companies to Modise-controlled entities. [News24]
  • The British BAE Systems Bribes: BAE Systems admitted paying approximately £100 million in "commissions" to secure the Hawk and Gripen fighter contracts. The UK's Serious Fraud Office investigated but was shut down by the Blair government in 2006 on "national security" grounds. In 2010, BAE settled with UK and US authorities for $450 million — one of the largest corporate corruption settlements in history. South Africa never recovered the money. [The Guardian]
📍 The Arms Deal Template — How It Became the Model

The arms deal established the corruption playbook that the ANC would use for the next 25 years: (1) a massive government procurement programme; (2) a politically-connected "advisor" or middleman who facilitates the deal; (3) inflated contract values to cover the cost of bribes; (4) "consulting fees" and "success fees" paid to ANC-connected entities; (5) destruction or suppression of evidence when investigators got close; (6) commissions of inquiry that mysteriously found no wrongdoing; (7) the rehabilitation of those caught — Yengeni, Shaik, and Zuma all returned to political prominence. The message was clear: crime pays in the ANC, and the party protects its own.

The Narrative Shift: "Political Conspiracy"

When the arms deal corruption allegations first surfaced, the ANC's response established the defensive playbook it would use for decades. The allegations were dismissed as a "political conspiracy" — a smear campaign by "counter-revolutionary forces" (the media, the DA, foreign intelligence agencies) to discredit the liberation movement. Zuma's supporters coined the phrase "spy tapes" — secret recordings that allegedly showed the NPA's case against Zuma was politically motivated. The tapes, when finally released, showed no such thing. But the narrative worked: millions of ANC supporters believed Zuma was the victim, not the beneficiary, of the arms deal corruption. This technique — delegitimising the institution rather than defending against the evidence — would be perfected over the following decades.

③ Phase 3: The Tenderpreneur Economy — How the System Was Industrialised (2000s–2010s)
📚 Moeletsi Mbeki, Architects of Poverty (2009):
“The ANC has created a new class — the tenderpreneurs. These are not entrepreneurs in any meaningful sense. They do not create products, innovate, or compete in markets. They simply capture government contracts through political connections, deliver shoddy work or nothing at all, and pocket the profits. They are a parasite class, feeding on the state, and they are the ANC's most loyal constituency because the ANC is the source of their wealth.”

The term "tenderpreneur" was coined to describe a specific phenomenon: ANC-connected individuals who built businesses — not through innovation or competition — but through capturing government tenders. By the 2010s, government procurement in South Africa exceeded R500 billion annually. At every level — national, provincial, municipal — this money flowed through a system designed not for value but for extraction.

How Tender Fraud Works — The Standard Playbook
📍 Scheme Type 1: The Front Company

A senior ANC official's relative or associate registers a company. The company has no track record, no equipment, and no expertise. It bids for a government tender — to supply goods, build infrastructure, or provide services. The tender specifications are written to match exactly what this company can offer (because the official wrote the specs or influenced the procurement committee). The company wins the tender at an inflated price. Subcontractors do the actual work at a fraction of the cost. The difference — often 40-60% of the contract value — is profit, shared between the company owner and the ANC official.

📍 Scheme Type 2: The Emergency Procurement Rush

A crisis is manufactured or exploited — COVID-19 was a goldmine for this. Emergency procurement regulations are invoked, bypassing normal tender procedures. Connected companies are awarded no-bid contracts at massively inflated prices. During COVID-19 alone, the Special Investigating Unit (SIU) identified over R500 million in irregular PPE contracts. The ANC's then-spokesperson, Pule Mabe, was implicated in a R27 million PPE tender. Mabe was charged with corruption — but the case, like so many, has proceeded at a glacial pace. [SIU]

📍 Scheme Type 3: The Politician as "Consultant"

A sitting ANC MP, MEC, or mayor — or their spouse — is hired as a "consultant" by a company bidding for government contracts. No actual consulting work is performed. The consulting fee is simply a bribe, structured to look legitimate. The company wins the contract. The politician's salary as a public representative is supplemented by millions in "consulting fees." The lines between public office and private enrichment are deliberately erased.

📍 Scheme Type 4: The Delivery that Never Comes

A company wins a tender for housing construction, road building, or service delivery. The company is paid upfront or in phases. The work is never completed, is done to a substandard level, or exists only on paper. The municipal or departmental officials who should inspect the work are the same ones who awarded the tender — they have no incentive to report failures. The company dissolves, re-registers under a new name, and bids for new tenders. This is why South Africa has thousands of unfinished RDP houses, collapsed roads, and municipalities with audit disclaimers year after year.

The Auditor-General has repeatedly reported that irregular expenditure — money spent in violation of procurement laws — runs into tens of billions annually. But "irregular expenditure" is a bureaucratic term. What it actually means: money stolen through rigged tenders, awarded to connected companies, for work never done, with no consequences for those responsible. The AGSA can report the numbers. It cannot prosecute. That is the NPA's job. And the NPA, for most of the ANC's rule, was led by people appointed by the ANC.

④ Phase 4: State Capture — The Gupta Project (2009–2018)
📚 Chief Justice Raymond Zondo, State Capture Commission Final Report, Volume 1 (2022):
“The evidence shows that the Gupta family, in association with former President Jacob Zuma and other senior ANC officials, was at the centre of a scheme to systematically capture and repurpose state institutions for private enrichment. The scale of the looting is without precedent in South African history. Eskom, Transnet, Denel, SAA, PRASA — every major state-owned enterprise was compromised. The damage will take a generation to repair.”

State Capture was not a metaphor. It was a project — organised, systematic, and methodical. The Gupta family, who arrived in South Africa from India in 1993 and built a business empire spanning computers, mining, and media, did not stumble into political influence. They bought it. And the ANC — in particular Jacob Zuma and his faction — sold it.

How the Gupta Family Captured the State — The Mechanics

1. The Entry Point: Zuma's Son. Duduzane Zuma, Jacob Zuma's son, became a business partner of the Gupta brothers — Atul, Ajay, and Rajesh ("Tony") Gupta. Duduzane was given directorships in Gupta companies and a lavish lifestyle. In return, he provided the Guptas with access to his father — and through his father, to the entire South African state. The relationship between the Guptas and Zuma was not political; it was familial and financial. The Guptas paid for the Zuma family's expenses, including renovations to Zuma's Nkandla compound. [Zondo Commission]

2. Control of Appointments. The Guptas did not just influence who got government jobs — they directly selected them. The "Gupta Leaks" — a trove of over 200,000 emails leaked to investigative journalists in 2017 — revealed that the Guptas offered cabinet positions to ANC MPs from their Saxonwold compound in Johannesburg. Minister of Finance? The Guptas offered it to Mcebisi Jonas, who refused and later testified about the attempted bribe of R600 million. Minister of Public Enterprises? The Guptas selected Lynne Brown — and Eskom and Transnet, which fell under her department, became the primary vehicles for looting. Minister of Mineral Resources? The Guptas selected Mosebenzi Zwane, who promptly facilitated mining deals for Gupta-owned companies. [AmaBhungane / Gupta Leaks]

3. Capture of State-Owned Enterprises. Each SOE became a Gupta cash machine:

  • Eskom: Over R50 billion looted through inflated coal contracts. The Guptas owned Tegeta Exploration and Resources, a coal mining company. Eskom was forced to buy coal from Tegeta at prices far above market rates — sometimes double or triple what other suppliers charged. The quality was often substandard. Eskom's own procurement officials were sidelined or fired when they objected. The Guptas used Eskom's money to buy the Optimum Coal Mine from Glencore — essentially using Eskom's own capital to fund their acquisition. The man who facilitated this: Matshela Koko, Eskom's acting CEO, whose stepdaughter was a director of a Gupta-linked company. [Daily Maverick]
  • Transnet: Over R41 billion in irregular contracts. The Guptas, through their company Trillian Capital Partners (in association with global consultancy McKinsey), secured contracts to advise Transnet on procurement — and then used that position to direct contracts to Gupta-owned entities. The Transnet executives who facilitated this — Siyabonga Gama (CEO), Anoj Singh (CFO) — were personally enriched through kickbacks. The contracts involved the purchase of Chinese locomotives at inflated prices — locomotives that were too tall for South African rail lines and could not be used. [Zondo Commission]
  • Denel: The state arms manufacturer was steered into a joint venture with a Gupta-linked company, VR Laser, that stripped Denel of intellectual property and revenue. Denel, once a world-class defence manufacturer, was reduced to technical insolvency.
  • South African Airways (SAA): The national airline was forced into unprofitable routes and contracts with Gupta-linked entities. SAA's chairperson, Dudu Myeni — a Zuma loyalist with no aviation experience — ran the airline into the ground, blocking profitable deals with Emirates and steering contracts to connected entities. SAA required over R30 billion in bailouts and entered business rescue in 2019. [News24]
  • PRASA (Passenger Rail Agency): Gupta-linked companies secured contracts for locomotive procurement and signalling systems. The contracts were grossly inflated, much of the equipment was unusable, and PRASA's rail network — which millions of working-class South Africans depend on — was effectively destroyed. Lucky Montana, PRASA's CEO, oversaw this destruction. [Zondo Commission]

4. Control of Law Enforcement. The Guptas understood that to loot with impunity, they needed to neutralise law enforcement. Through Zuma, they secured the appointment of compromised officials to key positions:

  • SARS (Tax Authority): Commissioner Tom Moyane, appointed by Zuma in 2014, systematically dismantled SARS's investigative capacity. The "rogue unit" narrative — the false claim that SARS had established an illegal intelligence unit — was used to purge experienced investigators, including Johann van Loggerenberg and Ivan Pillay. SARS's ability to investigate tax evasion by the politically connected was crippled. Tax collection collapsed, costing the fiscus billions. Moyane was later removed and the Nugent Commission found he had caused "catastrophic" damage to SARS. [Nugent Commission]
  • NPA (National Prosecuting Authority): Successive National Directors of Public Prosecutions — Mxolisi Nxasana, Shaun Abrahams — were appointed and removed based on their willingness to protect Zuma and his allies. Nxasana was removed and paid a R17 million golden handshake to make way for Abrahams, who dropped charges against Zuma and declined to prosecute senior ANC figures. Abrahams was later found by the Constitutional Court to have been improperly appointed. [Constitutional Court]
  • SAPS (Police): Police commissioners were appointed for their loyalty to Zuma, not their competence. Investigations into ANC corruption were stalled or buried. Bheki Cele, appointed by Ramaphosa, has been accused of the same — focusing on populist policing while politically sensitive cases gather dust.
  • Hawks (DPCI): The elite investigative unit was repeatedly purged of investigators working on politically sensitive cases. Hawks head Berning Ntlemeza was found by the courts to have lied under oath and lacked integrity — yet Zuma refused to remove him for years. [Daily Maverick]
🗣 The Narrative Shift — "White Monopoly Capital" and "Radical Economic Transformation"

As state capture accelerated, the ANC — and particularly the Gupta-owned media empire (ANN7 news channel, The New Age newspaper) — developed a sophisticated narrative to deflect criticism. The concept of "White Monopoly Capital" (WMC) was weaponised. Any criticism of corruption was reframed as an attack by WMC on black economic empowerment. The Guptas positioned themselves as agents of "Radical Economic Transformation" — black businesspeople fighting against white economic domination. This was, of course, a complete inversion of reality: the Guptas were not empowering black South Africans; they were looting the state that was supposed to do so. But the narrative worked because it tapped into genuine grievances about persistent white economic dominance. The phrase "White Monopoly Capital" became a shield behind which looting continued. Even today, some ANC factions use this framing to dismiss corruption allegations as racist.

⑤ Phase 5: In Bed with Criminals — When the ANC Went Soft on Crime (2000s–Present)
📚 Paul O'Sullivan, forensic investigator and founder of Forensics for Justice, 2022:
“The criminal justice system in South Africa doesn't exist to prosecute the powerful. It exists to manage the perception that justice is being done. The ANC has systematically captured the police, the NPA, and the Hawks. If you're connected to the ruling party, you're above the law. It's that simple. I have handed over dossiers with bank records, phone records, and sworn affidavits implicating sitting ministers in corruption. Nothing happens. The files disappear. The witnesses are threatened. The investigators are transferred. This is not incompetence. This is design.”

One of the most devastating consequences of ANC corruption is the collapse of the criminal justice system's willingness — and ability — to prosecute the politically connected. This is not an accident. It is the direct result of the ANC placing its own people in control of law enforcement and ensuring they understand their real job: protect the party, not the law.

The Political Protection Racket — How It Works
📍 Mechanism 1: The Appointment of Loyalists to Law Enforcement

The NPA, the Hawks, and the SAPS are not independent. Their leadership is appointed by the President and the Minister of Police — both ANC positions. Throughout the Zuma era and continuing under Ramaphosa, these positions were filled by individuals whose primary qualification was loyalty to the ANC faction in power. When a politically connected figure is investigated, a phone call is made. The investigation slows. The docket is "lost." The investigator is transferred to a rural station. The prosecutor develops sudden doubts about the strength of the case. This is not speculation — it is documented in multiple court cases and the Zondo Commission.

📍 Mechanism 2: The Destruction of Investigative Capacity

SARS's capacity was deliberately destroyed under Tom Moyane. The Hawks' capacity was gutted under Berning Ntlemeza. The NPA's capacity was hollowed out under Shaun Abrahams. The Independent Police Investigative Directorate (IPID) — supposed to investigate police misconduct — has been starved of resources and headed by ANC loyalists who refuse to investigate political cases. Each time an institution got too close to prosecuting ANC corruption, its leadership was replaced and its investigators purged. The damage is cumulative: South Africa no longer has a functional capacity to investigate and prosecute complex financial crimes committed by the political elite.

📍 Mechanism 3: The Assassination of Whistleblowers

South Africa has become a dangerous country for people who expose corruption. The list of murdered whistleblowers is long and growing. Babalwa Njenje, a municipal whistleblower who exposed tender fraud in the Eastern Cape, was shot dead in her home in 2021. Cloete Murray, the liquidator investigating the Bosasa corruption scandal, was assassinated alongside his son in 2023. Tina Joemat-Pettersson, an ANC MP who was allegedly about to expose corruption, died under suspicious circumstances in 2023. The message to other potential whistleblowers is unambiguous: if you speak out, you die. And no one will be prosecuted for your murder. The police investigate. The dockets are opened. Nobody is convicted. Because the people who order these hits are connected to the people who control the police. [Corruption Watch]

📍 Mechanism 4: The "Consultancy" — Laundering Criminal Proceeds

South Africa has become a hub for international criminal networks, and ANC-connected figures are at the centre of this. The mechanism is simple: a criminal enterprise generates cash (drugs, illegal mining, human trafficking, stolen vehicles). The cash is funnelled through a "consulting" company owned by a politically-connected figure. The company invoices a government department for fictitious services. The department pays. The cash is now clean — laundered through the South African state. The ANC-connected figure takes a cut. The criminal enterprise has legitimised its money. This is not hypothetical. The Zondo Commission documented multiple instances. The Financial Intelligence Centre has flagged hundreds of suspicious transactions linked to politically exposed persons. But the NPA has prosecuted almost none of them. [FIC]

Specific Cases: The Politician-Criminal Nexus
Ace Magashule — The "Premier" of Corruption

Former ANC Secretary-General and Premier of the Free State. Magashule is accused of overseeing a R255 million asbestos roof removal contract — a contract that was meant to replace dangerous asbestos roofs on low-income houses in the Free State. The money was paid. The roofs were never replaced. People continued living under asbestos — a known carcinogen. The money was allegedly laundered through a network of companies linked to Magashule's associates, including Edwin Sodi, a businessman with close ties to the ANC. Magashule was arrested in 2020 and charged with corruption. He was released on bail. The trial has been repeatedly delayed. Magashule remains a free man and continues to exert influence in ANC politics. [News24]

Gwede Mantashe — The Untouchable

The ANC's National Chairperson and former Minister of Mineral Resources, Mantashe has been implicated in multiple corruption scandals. The Zondo Commission heard evidence that Mantashe — then ANC Secretary-General — personally protected the Guptas from internal ANC disciplinary action. Mantashe was also implicated in the Bosasa scandal: Bosasa, a facilities management company, paid for security upgrades at Mantashe's home. Bosasa's former COO, Angelo Agrizzi, testified that Bosasa effectively bribed senior ANC officials — including Mantashe — in exchange for government contracts. Despite this evidence, Mantashe has never been charged. He remains the ANC's National Chairperson and a sitting minister. When the ANC talks about "fighting corruption," Mantashe is in the room — a symbol of the party's complete loss of moral credibility. [Zondo Commission]

Malusi Gigaba — The Fall Guy Who Never Fell

Former Minister of Home Affairs and Minister of Finance. Gigaba was intimately involved in facilitating Gupta capture — particularly at Transnet and Eskom. He used his position as Minister of Public Enterprises to appoint Gupta-friendly boards to SOEs. He later, as Minister of Finance, was captured on video receiving, shall we say, personal favours from individuals whose names then appeared on government tenders. The Zondo Commission found that Gigaba lied under oath. Yet Gigaba has never been prosecuted. He resigned from cabinet in 2018 but remains an ANC member in good standing. The message: even being caught lying to a judicial commission does not disqualify you from ANC membership. [Daily Maverick]

Zandile Gumede — The Durban Model

Former Mayor of eThekwini (Durban), Gumede is facing charges of corruption, fraud, and money laundering related to a R320 million waste management tender. The scheme was classic tender fraud: contracts were awarded to connected companies at inflated prices. Work was not done. Money was siphoned. Gumede was arrested in 2019 but the trial has been delayed for years — partly because the NPA and the courts are under-resourced, partly because her legal team has exploited every procedural delay, and partly because she remains politically powerful within the ANC's KwaZulu-Natal structures. She was re-elected as an ANC councillor while out on bail. [News24]

The Consequences: A Society Without Consequences

When the political elite is seen to commit crimes with impunity, the social contract collapses. If a mayor can steal R320 million and remain in office, why should a police officer not take a R200 bribe? If a minister can lie to a judicial commission and remain a minister, why should a citizen tell the truth to a traffic officer? The ANC's corruption has not just stolen money; it has destroyed the norm of accountability. South Africa's high crime rate — its epidemic of violent crime, its culture of impunity — is not separate from political corruption. It is downstream from it. The signal from the top is clear: the law is for little people. The powerful operate in a different system. And millions of South Africans have absorbed this lesson and act accordingly.

⑥ Phase 6: The Narrative War — How the ANC Protected Its Corruption (2005–Present)
📚 ANC Statement on the State Capture Commission, 2022:
“The ANC has always been at the forefront of the fight against corruption. The State Capture Commission's findings, while serious, must not be used to delegitimise the ANC as an organisation. The ANC remains the only vehicle capable of delivering radical economic transformation to the people of South Africa.”

The ANC's corruption has been protected not just by institutional capture but by a sustained narrative campaign — a deliberate strategy to delegitimise critics, confuse the public, and maintain political support despite overwhelming evidence of criminality. This section traces the key narrative shifts the ANC has deployed to protect its corruption.

Narrative 1: "It Was Apartheid's Fault" (1994–2010)

The earliest and most durable narrative. Every governance failure — every collapsed municipality, every looted tender, every broken promise — was attributed to "the legacy of apartheid." And apartheid's legacy was, and is, real. But after 10, 20, 30 years, the question became: at what point does the responsibility shift from the architects of apartheid to those who have governed for three decades? The ANC never answered this. Instead, the "apartheid legacy" frame was stretched to cover everything: from Eskom load shedding (caused by decades of ANC looting, not 1994-era infrastructure) to failing schools (caused by the ANC's cadre deployment of unqualified teachers, not the Bantu Education Act).

Narrative 2: "Foreign/White/Imperialist Conspiracy" (2010–2018)

When the evidence of state capture became undeniable, the ANC shifted. The corruption was not corruption — it was a "political conspiracy." The Guptas were not looting the state — they were victims of "anti-black racism" by "White Monopoly Capital." The Zondo Commission was not an investigation — it was a "witch hunt." This narrative was amplified by the Gupta-owned ANN7 news channel and The New Age newspaper. It was repeated by ANC leaders. And it worked with a significant portion of the ANC's base, who already distrusted the media (historically white-owned), the judiciary (historically white), and the "establishment" (which they associated with apartheid).

Narrative 3: "We Are Fighting Corruption" (2018–Present)

The Ramaphosa era introduced a new narrative: the ANC is fighting corruption. Ramaphosa himself campaigned on this promise in 2017. The narrative claims that the ANC under Ramaphosa is "cleaning up" the party and the state. But the evidence tells a different story. Not one senior ANC politician has been convicted for state capture. The NPA has not prosecuted any of the figures named in the Zondo Commission's report — despite the commission handing over detailed evidence files. The ANC has not expelled senior members implicated in corruption — Ace Magashule was suspended for factional disloyalty, not for corruption; Gwede Mantashe remains National Chairperson; Zuma remains a member (until he formed his own party). The "we are fighting corruption" narrative is designed not to deliver accountability but to manage the perception of accountability — to give the public and international investors enough reassurance to prevent a complete collapse of confidence, while ensuring that the party's internal balance of power is not disrupted by actual prosecutions.

🗣 The Meta-Narrative: "Only the ANC Can Govern"

Underpinning all these specific narratives is the meta-narrative: only the ANC can govern South Africa. Any alternative — the DA, the EFF, a coalition — is presented as chaos, as a return to apartheid, as the destruction of the liberation dream. This is the ANC's ultimate protection: the argument that its corruption, however bad, is still better than the alternative. And this narrative has worked — for 30 years. It worked in 2024, when the ANC fell below 50% but remained the largest party, and the DA and IFP agreed to join a GNU rather than attempt to govern without it. The ANC's corruption is sustained not just by theft and violence but by a narrative that holds the country hostage: you have to accept us, corrupt and failing as we are, because the alternative is unthinkable. Until that narrative is broken — until South Africans can imagine a post-ANC future — the corruption will continue.

🔎 Where Are We Now? The Corruption Continues
📚 Paul Holden, author of The Zondo Commission: A Summary (2023):
“The Zondo Commission's final report runs to over 5,000 pages. It documents corruption in excruciating detail. It names the perpetrators. It recommends prosecutions. And nothing has happened. The NPA has not acted. The ANC has not disciplined its members. The looted money has not been recovered. The report sits on a shelf. The people it names remain in power, in Parliament, in the ANC's National Executive Committee. State capture has not ended. It has adapted. It no longer operates through a single family — it operates through networks of tenderpreneurs, through municipalities, through the same mechanisms of cadre deployment that the High Court declared unconstitutional. The faces change. The system endures.”

The 2024 election did not end ANC corruption. The Government of National Unity has not ended it. The Zondo Commission's report has not ended it. The system — cadre deployment, tender fraud, political interference in law enforcement, the narrative of impunity — remains intact. The ANC has not been reformed. It has merely been forced to share power. The party that liberated South Africa is also the party that has done more than any other to destroy its future. Understanding exactly how it happened — the phases, the schemes, the specific people, the narrative shifts — is the first step toward accountability. The second step — actually holding those responsible to account — has not yet been taken.

⚠ The Core Question

How did the party of Mandela become the party of tender fraud, murdered whistleblowers, and gangsters in cabinet? The answer is not mysterious. It happened through identifiable mechanisms — cadre deployment, the arms deal template, the tenderpreneur economy, state capture, the neutralisation of law enforcement, and a sustained narrative war. It happened through identifiable individuals — Zuma, Mbeki, Mantashe, Magashule, Gigaba, the Guptas, and dozens more. And it has not been stopped because the people who built the system still run it. The ANC is not a liberation movement that lost its way. It is an organisation that found a new way — the exploitation of state power for private enrichment — and has pursued it relentlessly for three decades. Until South Africans withdraw their consent from this system — at the ballot box and beyond — it will continue. The history of ANC corruption is not past. It is present. And its architects are still in the room.
