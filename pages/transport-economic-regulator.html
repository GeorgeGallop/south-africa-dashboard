⚖️ Transport Economic Regulator (TER)

The Independent Referee of South Africa's Rail, Port & Pipeline Reform

The Transport Economic Regulator is the new independent body created by the Economic Regulation of Transport Act (2024) to referee the opening of South Africa's freight logistics network. It is the statutory counterweight to the monopoly reform: if TRIM is the neutral manager of the rail network and the landlord port model opens terminals to private operators, the TER is the independent umpire that sets the pricing rules, guarantees fair access, and holds the infrastructure owners and operators accountable. Its defining feature is independence — from Transnet, from the Department of Transport, and from political control.

Created by: Economic Regulation of Transport Act (No. 6 of 2024)
Status: Being established / becoming operational
Accountable to: Parliament (not the Minister)
Key power: Sets maximum access tariffs
Covers: Rail, ports, pipelines, future essential infra

Why It's Needed — The Conflict of Interest It Fixes

For decades, Transnet owned the infrastructure and ran the trains and operated the terminals and set its own prices. This created an irreconcilable conflict of interest: the entity that controlled the network also decided who could use it and at what price. A mining company wanting to run its own trains, or a private terminal operator wanting port access, had to negotiate with the very company it was trying to compete against.

The TER solves this by placing the pricing and access decisions with an arms-length, expert body — not with the monopolist. The core principle: the entity that owns the infrastructure cannot also set the rules of access to it.

The structural fix: Under the old model, there was no independent referee — Transnet was effectively player, referee, and rule-maker simultaneously. The TER breaks that by making access decisions based on published, non-discriminatory rules, reviewable by an independent body rather than by Transnet management.

How It Will Be Independent

Independence is the TER's defining institutional feature — and it is engineered, not hoped for.

🏛️ Accountable to Parliament, not the Minister

The Act places the Regulator outside the Department of Transport's direct control. It is an independent statutory body accountable to Parliament — not to the Minister of Transport. This removes the ability of politicians or the regulated entity to lean on the regulator.

🔒 Security of tenure

Members are appointed through a transparent process with parliamentary oversight, have security of tenure, and can only be removed for cause. A member who rules against the Minister or against Transnet cannot simply be fired.

💵 Independent funding

Funded through a combination of parliamentary appropriation and industry levies — reducing dependence on any single funding source that could be used as leverage. It is not a subsidiary of Transnet or the Department.

📢 Public & transparent decisions

Decisions must be reasoned, published, and reviewable. The Regulator publishes annual reports on market performance, keeping pricing and access decisions in the open — the antidote to the opacity the Zondo Commission found enabled state capture.

What It Will Do — The Five Core Functions

The Act gives the Regulator five core functions, each backed by enforceable legal powers.

1. Sets Access Tariffs

Determines the maximum prices that Transnet (or any infrastructure owner) can charge for third-party access to rail tracks, ports, and terminals. Tariffs must be transparent, non-discriminatory and cost-reflective. This ends the practice of Transnet setting its own prices without external scrutiny.

Critical for private operators: they can build business cases knowing access charges are objectively set, not negotiated in a rigged game.

2. Enforces Open Access

Ensures any licensed operator who applies for train paths or terminal slots receives fair treatment. If the Infrastructure Manager denies access, the operator can appeal to the Regulator, which can compel access if the denial is anti-competitive or unjustified.

This is the sharp end of the reform — Transnet cannot quietly refuse slots to competitors and claim "operational constraints."

3. Investigates Anti-Competitive Conduct

Has investigatory powers comparable to the Competition Commission. Probes discriminatory pricing, refusal to supply, margin squeeze, cross-subsidisation, and other abuses of dominant position by Transnet or any market participant.

Covers both the infrastructure owner (Transnet) and future dominant private operators — the framework is forward-looking.

4. Imposes Remedies

Can impose structural and behavioural remedies: fines, mandatory access orders, price caps, accounting separation requirements, and divestiture recommendations. In extreme cases of non-compliance, it can refer matters for criminal prosecution.

Without enforcement teeth, regulation is theatre. This Act gives the Regulator a full set of remedies.

5. Monitors & Reports on Market Performance

Publishes annual reports on the state of competition in rail and ports: market shares, investment levels, service quality metrics, tariff trends, and barriers to entry. This creates transparency and public accountability — and gives investors the data they need to assess market opportunities.

Who Will Run It — Governance & Composition

Appointment process

Members are appointed through a transparent process with parliamentary oversight, designed to ensure the regulator is staffed by independent experts rather than political appointees. Appointments are in the hands of a process that Parliament scrutinises.

Expertise required

The Regulator is intended to be an expert, arms-length body — modelled on successful economic regulators in network industries worldwide: the UK's Office of Rail and Road, Australia's ACCC, and South Africa's own NERSA for electricity. Members would have regulatory, economic, legal and technical expertise.

Role relative to the market

The TER sits above the market players as the referee: it sets maximum tariffs (so TRIM cannot gouge), arbitrates disputes between TRIM and operators, and monitors conduct. It does not own infrastructure or run trains — it regulates.

From IRERC to the permanent TER

In the interim, the Interim Rail Economic Regulatory Capacity (IRERC) is serving as the temporary market-sounding and regulatory body that oversaw the draft Network Statement and tariff methodology. The permanent TER will replace it once fully established.

What It Will Enable — The Outcomes

The TER is the enabling institution that makes the whole reform credible and investable.

1. Genuine competition

By guaranteeing fair, non-discriminatory access and enforceable remedies, the TER makes the multi-operator model real. Private rail operators and terminal operators can enter on equal terms rather than relying on the incumbent's goodwill.

2. Private investment & investor certainty

Investors need to know access charges are objectively set and disputes will be fairly arbitrated — not subject to political or corporate whim. The TER provides that certainty, unlocking capital for rolling stock, terminals, and maintenance.

3. Road-to-rail modal shift

A credible, fairly-priced rail network pulls freight off the N3 and national roads — cutting the ~11–12% of GDP logistics cost, road damage, congestion and emissions, and restoring export competitiveness.

4. End to self-dealing

Denied applicants can appeal to the Regulator, not to Transnet. This is the anti-corruption and anti-discrimination guarantee — the opacity that enabled the Zondo Commission's documented state capture is structurally eliminated.

5. Fair pricing that funds maintenance

The Regulator's tariff methodology balances two needs: high enough to fund the R65bn+ catch-up maintenance backlog, low enough not to choke the road-to-rail shift. An independent body is best placed to strike that balance credibly.

6. Accountability to users

Quarterly, terminal-by-terminal KPIs (vessel turnaround, crane moves per hour, truck turnaround) and annual market reports give importers, exporters, farmers and miners the data to hold infrastructure owners and operators accountable — exactly what the DA has demanded.

Scope — What It Covers

✅ Rail Infrastructure

Tracks, signalling, stations, marshalling yards — access to this is the primary regulatory target, with TRIM as the manager and the TER as the referee.

✅ Port Infrastructure & Services

Harbour channels, berths, cargo terminals, pilotage, towage, stevedoring. The Regulator can set port tariffs and enforce non-discriminatory terminal access — critical for the landlord port model and private terminal operators.

✅ Pipelines

Transnet's ~3,000 km petroleum and gas pipeline network falls under the Regulator's jurisdiction for access and tariff-setting — ensuring energy logistics are also opened to competition.

✅ Future Essential Infrastructure

The Act is forward-looking: any future privately built rail line, port terminal, or pipeline that constitutes "essential infrastructure" can be brought under the Regulator's jurisdiction — preventing new private monopolies from replacing the state one.

From Bill to Regulator — The Timeline

2022 — National Rail Policy

The policy commitment to independent regulation is made in the National Rail Policy (2022).

2023 — Bill tabled

The Economic Regulation of Transport Bill is tabled in Parliament; public hearings and committee review follow.

2024 — Act signed

The Act is signed into law. The process of appointing the Regulator's board and building the institution begins.

Interim — TRIM & IRERC

TRIM (established Nov 2023) publishes the Network Statement and access tariffs under the oversight of the interim IRERC, while the permanent regulator is stood up.

Late 2025 onward — Fully operational

The Transport Economic Regulator becomes fully operational: first access tariff determinations, first enforcement actions, first dispute resolutions.

How the TER Fits With the Other Institutions

TRIM — Infrastructure Manager

Owns, manages, maintains the network; allocates train paths; publishes the Network Statement. The player being regulated.

TER — The Regulator

Sets maximum tariffs, enforces open access, resolves disputes, monitors conduct. The independent referee.

TFROC + Private TOCs

The train operators who compete for paths and customers. The competitors protected by the referee.

The three pillars working together: TRIM allocates and maintains, the TOCs (public and private) compete, and the TER guarantees the access and pricing rules are fair and enforceable. The same logic extends to ports under the landlord model, where the TER regulates terminal access and tariffs alongside the Ports Regulator function.

The bigger picture

South Africa spent three decades trying to fix Transnet through management changes, turnaround plans and financial bailouts — all inside the same monopoly structure. None worked. The Economic Regulation of Transport Act, and the Transport Economic Regulator it creates, represent a recognition that the structure was the problem, not the people. You cannot regulate a monopoly by asking it nicely to behave better; you have to break open the monopoly and create an independent referee with real power. The TER does exactly that. It is, alongside the unbundling of Eskom and the NERSA-led market reforms, one of the most important structural economic reforms in post-apartheid South Africa.
