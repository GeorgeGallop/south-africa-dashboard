⚡ NTCSA — National Transmission Company South Africa

The state-owned transmission company responsible for planning, building, operating, and financing South Africa's high-voltage electricity grid.

📄 Overview & Mandate

The National Transmission Company South Africa (NTCSA) was established as a wholly owned subsidiary of Eskom Holdings as part of the electricity sector unbundling process. It officially launched in October 2024. Its core mandate is to own, operate, plan, and maintain the national high-voltage transmission network (typically 132 kV to 765 kV and HVDC) and to act as the System Operator for the South African electricity grid.

The creation of the NTCSA is a critical structural reform. Previously, Eskom was vertically integrated — owning generation, transmission, and distribution. Under the unbundling roadmap, the NTCSA takes over the transmission function, allowing it to operate as an independent, non-discriminatory grid operator. This is a prerequisite for a competitive wholesale electricity market (SAWEM) because it ensures all generators — Eskom and independent power producers alike — have equal access to the grid.

Key statistics (as at 31 March 2025):

  • ~33,000 km of high-voltage transmission lines
  • ~57,000 MVA transformer capacity
  • ~3,600+ transformers
  • ~35,000 MW peak system demand
👥 Board & Leadership

In late 2024, the NTCSA Board was completed with a team of experienced professionals drawn from energy, finance, engineering, and regulatory backgrounds. The Board is responsible for governance, strategic direction, and ensuring the company fulfils its mandate under the Electricity Regulation Act.

The NTCSA reports to the Minister of Electricity and Energy, and is regulated by NERSA (National Energy Regulator of South Africa) for tariff approvals, licence conditions, and grid code compliance.

📊 The Transmission Development Plan (TDP) — Financing & Managing Assets
What Is the TDP?

The Transmission Development Plan (TDP) is the NTCSA's flagship planning document, published annually in compliance with its NERSA licence conditions. The TDP sets out the required transmission infrastructure investments over a 10-year horizon (currently TDP 2025-2034). It identifies where new lines, substations, transformers, and reactive power compensation are needed to:

  • Connect new generation capacity (renewable energy zones, gas, nuclear, battery storage)
  • Reinforce the grid for reliability and security of supply
  • Replace ageing or obsolete assets
  • Enable cross-border interconnections and power pool trading
How Will Transmission Assets Be Managed?

The NTCSA will manage the transmission network through a combination of:

  • Direct ownership & operation: The NTCSA owns the physical assets (lines, substations, transformers) and operates them through its regional control centres. This includes real-time monitoring, maintenance scheduling, and outage planning.
  • Asset management framework: A structured asset management system aligned with ISO 55001 (international standard for asset management) governs the lifecycle of every asset — from planning and procurement through operation, maintenance, refurbishment, and decommissioning.
  • Condition-based and risk-based maintenance: Rather than fixed-interval maintenance, the NTCSA is moving toward condition monitoring (thermal imaging, dissolved gas analysis, SF6 monitoring) and risk-based prioritisation to optimise maintenance spend.
  • System Operator function: The NTCSA acts as the Transmission System Operator (TSO), managing real-time dispatch, frequency control, voltage control, and congestion management. In the future SAWEM, the System Operator function may be separated into a standalone SO (System Operator) company, but currently it resides within the NTCSA.
How Will Transmission Assets Be Financed?

The scale of investment required is enormous. The TDP 2024 identified the need for approximately R210 billion over 10 years (roughly R21 billion/year). The financing strategy includes:

1. NTCSA Balance Sheet / Tariff Revenues

The primary source of funding. The NTCSA earns revenue through transmission use-of-system (TUoS) charges levied on all grid-connected users (generators and distributors). NERSA approves the tariff methodology and annual adjustments. A portion of this revenue is ring-fenced for capital expenditure and debt servicing. The TDP 2024 estimates that tariff-funded capex can cover approximately 40-50% of the required investment.

2. Government Guarantees & Direct Funding

Given the national strategic importance of the grid, the government has committed to R112 billion over the first five years (announced at the NTCSA launch in October 2024). This includes direct budget allocations from the fiscus, concessional loans from multilateral development banks (AfDB, World Bank, BRICS New Development Bank), and government guarantees to enable cheaper debt financing.

3. Private Sector Participation (ITIPP)

The Independent Transmission Infrastructure Procurement Programme (ITIPP) is a flagship initiative to bring private capital into transmission. Under ITIPP, private developers will finance, build, own, and operate (or transfer) specific transmission facilities — such as new power lines and substations in strategic corridors. The NTCSA (or the government) will pay the private operator a "transmission availability payment" over the concession period, similar to a PPP. See the separate ITIPP page for full details.

4. Development Finance & Green Bonds

The NTCSA is exploring green bonds, sustainability-linked loans, and climate finance to fund the grid expansion needed to connect renewable energy zones (REZs). The Just Energy Transition (JET) Partnership — a $8.5 billion commitment from the UK, US, EU, Germany, and France — includes a substantial component for grid infrastructure. The NTCSA is expected to leverage this concessional finance to reduce the overall cost of capital.

The "Paper" — TDP Public Forum & Regulatory Submission

Each year, the NTCSA presents its TDP at a public forum (typically October/November), as required by its NERSA licence. At this forum, the NTCSA presents:

  • The demand forecast (how much electricity will be needed over 10 years)
  • The generation connection queue (which new power plants are seeking grid access)
  • The identified grid constraints and proposed solutions
  • The capital expenditure plan and funding strategy
  • The impact on tariffs (TUoS charges)

Stakeholders — including IPPs, municipalities, industry associations, and civil society — can submit comments. The final TDP, incorporating feedback, is submitted to NERSA for approval. Once approved, the TDP becomes the binding basis for transmission investment decisions.

The TDP 2025-2034 public forum was held in October 2025, with a focus on accelerating grid expansion to connect ~56 GW of new generation by 2034, including 29 GW of renewables, 8 GW of gas, 5 GW of nuclear, and 14 GW of battery storage.

🔗 Relationship with SAWEM

The NTCSA is the backbone of the South African Wholesale Electricity Market (SAWEM). Under the SAWEM design, the NTCSA (through its System Operator function) will:

  • Receive day-ahead and real-time generation bids from market participants
  • Perform security-constrained economic dispatch (SCED) — i.e., dispatch the cheapest generation subject to grid limits
  • Determine the System Marginal Price (SMP) at each node or zone
  • Manage congestion through nodal pricing or re-dispatch costs
  • Allocate transmission rights (firm and non-firm) to market participants
  • Ensure that grid access is non-discriminatory — the NTCSA cannot favour Eskom generation over IPPs

Without a strong, independently operated, and adequately financed NTCSA, the SAWEM cannot function. The NTCSA is therefore the most critical institutional building block in South Africa's electricity market reform.
