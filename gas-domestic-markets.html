📊 How the Current Domestic Gas Market Works

South Africa's gas market is not a competitive market in the traditional sense. It is a single-supplier, vertically integrated system dominated by Sasol, which controls both the import pipeline and the majority of the distribution network. There is no gas exchange, no spot market, and no third-party trading. All gas flows through Sasol's commercial arrangements.

The structure is simple but fragile:

  • Upstream (Mozambique): Gas is produced from the Pande and Temane fields in southern Mozambique by Sasol Petroleum Temane (SPT) — a joint venture between Sasol (50%), the Mozambican government (25%), and the International Finance Corporation (IFC, 25%).
  • Midstream (ROMPCO): The 865 km, 36-inch ROMPCO pipeline transports the gas from Temane to Secunda. ROMPCO (Republic of Mozambique Pipeline Investments Company) is owned by Sasol (50%), the Mozambican state (25%), and a South African BEE consortium (25%). The pipeline is operating at its maximum capacity of ~4.3 bcm/yr.
  • Downstream (South Africa): Sasol Gas Pty Ltd (a subsidiary of Sasol Ltd) buys all the gas at the border under a long-term supply agreement with SPT. Sasol then either consumes the gas itself at Secunda (for its petrochemical operations) or on-sells it to third-party customers through the Sasol-Gas distribution network (~2,900 km of high-pressure steel pipeline).
💡 Key fact: There is no independent gas trader or third-party shipper on the ROMPCO pipeline. Sasol is the sole off-taker, the sole pipeline capacity holder, the sole transporter, and the largest consumer. This vertical integration is the single most important feature — and constraint — of the domestic gas market.
🚢 Who Imports, From Where, and How

South Africa imports gas through two channels: pipeline gas from Mozambique (the overwhelming majority) and small volumes of LNG/LPG for niche uses. There is currently no commercial LNG import terminal in operation.

⚠️ The looming gas cliff: The Pande and Temane fields are in natural decline. Sasol expects supply from Mozambique to begin declining from 2028. Without new supply sources (LNG imports, Rovuma Basin pipeline, or domestic discoveries), the ROMPCO pipeline will be unable to meet existing demand, let alone new gas-to-power demand. This is the central problem driving the Gas Master Plan.

Channel 3: Small-Scale / Emerging
  • Renergen (Virginia Gas Project): Produces helium and natural gas from onshore coalbed methane in the Free State. Still very small (~0.003 bcm/yr) but growing. Renergen has a gas trading licence from NERSA.
  • LPG imports: South Africa imports significant volumes of liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) for household and industrial use. LPG is a different product (propane/butane) from natural gas but fills some of the same energy needs in areas without pipeline access.
  • Virtual pipeline (CNG): A handful of small operators (e.g., Easigas) truck compressed natural gas (CNG) in cylinders to off-grid industrial users. This is an informal "virtual pipeline" market but volumes are negligible compared to the ROMPCO flow.

💡 The Sasol dominance problem: Sasol controls every layer — it is the upstream producer in Mozambique (50% of SPT), the pipeline owner (50% of ROMPCO), the sole importer (Sasol Gas buys 100% of ROMPCO gas), the transmission network owner (Sasol-Gas), and the largest single consumer (Secunda petrochemicals). This vertical integration means there is no wholesale gas market — no independent traders, no transparent reference price, no spot market. A third party cannot simply "buy gas at the border" because Sasol controls the border. The Gas Master Plan identifies third-party access (TPA) to pipelines as the single most important reform needed to create a competitive market.
💰 Pricing & Tariff Structure

Gas pricing in South Africa is opaque and non-transparent. There is no published benchmark or exchange price. The system works as follows:

1. Border Price (Mozambique-to-Sasol)

The price at which Sasol Gas buys gas from SPT at the Mozambique-South Africa border is governed by a long-term contract with take-or-pay provisions. The exact formula is commercial-in-confidence but is understood to be linked to international crude oil benchmarks (Brent) with a lag, plus a discount for the regional pipeline transport cost advantage over LNG. Estimated range: $6–9/MMBtu delivered at the border (2024).

2. Pipeline Transportation Tariff (ROMPCO)

The ROMPCO pipeline tariff is regulated by NERSA (on the South African side) and by the Mozambican authorities. NERSA published a new gas tariff methodology in 2024, but implementation is pending. The current tariff is estimated at ~$1.50–2.50/MMBtu depending on distance and pressure service.

3. End-User Price (Sasol-Gas customers / Egoli Gas)

Sasol-Gas and Egoli Gas charge end users a bundled price covering the commodity + transport + distribution margin. NERSA approves the maximum price. For typical industrial customers in Gauteng, the delivered price is estimated at R250–400/GJ (~$14–22/MMBtu at current exchange rates). This is competitive with diesel (R600–1,000/GJ) but more expensive than coal (R50–100/GJ) before carbon taxes.

📈 The Case for a Domestic Gas Trading Market

The user asked specifically: "I feel like there could be trading if the gas power plants get built and the pipes get built." This is exactly right. A domestic gas trading market cannot exist without three preconditions that do not yet exist: (1) multiple gas suppliers, (2) open-access pipeline infrastructure, and (3) a transparent pricing mechanism. Here is what the development path looks like:

What a Developed Gas Trading Market Would Look Like
✅ Wholesale Gas Trading
  • Multiple suppliers: LNG importers (Shell, TotalEnergies, traders), Rovuma pipeline gas, domestic producers (Renergen, Karoo), and Sasol as one player among many
  • Gas exchange / hub: A physical or virtual trading point (like the UK's NBP or the Dutch TTF) where gas is bought and sold by multiple participants at transparent, published prices
  • Standardised contracts: Day-ahead, weekend, monthly, seasonal products — enabling power generators, industrial users, and traders to manage price and volume risk
  • Market operator: An independent gas market operator (modelled on SAWEM for electricity) that manages the trading platform, settles trades, and publishes reference prices
🔧 What Must Be Built First
  • LNG import terminals: At Coega/Ngqura and/or Richards Bay — these are the gateways for multiple international suppliers to enter the market
  • New pipeline corridors: Coega → Mossel Bay → Gauteng (Corridor A) and Richards Bay → Gauteng (Corridor B) — without pipelines connecting import terminals to demand centres, there is no market
  • Third-party access (TPA): Sasol must unbundle its network or allow third-party shippers on non-discriminatory terms. This is the critical regulatory reform
  • Gas-to-power plants: 8–12 GW of gas-fired power capacity under the IRP — these create the anchor demand volume that makes the infrastructure investment viable
  • NERSA tariff methodology: Published, cost-reflective, transparent pipeline tariffs that enable independent shippers to calculate transport costs

Pricing Mechanisms That Could Develop
  • Gas hub price (SA TTF): A South African gas trading hub would establish a daily reference price (in R/GJ) based on the marginal cost of supply — typically the LNG price at the import terminal plus pipeline transport. This would be the benchmark for all gas contracts in the region.
  • LNG-linked formula: Most long-term gas supply agreements in emerging markets are priced at a discount to an international LNG benchmark (JKM, TTF, or Brent). South Africa's domestic price would likely be set as JKM minus shipping cost plus a pipeline transport charge.
  • Seasonal pricing: Gas demand would be higher in winter (power generation for heating, peak electricity demand) and lower in summer. A functioning market would show seasonal spreads, enabling storage arbitrage (if storage is built).
  • Gas-to-power price linkage: The gas price and the electricity price (from gas-fired plants) would be linked. A typical CCGT plant at 50% efficiency needs gas at ~R200/GJ to produce electricity at ~R1,000/MWh — competitive with diesel but above coal.
💡 The vision: Imagine a future where a gas trading desk in Johannesburg buys LNG at Coega, ships it via pipeline to Gauteng, sells it to a gas-to-power IPP who generates electricity for Eskom's grid, while a ferrochrome smelter in Steelpoort buys gas from the same pipeline at a different tariff for its furnaces. All of this requires physical infrastructure (LNG terminals + pipelines) and market infrastructure (TPA + trading platform + transparent pricing). Neither exists today. Both are technically feasible and economically viable if the policy decisions are made. The Gas Master Plan lays out the roadmap, but as of 2025, implementation has not yet begun in earnest.
🔒 Barriers to a Competitive Gas Market

Why doesn't the gas trading market exist yet? The barriers are structural, regulatory, and commercial:

1️⃣ Single Supplier Dominance

Sasol controls ~95% of the pipeline gas market. No trader can buy gas at the border because Sasol holds the exclusive ROMPCO capacity rights. TPA has been proposed since the Gas Act (2001) but not effectively implemented. Without multiple suppliers, there can be no competitive market.

2️⃣ No LNG Import Infrastructure

LNG terminals are the gateway for international suppliers to enter the domestic market. Without them, the only gas available is Sasol's Mozambique supply. The Gas Master Plan proposed Coega and Richards Bay LNG terminals for 2025–2028, but no FID has been taken as of mid-2025. The chicken-and-egg problem: no terminal without committed off-takers, no off-takers without infrastructure.

3️⃣ Lack of Pipeline Interconnection

The existing Sasol-Gas network is not a "grid" in the electricity sense. It is a set of radial pipelines from Secunda to Gauteng, Mpumalanga, and KZN. There is no national pipeline loop that would allow gas to flow from multiple entry points to multiple demand centres. Building this is a multi-decade, multi-billion-rand project.

4️⃣ Regulatory Uncertainty

NERSA has not yet finalised the gas tariff methodology, the TPA framework, or the LNG import licensing regime. Investors need regulatory certainty before committing R20–50bn to LNG terminals and pipelines. The Gas Master Plan remains in draft form (not yet approved).

5️⃣ Limited Anchor Demand

The IRP (2023 update) specifies only ~3–6 GW of gas-to-power by 2030, down from earlier projections of 8–12 GW. Without large, creditworthy anchor buyers (Eskom), LNG terminal developers cannot reach FID. The Sasol-Eskom MOU (Sept 2024) is a positive step but has not yet produced binding commitments.

6️⃣ Price Risk & Affordability

LNG prices are volatile and denominated in US dollars. South Africa's currency weakness (ZAR) adds 10–15% annual cost escalation risk. Industrial users are reluctant to switch from coal to gas without long-term price certainty, and generators cannot sign PPAs with Eskom at unknown gas prices.

🔮 Future Scenarios — Where the Market Could Go

Three plausible futures for South Africa's domestic gas market, depending on policy decisions, infrastructure investment, and global energy markets:

❌ Scenario 1: Stagnation
  • LNG terminals not built (no FID)
  • ROMPCO gas declines post-2028
  • Sasol-Gas contracts expire, customers switch to coal/diesel/renewables
  • PetroSA GTL shuts down (Mossel Bay)
  • No TPA implemented — Sasol remains monopoly
  • Gas share of primary energy falls below 2%
  • No trading market develops

Likelihood: Moderate. This is the default path if no decisions are made.

⚖️ Scenario 2: Managed Transition
  • Coega LNG terminal built (FSRU, 2 MTPA, 2027–2029)
  • Corridor A pipeline built (Coega→Mossel Bay→Gauteng)
  • TPA implemented — multiple traders enter market
  • 3–5 GW of gas-to-power built (peaking + mid-merit)
  • NERSA publishes transparent tariffs
  • A basic over-the-counter (OTC) gas trading market develops
  • Gas stabilises at ~5–6% of primary energy mix

Likelihood: Moderate-High. This is the GMP's base case.

✅ Scenario 3: Full Market Development
  • Both Coega and Richards Bay LNG terminals built
  • Mozambique second pipeline built (Rovuma→SA)
  • National pipeline grid with multiple entry points
  • Gas storage facilities built (depleted fields / salt caverns)
  • Full TPA — competitive gas trading hub (SA TTF)
  • 8–12 GW of gas-to-power (baseload + flexible)
  • Gas reaches 10–12% of primary energy
  • Hydrogen blending infrastructure in place by 2040

Likelihood: Low-Moderate. Requires massive capital and policy alignment.

💡 Bottom line: The user's intuition is correct — gas power plants and pipelines are precisely the infrastructure that would enable a domestic gas trading market. Without them, the market is a single-supplier monopoly. With them, the conditions for competitive trading emerge: multiple sellers (LNG importers, Rovuma pipeline, domestic producers), multiple buyers (gas-to-power IPPs, industrial users, distributors), and the need for a transparent price discovery mechanism. The Gas Master Plan envisions this, but actual development has been slow. The next 2–3 years (2026–2028) will determine whether South Africa moves toward Scenario 2 or Scenario 3 — or loses momentum entirely.
