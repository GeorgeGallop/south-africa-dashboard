💰 The Public Investment Corporation (PIC)

What the PIC is, how it has underdelivered under a cloud of fraud allegations, and the 2026 board exodus and overhaul that just reshaped Africa's largest asset manager.

What is the PIC?

The Public Investment Corporation (PIC) is the asset management arm of the South African government and Africa's largest asset manager. It invests money on behalf of public funds, principally:

  • Government Employees Pension Fund (GEPF) – the pension fund for public servants.
  • Unemployment Insurance Fund (UIF) – worker unemployment and illness benefits.
  • Compensation Fund – injury-on-duty insurance for workers.
  • Other public entities and state funds.

It manages on the order of R3.6 trillion in assets — more than 150% of GDP — making it one of the most important financial institutions in the country. Nearly every South African public servant or worker pays into a fund the PIC invests, which means the PIC's decisions directly affect the pensions and benefits of millions of people.

Historically it was a conservative investor, largely buying government bonds. Under successive mandates it evolved into a major direct investor in listed equities, unlisted companies, infrastructure, property and private equity across South Africa and the rest of the continent.

How it has underdelivered: scandal and fraud allegations

Over the past decade the PIC has been repeatedly accused of mismanagement, political interference and corruption — with a string of investments that cost public funds billions of rands in losses.

Steinhoff

Through the GEPF, the PIC held a large stake in Steinhoff International, the global retail group that collapsed in December 2017 following the discovery of accounting fraud. The PIC invested roughly R5 billion in Steinhoff just months before the collapse, and the exposure became one of the single biggest losses in its history — a stark example of due diligence failure at the very top of South African public finance.

Ayo Technology Solutions

In December 2017 the PIC invested R4.3 billion in Ayo Technology Solutions, an IT company tied to Iqbal Survé that had only recently listed. The investment was made at a R57-per-share price that critics said vastly overvalued the company. The share price collapsed within months, wiping out billions. The PIC later sued over "positive misrepresentations" and reached a confidential settlement in March 2023 after legal advice warned that prospects of recouping the R4.3bn were grim. Ayo remains one of the defining symbols of the PIC's questionable-deal era.

VBS Mutual Bank

While the PIC did not itself invest in VBS, the VBS Mutual Bank scandal sits in the same corruption ecosystem of public-funds capture. More than R2 billion was looted from VBS through fake deposits, much of it involving municipalities and public money. The PIC's name surfaced repeatedly in investigations into the patronage networks around public finance, and the scandal sharpened public focus on how state-linked financial institutions guard the money of ordinary South Africans.

The Mpati Commission of Inquiry

The losses triggered a formal inquiry. In October 2018, President Cyril Ramaphosa appointed a Judicial Commission of Inquiry into allegations of impropriety at the PIC, chaired by retired judge Lex Mpati. The commission investigated allegations of corruption, poor governance, irregular appointments and politically motivated investments. Its 2020 report found serious governance failures and recommended fundamental reform — including that the PIC be chaired by an independent non-executive director rather than the deputy finance minister, and that investment decisions be insulated from political interference. Most of the system-level recommendations were never fully implemented.

Bounty Brands, Lanseria and the pattern

The pattern extended to other deals — including losses tied to Bounty Brands and questions around the PIC's Lanseria International Airport investment, which led to arbitration, a costly forensic investigation by PricewaterhouseCoopers and later a damages claim. Each case reinforced the same criticism: politically connected decision-making, weak oversight, and public money put at risk without accountability.

What just happened: the 2026 board crisis and overhaul

The governance discontent that built for years exploded into a full crisis in mid-2026. Here is the sequence of what just happened:

  • CEO suspended. In July 2026, the PIC board placed chief executive Patrick Dlamini on precautionary suspension after whistleblower allegations, with an independent investigation launched and Leon Smit initially stepping in as acting CEO. The suspension was defended by the board as following the PIC's whistleblower policy after a protected disclosure — but Dlamini challenged its legality in the Gauteng High Court, with judgment reserved.
  • Six directors quit in a week. Within days, Thabi Nkosi, Nosiphiwo Balfour, Lerato Makwetla, Lindy Bodewig, Mpumelo Maseko and Dorothy Kobe — six of the board's non-executive directors — resigned, leaving the board stripped of most of its non-executive leadership.
  • Chairperson resigns. On 23 July 2026, chairperson David Masondo resigned, saying he was stepping down to help restore stability at the institution.
  • Investigations multiply. The Financial Sector Conduct Authority (FSCA) opened a governance investigation, and protected disclosures were submitted to Parliament alleging political interference in the running of the PIC. The Public Servants Association (PSA), which represents the public servants whose pensions the PIC manages, urged the finance minister not to dissolve the board.
  • New board appointed. On 30 July 2026, Cabinet appointed a new board of eight non-executive directors, with Seiso Joel Mohai — Deputy Minister in the Presidency for Planning, Monitoring and Evaluation — as chairperson. He was joined by Patience Nqetho, Lebogang Mokgabudi, advocate Gatlelane Ouma Rasethaba, Vivien McMenamim, Moipone Ramoipone, Bajabulile Swazi Tshabalala and Itani Mafune. Acting CEO Bathandwa Damoyi welcomed the new board as a "crucial step" toward restoring stability.

The appointment partly implements the Mpati Commission's recommendation that the PIC be chaired independently rather than by a deputy finance minister. But the central governance questions — the concentration of power, the separation of political oversight from executive management, and the protection of pensioners' money from patronage — remain unresolved as the FSCA, Parliament and the courts continue their work.

What happens next

  • The new board's immediate priorities: restore stability, oversee the response to Dlamini's court challenge, and navigate ongoing regulatory and parliamentary scrutiny.
  • The FSCA investigation into the PIC's governance is continuing.
  • Parliament continues to receive protected disclosures, and the authenticity of the political-interference allegations remains untested.
  • The core question — whether public funds can be protected from political direction — will ultimately determine whether the PIC delivers on its mandate to grow the pensions of South African workers.
