Platinum Group Metals (PGMs)

South Africa produces ~70-80% of global supply
What are PGMs?

Group of six rare metals: Platinum, Palladium, Rhodium, Ruthenium, Iridium, Osmium.

Where are they found?

Bushveld Igneous Complex (North West, Limpopo, Mpumalanga) — the largest deposit on Earth.

What are they used for?

Automotive catalytic converters, hydrogen fuel cells, jewellery, electronics, medical devices.

Supply Chain

Mined → Concentrated → Smelted → Refined → Sold to auto manufacturers and industrial users.

History of South African PGMs

Discovery: The Merensky Reef (1924)

The story of South Africa's platinum group metals begins with German geologist Dr. Hans Merensky, who in 1924 discovered the platinum-bearing reef that now bears his name in the Bushveld Igneous Complex of what is now Limpopo/Mpumalanga. Merensky's discovery came after years of prospecting; he identified the distinctive pyroxenite layer that contains platinum, palladium, rhodium, and other PGMs. The Merensky Reef remains the most important PGM deposit on Earth.

Early Mining & Expansion (1930s-1970s)

Commercial mining began in the 1930s through Rustenburg Platinum Mines, but production was limited — the world had little use for platinum before the automotive catalytic converter. During World War II, platinum production ramped up for military uses (electrical contacts, spark plugs, catalysts). Post-war, the Union and Rustenburg mines expanded, and in the 1960s Impala Platinum discovered the Bafokeng deposit on tribal land in North West province, setting up a royalty arrangement that would make the Royal Bafokeng Nation one of South Africa's wealthiest traditional communities.

The Catalytic Converter Revolution (1975)

The invention of the automotive catalytic converter by General Motors and Engelhard in 1975 transformed PGM demand overnight. Platinum and palladium proved uniquely effective at converting toxic exhaust gases into harmless compounds. The US Clean Air Act and similar regulations worldwide created permanent demand. This single application turned PGMs from a niche industrial curiosity into a strategic mineral essential to the global automotive industry.

Corporate Consolidation (1987-2000s)

In 1987, Anglo American consolidated its PGM interests into Anglo American Platinum (Amplats), creating the world's largest PGM producer. The 1990s saw palladium demand surge as gasoline catalytic converters shifted toward palladium-rich formulations, driving palladium prices above platinum for the first time. By 1994, South Africa was producing 188 tonnes of platinum annually — the dominant global supplier.

Marikana & The Labour Crisis (2012-2014)

The PGM sector became the site of South Africa's most traumatic mining labour event: the Marikana massacre of 16 August 2012, when police killed 34 striking miners at Lonmin's PGM mine near Rustenburg. It was the deadliest police violence since 1960. This was followed in 2014 by the longest strike in South African history — five months led by the Association of Mineworkers and Construction Union (AMCU), involving 70,000 workers and costing R10 billion in lost production. The strike reshaped labour relations in the sector, breaking the dominance of the National Union of Mineworkers (NUM) and establishing AMCU as a major power.

PGMs and the Energy Transition (2020s)

The rise of electric vehicles presents both a threat and an opportunity for PGMs. On one hand, EVs don't need catalytic converters, threatening the largest source of PGM demand. On the other, hydrogen fuel cell vehicles (FCEVs) and PEM electrolysers depend heavily on platinum and iridium. PGMs are essential for splitting water into hydrogen and for converting that hydrogen back into electricity. This positions South Africa's Bushveld Complex as a strategic asset in the global hydrogen economy.

The Bushveld Complex: Geological Treasure

The Bushveld Igneous Complex is a geological wonder — a massive layered intrusion covering 65,000 square kilometres, containing roughly 70-80% of the world's known PGM reserves. The Merensky Reef, UG2 chromitite layer, and Platreef are the three main PGM-bearing layers. The Bushveld also contains the bulk of South Africa's chrome and vanadium resources, making it arguably the most valuable mineral province on Earth. The complex was formed ~2 billion years ago when magma from the Earth's mantle intruded into the crust and crystallised in distinct layers, concentrating rare metals into rich seams that are now mined at depths ranging from surface outcrops to over 2,000 metres.

The Future: Hydrogen or Decline?

PGMs sit at a crossroads. The decline of the internal combustion engine threatens the catalytic converter market — the single largest source of PGM demand. However, the rise of the hydrogen economy could open a new and potentially large market. The outcome depends on the pace of the EV transition and whether hydrogen technology becomes viable for heavy transport, industry, and power storage. What is certain is that South Africa's Bushveld Complex will remain the world's dominant PGM source for decades. The question is whether the sector can navigate the labour, energy, and infrastructure challenges that have hollowed out other parts of South African mining.
